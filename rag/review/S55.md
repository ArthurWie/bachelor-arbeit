## 1. Introduction

In  today's  rapidly  evolving  global  markets,  supply  chains  have become  increasingly  complex  networks  of  interconnected  firms  (Lee et al., 2025) in which artificial intelligence (AI) plays a critical role in driving operational efficiency and strategic decision-making. AI enables firms to forecast their demand more accurately, optimize their resource allocation, and  improve  their  responsiveness,  creating significant competitive advantages (Benbya et al., 2021; Roberts and Candi, 2024). However, the widespread adoption of AI also introduces new relational challenges (Benbya et al., 2020; Fosso Wamba et al., 2023). The ' black box ' nature  of  AI  systems  and  their  potential  embedded  biases  can obscure  how  decisions  are  made  and  raise  concerns  about  fairness among supply  chain  partners  (Agrawal  et  al.,  2020). A B S T R A C T

In highly interconnected supply chains, firms are increasingly adopting artificial intelligence (AI) to improve their  forecasting,  resource  allocation,  and  responsiveness.  However,  opaque  and  bias-prone  algorithms  can obscure decision-making processes and undermine collaboration between supply chain partners. Responsible AI, in which AI is designed, developed, and deployed in such a way that ensures its ethical conduct, transparency, and alignment with human values, offers a potential remedy, but its relational effects in supply chains remain unclear.  Drawing on organizational justice theory, we test whether supply chain justice, encompassing both distributive and procedural justice, mediates the responsible AI -competitive advantage link, with supply chain complexity as a boundary condition. Survey data from 218 Chinese firms show that responsible AI fosters both distributive and procedural justice, which in turn facilitates firms ' competitive advantages. Further, we identify an asymmetric moderating effect: high supply chain complexity weakens the mediating effect of distributive but not procedural justice. Our study advances the research on AI-enabled supply chains by identifying how ethical AI practices can foster firm performance via supply chain justice and when supply chain complexity weakens this effect. Practically, our findings suggest that in complex supply networks, ensuring transparent and consistent decision-making processes provides a more robust mechanism for sustaining competitive advantages compared with focusing only on outcome allocation.

In response to this challenge, the concept of ' responsible AI ' has emerged  as  a  strategic  approach  to  the  design,  development,  and deployment  of  AI  systems  to  ensure  they  operate  ethically,  promote social good, and align with fundamental human values (Dignum, 2019; Laine  et  al.,  2024).  Within  supply  chains,  responsible  AI  involves partner-facing practices such as bias mitigation in AI-driven resource allocation and partner selection, explainable AI outputs that are accessible to stakeholders, and robust data protection mechanisms (Kumar et al., 2023). While responsible AI holds great promise, its outcomes will depend largely on how supply chain partners perceive and respond to the fairness of AI-driven decisions and processes. This brings into focus the crucial role of supply chain justice -the shared perception among partners that both the outcomes they receive (distributive justice) and the procedures used to reach those outcomes (procedural justice) are fair and equitable (Narasimhan et al., 2013). These perceptions are not peripheral;  rather,  they  fundamentally  shape  how  partners  trust  and engage with one another (Lee et al., 2025). However, while prior researchers have explored justice in traditional buyer -supplier contexts (Liu et al., 2012), whether responsible AI serves as an antecedent to interorganizational justice and, subsequently, competitive advantages remains a critical gap.

To address this gap, we draw on organizational justice theory (OJT), which posits that perceptions of fairness are fundamental determinants of organizational behavior and performance (Greenberg, 1987). Unlike purely economic views of exchange, OJT suggests that when partners perceive decision-making outcomes and processes as fair, they are less likely to engage in defensive behaviors and more likely to commit to long-term cooperation (Griffith et al., 2006). Drawing on this theoretical framework, we argue that responsible AI serves not only as a technical tool  but  also  as  a  governance  mechanism  that  signals  fairness.  With transparency, accountability, and equity embedded into its algorithms, responsible  AI  may  improve  supply  chain  partners ' perceptions  of distributive and procedural justice, in turn reducing transaction costs and fostering the necessary collaboration for competitive advantages.

However, these relationships do not exist in a vacuum. Supply chains operate in increasingly complex environments, characterized by multiple tiers of partners and fragmented information flows (Choi and Krause, 2006). Supply chain complexity (SCC) refers to the difficulties involved in managing and coordinating a diverse network of firms, processes, and relationships (Choi and Krause, 2006). From an OJT perspective, high complexity creates ' noise ' that may obscure the visibility of fairness signals (Colquitt, 2001). In complex networks, partners may struggle to verify whether an AI-driven outcome is truly equitable or a process is consistently applied (Wilhelm et al., 2016). Recognizing complexity as a critical boundary condition, we examine when and how it conditions the effectiveness of responsible AI. Accordingly, we propose two specific research questions:

RQ 1: Do distributive and procedural justice mediate the positive relationship between responsible AI and competitive advantages?

RQ2 : Does SCC moderate the relationship between responsible AI and supply chain justice and, consequently, competitive advantages?

We  gather  data  from  218  Chinese  firms  actively  engaged  in  AI implementation in their supply chain operations. The empirical results allow us to make three key contributions to the existing literature. First, we extend the research on AI value generation by moving beyond the binary view of AI adoption. While some scholars argue that the adoption of responsible AI can enhance user satisfaction (Wang et al., 2021) and organizations ' market  (Kumar  et  al.,  2023)  and  environmental  (Lin et al., 2024) performance, others caution against overestimating its effects, emphasizing the challenges involved in its implementation (Lee et al., 2023; Tehrani et al., 2024). We identify supply chain justice as a specific  relational  mechanism that translates responsible AI practices into competitive advantages. Second, we enrich the application of OJT in digital supply chains. By demonstrating that responsible AI acts as a distinct antecedent to both distributive and procedural justice, we provide a new theoretical explanation for how algorithmic governance influences partner behaviors and strategic performance. Third, we provide a nuanced understanding of SCC as a boundary condition. Contrary to the assumption that complexity universally degrades relationships, our findings reveal an asymmetry: high complexity weakens the signaling power of distributive justice (outcomes) but leaves procedural justice (process) unaffected. This offers a theoretical refinement regarding the robustness  of  process-based  fairness  in  uncertain  environments  and provides managers with practical strategies for operating in complex supply networks.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Responsible artificial intelligence

The strategic importance of responsible AI can be examined through the lens of corporate social responsibility (Seelent et al., 2025). Studies show that ethical practices build intangible assets such as reputational capital  and  stakeholder  trust,  which  translate  into  customer  loyalty, better  resource  access,  and  superior  financial  performance,  fostering sustainable  competitive  advantages  (Branco  and  Rodrigues,  2006). Responsible AI represents an extension of these ethical commitments into  the  realm  of  digital  governance.  By  embedding  principles  of accountability,  responsibility,  and  transparency  into  the  design  and deployment of AI tools (Dignum, 2020), firms can signal their commitment  to  ethical  conduct  in  their  technological  operations  (Li  et  al., 2025). Recent research highlights  that transparency, in particular,  is critical for establishing legitimacy and reducing uncertainty in dyadic supply  chain  relationships (Liu et al.,  2024).  Consequently,  the responsible governing of AI through these principles serves to mitigate substantial business risks, including reputational harm stemming from biased outcomes (Obermeyer et al., 2019), legal exposure from privacy violations  (Cheng  et  al.,  2022),  and  diminished  partner  trust  arising from opaque ' black box ' decision-making (Haibe-Kains et al., 2020).

Despite these potential benefits, empirical evidence on the business value of adopting responsible AI practices remains inconclusive (Hendriksen, 2023). While some studies show that responsible AI enhances user engagement in health care (Wang et al., 2021), strengthens risk management capabilities (Renieris et al., 2022), and positively influences market  performance  through  customer  value  cocreation (Kumar et al., 2023), others caution that these benefits are not guaranteed (Akter et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). The considerable financial investment,  specialized  expertise,  and  organizational  transformation needed for the implementation of responsible AI can present formidable barriers, particularly for resource-constrained small and medium-sized enterprises (De Bruyn et al., 2020; Wamba and Queiroz, 2023).

This tension underscores a critical gap in the literature. To reconcile these mixed findings, research must move beyond the binary question of whether adopting responsible AI leads to competitive advantages and instead investigate the mechanisms that translate internal commitments into  externally  recognized  benefits.  In  particular,  in  the  context  of complex and interdependent supply chain relationships, the pathways by which responsible AI creates relational value remain underexplored (Makarius et al., 2020). Merely deploying a technically ' responsible ' AI tool does not guarantee positive outcomes if supply chain partners do not  perceive  its  operations  and  results  as  fair  and  transparent.  This suggests  that  the  value  of  responsible  AI  may  hinge  on  relational mechanisms that shape partners ' perceptions and behaviors. Therefore, understanding how responsible AI influences perceptions of interorganizational  justice  within  the  supply  chain  is  crucial  to  explaining  its contribution to sustainable competitive advantages.

## 2.2. Supply chain justice

Justice is commonly defined as the fair and equitable treatment of individuals and groups (Colquitt, 2001). Because supply chains often involve multiple stakeholders, including suppliers, manufacturers, and distributors (Bozarth et al., 2009), scholars are increasingly recognizing the importance of justice and exploring how different types of supply chain  justice  apply  specifically  to  partnerships  (Porter  and  Kramer, 2006). Distributive justice refers to the perceived fairness of resource allocation and outcomes (Adams, 1965). Procedural justice focuses on the fairness of the processes and decision-making procedures that lead to these outcomes (Leventhal, 1980).

A growing body of work has investigated the effects of various types of supply chain justice on interorganizational relationships and performance (Griffith et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012; Qiao et al., 2022). For example, in a study of 3225 wholesale distributors, Griffith et al. (2006) found  that  both  procedural  and  distributive  justice  were  positively associated with buyer -supplier relationship quality, ultimately improving  performance.  Similarly,  based  on  data  from  402  Chinese dyads, Y. Liu et al. (2012) demonstrated that supply chain justice enhances  buyer -supplier relationship performance  by  strengthening knowledge  sharing,  commitment,  and  relationship  investment.  More recently,  Qiao  et  al.  (2022) highlighted  the  moderating  role  of supplier-perceived  justice,  which  positively  influences  the  effect  of relationship attractiveness on environmental collaboration and supplier environmental commitment. Together, these studies illustrate the significant role of supply chain justice in the dynamics of interorganizational  relationships  and  driving  both  economic  and  non-economic performance outcomes.

[S. 3]

While prior studies provide valuable insights (Liu et al., 2012), a critical  gap  remains  in  linking  supply  chain  justice  to  the  rapidly evolving  field  of  responsible  AI.  Earlier  justice  work  predates  the widespread adoption of AI, which is now guided by accountability, responsibility, and transparency principles to enhance stakeholder trust and acceptance (Kumar et al., 2023). More importantly, these principles should directly influence distributive and procedural justice. Distributive justice relates to the fairness of the AI-mediated allocation of benefits  and  burdens,  such  as  pricing,  inventory  prioritization,  or  risk sharing (Ziari and Taleizadeh, 2025). Procedural justice relates to the fairness of AI-driven decision-making processes, including supplier selection, contract evaluation, and demand forecasting (Prashar, 2025). These two dimensions are emphasized because they capture the most direct and observable ways in which responsible AI principles manifest in interorganizational exchanges, shaping both outcomes and processes. In other words, understanding the effect of responsible AI on these two types of justice is key to uncovering its business value.

## 2.3. Supply chain complexity

Supply chain complexity (SCC) refers to the difficulties of managing and coordinating a diverse network of firms, processes, and relationships within a supply chain (Choi and Krause, 2006). Because SCC reflects  a  firm's  operating  environment  (Bode  and  Wagner,  2015), researchers have examined its moderating role. For example, using data from 145 companies from Spain and the Netherlands, Gimenez et al. (2012) found that the performance benefits of supply chain integration are amplified in high-complexity supply chains but less pronounced in simpler contexts. In contrast, analyzing data from 1161 German firms, Blome et al. (2014) found that SCC positively moderates the relationship between  external  knowledge  transfer  and  flexibility  and  negatively moderates  the  relationship  between  internal  knowledge  transfer  and flexibility.

Despite the extensive research on SCC, its effect on social and ethical dynamics,  particularly  on  the  implementation  of  responsible  AI,  has received  limited  scholarly  attention.  In  complex,  multitiered  supply chains,  information  flow  often  becomes  fragmented  and  opaque (Mackelprang and Malhotra, 2015). As a result, a focal firm's responsible AI initiatives may lose clarity and influence when transmitted to Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers (Wilhelm et al., 2016). This diminished visibility can  foster  suspicion  and  reduce  stakeholders'  ability  to  accurately perceive fairness. Further, the logistical challenges of  uniformly applying responsible AI principles increase significantly with the size and  diversity  of  the  supply  network  (Busse  et  al.,  2017).  Overall, neglecting this critical interaction carries the risk of overestimating the benefits of responsible AI because structural fragmentation and operational complexities may undermine efforts to ensure fairness across the supply chain. Therefore, SCC should be considered not merely an independent  factor  but  a  pivotal  moderator  that  determines  whether responsible AI can successfully foster justice perceptions necessary for sustainable competitive advantages.

## 3. Theory and hypotheses

## 3.1. Organizational justice theory

Organizational  justice  theory  (OJT)  posits  that  the  perception  of fairness with respect to resource allocation and decision-making processes is a fundamental determinant of individual and group behavior in organizational settings (Greenberg, 1987). While initially developed to explain employee attitudes, OJT has been widely extended to supply chain management to understand interorganizational dynamics (Griffith et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2012). In the context of supply chain relationships, interorganizational justice refers to the extent to which a firm perceives that it is being treated fairly by its partners in terms of both outcomes and procedures (Alghababsheh et al., 2023). OJT suggests that when  partners  perceive  justice,  they  are  more  likely  to  engage  in cooperative  behaviors,  reduce  opportunistic  actions,  and  commit  to long-term relationship stability (Kaynak et al., 2015), all of which are critical drivers of competitive advantages (Branco and Rodrigues, 2006).

Guided by OJT, we theorize a moderated mediation pathway from responsible AI to competitive advantages via supply chain justice (see Fig. 1). Specifically, by embedding principles of equity, transparency, and  ethicality  into  AI  systems,  firms  may  strengthen  their  partners ' perceptions of distributive justice (fairness of outcomes) and procedural justice (fairness of processes), in turn fostering relations conducive to efficiency and innovation, thereby enhancing competitive advantages. Our model focuses specifically on distributive and procedural justice because they capture the most direct structural effects of responsible AI on supply chain exchanges. Responsible AI fundamentally alters what partners  receive  through  algorithmic  allocation  (distributive  justice) and how those decisions are computed through standardized protocols (procedural justice). Finally, we position SCC as a boundary condition that alters the visibility and interpretability of these justice signals.

## 3.2. Mediating role of supply chain justice

Distributive  justice  refers  to  the  extent  to  which  a  supply  chain partner perceives that the returns from its inputs into the relationship are essentially equal to those of the other party (Alghababsheh et al., 2023).  Central  to  the  conceptualization  of  distributive  justice  is  the principle  of  equity  (Adams,  1965),  in  which  firms  assess  fairness  by comparing their own outcomes-to-inputs ratio with that of their partners or peers. When this ratio is perceived as equal, equity is established. Further, distributive justice encompasses needs-based allocation, where outcomes are distributed according to a firm's needs, regardless of its contributions, preserving social relations (Deutsch, 1975). We posit that responsible AI significantly fosters perceptions of distributive justice by optimizing equity and needs-based allocations.

First, in demand forecasting and inventory management, responsibly designed AI can optimize resource allocation based on objective data (i. e.,  historical  sales  and  capacity)  rather  than  subjective  favoritism, increasing  the  perceived  consistency  and  fairness  of  allocations.  By precisely  balancing supply  with demand, responsible AI ensures that partners receive an appropriate share of resources commensurate with their  contributions  (Seyedan  and  Mafakheri,  2020).  The  objective alignment  of  inputs  (partner  contributions)  and  outcomes  (resource allocation)  satisfies  the  core  equity  criterion  of  distributive  justice. Second, in pricing mechanisms, responsible AI implements transparent algorithms that link prices directly to clear cost structures and market conditions.  This  visibility  enables  partners  to  trace  the  logic  behind economic decisions and perform the necessary social comparisons to verify  that  margins  are  distributed  fairly  (Huo  et  al.,  2015).  Such transparency reduces suspicions of negative equity (i.e., being exploited) and reinforces the belief that financial outcomes are grounded in verifiable market realities. Third, with respect to payments, responsible AI  facilitates  accurate  and  timely  disbursements,  which  addresses needs-based justice (Deutsch, 1975). By reducing cash flow uncertainty, the  focal  firm  demonstrates  a  commitment  to  the  partner's  financial stability (Kordzadeh and Ghasemaghaei, 2022), signaling that the distribution of financial risks is shared equitably rather than offloaded onto the supplier.

According to OJT, these enhanced perceptions of distributive justice translate into competitive advantages by reducing relational frictions and motivating cooperative resource sharing. When partners perceive equity, they are less inclined to engage in costly bargaining or defensive monitoring to protect their interests, thereby lowering transaction costs. More importantly, perceived fairness motivates partners to voluntarily benefit the focal firm. For example, during the post-pandemic recovery, automobile  manufacturers  that  consistently  applied  fair  allocation principles to their suppliers received priority access to critical components, helping them to directly strengthen their operational resilience and market share (Chen et al., 2022). Similarly, JD.com's use of intelligent platforms to ensure fair pricing and equitable fulfillment during COVID-19  not  only  secured  partner  cooperation  but  also  enhanced consumer  loyalty  and  repurchase  rates,  directly  influencing  revenue growth and brand competitiveness (Shen and Sun, 2023).

Further, by incorporating explainable AI,  responsible  AI  articulates  the  rationale  behind  these  decisions (Arrieta et al., 2020), allowing partners to verify that the ' rules of the game ' are stable, predictable, and free from hidden biases. This combination of consistency and transparency satisfies the core requirement of procedural regularity. Second, responsible AI fosters representativeness (or ' voice ' ) by embedding stakeholder participation into the system. Mechanisms that enable partners to query AI outputs or appeal decisions signal that their perspectives are valued. In sum, responsible AI indirectly fosters a firm's competitive advantages  by  cultivating  its  partners'  perceptions  of  distributive  justice. Therefore, we propose:

H1 . Distributive  justice  mediates  the  positive  association  between responsible AI and competitive advantages.

Procedural  justice  refers  to  the  degree  to  which  a  supply  chain partner  perceives  that  the  procedures,  policies,  and  decision-making processes  governing  the  relationship  are  fair  (Alghababsheh  et  al., 2020). According to Leventhal's (1980) framework, procedural fairness is based on specific structural criteria, most notably consistency, unbiasedness, and representativeness (voice). We posit that responsible AI fosters  procedural  justice  not  only  by  automating  tasks  but  also  by embedding these specific fairness criteria into supply chain governance.

First,  responsible  AI  strengthens  consistency  and  unbiasedness  by replacing opaque or arbitrary manual decisions with transparent, rulesbased algorithms. Traditional decision-making is prone to human error and ad hoc changes, creating uncertainty. In contrast, responsible AI helps ensure that decision rules (e.g., regarding supplier selection or logistics  routing)  are  applied  consistently  across  different  times  and In  turn,  high  procedural justice  drives  competitive advantages by influencing partners' attitudes and behaviors in ways that reduce costs and  enhance  value.  First,  procedural  justice  significantly  reduces opportunistic  behaviors  and  conflict  (Brown  et  al.,  2006;  Luo  et  al., 2015). When partners believe that decision-making rules are consistent and unbiased (e.g., automated by AI), their perceived need to engage in defensive monitoring or costly safeguards against potential exploitation decreases.  The  reduction  in  friction  and  conflict  resolution  lowers overall transaction costs, directly contributing to the focal firm's cost advantage (Narasimhan et al., 2013). Second, procedural justice fosters relationship-specific  investment  and  knowledge  sharing  (Huo  et  al., 2016;  Liu  et  al.,  2012).  According  to  OJT,  fair  procedures  signal long-term orientation and commitment (Hofer et al., 2012), encouraging partners to invest in specialized assets and share proprietary information without fear of misappropriation. For example, Ling-yee (2010) found that  procedural  justice  drives  voluntary  cooperation  and  knowledge exchange. These collaborative behaviors enable the focal firm to innovate  faster,  improve  product  quality  (Carnovale  et  al.,  2019),  and respond  more  agilely  to  market  changes,  thereby  strengthening  its competitive advantages.

In sum, empirical evidence supports a direct link between procedural justice and strategic performance (J. Hoppner et al., 2014). By institutionalizing  fair  processes  through  responsible  AI,  firms  can  cultivate perceptions of procedural justice needed to not only secure compliance but also actively unlock the operational synergies that underpin sustained competitive advantages. Therefore, we propose:

H2 . Procedural  justice  mediates  the  positive  association  between responsible AI and competitive advantages.

## 3.3. Moderating role of supply chain complexity

The process through which responsible AI fosters supply chain justice and,  subsequently,  competitive  advantages  depend  on  the  context. Because  SCC  shapes  the  structural  and  operational  environment  in which partners interpret and respond to responsible AI initiatives, we posit that it acts as a critical boundary condition (Bozarth et al., 2009; Choi  and  Krause,  2006).  From  an  OJT  perspective,  the  formation  of justice  perceptions  relies  heavily  on  the  visibility  and  clarity  of  the treatment  received  (Colquitt,  2001).  However,  high  complexity  introduces  environmental  noise  and  information  asymmetry  (Wilhelm et al., 2016), which may distort or dilute the fairness signals generated by  responsible  AI,  thereby  altering  the  effectiveness  of  the  justice mechanism.

In the case of distributive justice, OJT posits that partners assess the fairness of outcomes through a process of social comparison, meaning they compare their input-to-outcome ratio with that of others (Adams, 1965). High SCC undermines this comparison process by introducing severe informational opacity (Wang et al., 2022). In a multitier, highly interconnected  network,  the  link  between  the  focal  firm's  AI-driven decisions (e.g., resource allocation) and the final outcome experienced by a partner is often obscured by intermediaries or network fluctuations. This  attributional  ambiguity  makes  it  difficult  for  partners  to  verify whether an allocation is truly based on equitable principles or is a result of  systemic  randomness.  When  partners  cannot  confidently  attribute outcomes to the focal firm's fairness, the positive effect of responsible AI on  distributive  justice  is  weakened.  Consequently,  the  ability  of distributive  justice  to  mediate  competitive  advantages  is  diminished because the equity signal is lost in the noise of complexity. Therefore, we propose:

H3a . High SCC negatively moderates the mediating role of distributive justice  in  the  relationship  between  responsible  AI  and  competitive advantages.

Similarly,  for  procedural  justice,  high  SCC  challenges  Leventhal (1980) criteria,  particularly  consistency  and  accuracy.  While  responsible AI aims to deliver consistent and transparent decision-making, high SCC  imposes  significant  barriers  to  the  consistent  delivery  of  these procedures across a diverse and dispersed network (Ho et al., 2015). In complex supply chains, partners possess varying levels of technological capability  and  cultural  understanding.  Presenting  AI-driven  explanations in a form that is uniformly accessible and accurate for all stakeholders is logistically challenging (Busse et al., 2017). Further, mechanisms for stakeholder voice, a key element of procedural justice, may become overwhelmed. As the number of network nodes increases, soliciting,  integrating,  and  responding  to  feedback  from  all  partners becomes increasingly difficult (Dukes and Streeck, 2022). This potential for communication overload and inconsistent implementation can erode partners ' perceptions  that  the  process  is  truly  fair  and  inclusive. Consequently, we anticipate that in highly complex environments, the mediating  effect  of  procedural  justice  on  the  relationship  between responsible AI and competitive advantages could be weakened. Therefore, we propose:

H3b . High SCC negatively moderates the mediating role of procedural justice  in  the  relationship  between  responsible  AI  and  competitive advantages.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Data collection

For  our  empirical  setting,  we  selected  China's  manufacturing  and information  technology  (IT)  sectors,  which  are  among  the  most  AI- intensive sectors in China's supply chain operations, with recent evidence showing widespread deployment of AI in supplier selection, order allocation,  dynamic  pricing,  logistics  planning,  and  risk  monitoring (World Economic Forum, 2025). Importantly, these sectors differ systematically in their supply chain network positions and technological roles: manufacturing firms serve as the core of physical flows and production coordination, while IT firms act as both adopters and providers of  the  AI  infrastructure  that  underpins  digital  supply  chains  (Maslej et al., 2025). Studying both sectors provides a broader test of the universality  and  boundary  conditions  of  our  theorized  relationships. Finally, China offers a timely and policy-relevant context. Since 2023, regulators have issued detailed rules on algorithmic transparency, bias mitigation, explainability, and privacy (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2023), making the responsible use of AI critically important in supply chain operations.

We employed a rigorous, multistage process to develop and validate our  survey  instrument.  First,  the  survey  items  were  translated  from English to Chinese by a bilingual academic expert in operations management, then translated back to English by another bilingual academic expert in supply chain management. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved, with minor wording adjustments made for cultural appropriateness  while  preserving  the  original  meaning.  To  ensure  contextual relevance,  we  conducted  an  expert  review  with  two  senior  industry professionals with over 10 years' experience in AI-enabled supply chain management. These experts were identified and invited through one coauthor's professional network, ensuring both expertise and willingness to provide in-depth feedback. Their input led to minor adaptations to  reflect  the  differences  between  Western  and  Chinese  business environments.

We then conducted a pretest with a professional survey provider using a small sample of managers with experience in supply chain or technology roles from firms similar to our target population. Feedback from the pretest led to further refinements to improve clarity and reduce ambiguity. Finally, our target population comprised senior managers (e. g., vice presidents, directors, and senior managers) in Chinese manufacturing and IT firms who have actively adopted AI in their supply chain operations. Firms were excluded if they were only considering AI adoption, had made no AI investments in the past 3 years, or had no plans to expand AI usage in the next 3 years. We invited 335 qualified firms from the survey provider's database and received 218 valid responses after applying exclusion criteria and quality checks (abnormal completion time, missing data, or patterned answers), resulting in an effective response rate of 65.07%. ## 4.2. Measures

This scale captures  managerial  perceptions  of  how  difficult  the  supply  chain  is  to explain, understand, or analyze, making it more appropriate for examining strategic responses across diverse firms.

## 4.3. Bias statements

First,  the  questionnaire  underwent  iterative  refinement through cognitive pretesting and expert review to eliminate ambiguous phrasing, reduce item complexity, and mitigate the effects of question order.  Additionally,  response  patterns  were  systematically  monitored via temporal analysis, with particular attention paid to response latency metrics. Finally, a comparative analysis between early respondents (first quartile)  and  late  respondents  (final  quartile)  was  conducted  using Levene's  test  for  homogeneity  of  variance  (Armstrong  and  Overton, 1977), revealing no statistically significant differences in response distributions between the two groups ( p > .05).

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

To assess the reliability and validity of the measurement instruments, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis of all five latent constructs: responsible AI, distributive justice, procedural justice, competitive advantages, and SCC. The results indicate that the model fit the data well ( χ 2 = 172.999, df = 94, χ 2 / df = 1.840, GFI = 0.909, TLI = 0.935, CFI = 0.949, IFI = 0.950, RMSEA = 0.062). The analysis also yielded standardized factor loadings for each item on its respective construct. As shown in Fig. A1, all factor loadings were statistically significant and ranged from 0.633 to 0.909, indicating that each item made a strong contribution to the measurement of its underlying construct (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). We then assessed internal consistency and convergent validity. Internal consistency was evaluated using composite reliability and Cronbach's alpha. As detailed in Fig. A1, the composite reliability values  for  all  constructs  ranged  from  0.799  to  0.879,  surpassing  the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Bentler, 1990). Similarly, Cronbach's alpha  values  ranged  from  0.796  to  0.876,  also  exceeding  the  0.70 threshold  (Nunnally  and  Bernstein,  1978).  Convergent  validity  was evaluated using average variance extracted (AVE). The AVE values for all constructs ranged from 0.521 to 0.709, surpassing the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Bentler, 1990). This suggests that the items within each  construct  share  a  substantial  proportion  of  common  variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Finally, as shown in Table 2, the square root of the AVE for each construct was greater than pairwise correlations with other constructs, demonstrating that each construct was distinct from the others, providing evidence of discriminant validity (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

Before testing our hypotheses, we checked for the presence of multicollinearity  among  the  predictor  variables  in  our  models.  Variance inflation factor analysis measures the extent to which the variance of a regression coefficient is increased as a result of multicollinearity with other variables (Joseph et al., 2022). The results of our analysis revealed that all values were well below the commonly recommended threshold of 10 (Neter et al., 1996). Hence, multicollinearity was not a significant concern in our analysis, ensuring the reliability of our regression models and the validity of our hypothesis testing.

To test our hypotheses, we used the PROCESS macro in SPSS 23.0 (Hayes,  2014),  with  responsible  AI  as  the  independent  variable, distributive justice and procedural justice as the mediators, SCC as the moderator, and competitive advantages as the dependent variable. Firm age, firm size, industry sector, and respondent position were included as control variables.

## 6. Discussion

The surge in AI adoption across industries brings both opportunities and challenges for firms, which must find ways to use AI to strengthen competitive  advantages  while  maintaining  fair  and  trustworthy  relationships with their supply chain partners. Our study addresses this tension by demonstrating that responsible AI may serve not only as a technical tool but also as a mechanism conducive to perceived justice in supply chain relationships. By embedding principles of accountability, responsibility, and transparency into AI use, firms can actively shape their partners ' perceptions of fairness, which in turn acts as a catalyst for competitive advantages. Importantly, this effect is unlikely to arise from fairness perceptions alone; rather, it is realized through concrete cooperative  behaviors  between  supply  chain  partners  that  affect  costs, responsiveness, and relational investments.

First, our results indicate that responsible AI is positively related to distributive  justice  by  optimizing  the  equity  of  resource  allocation. Grounded in OJT, partners assess fairness by comparing their input-tooutcome ratio to that of others (Adams, 1965). Responsible AI facilitates this process by using objective data to allocate orders, prices, and resources  (Floridi  et  al.,  2018).  Further,  AI's  capacity  for  dynamic sensing  enables  needs-based  allocation  during  shortages,  ensuring partners  receive  support  based  on  urgency.  When  partners  perceive these outcomes as equitable and derived from objective criteria, they are less inclined to engage in defensive bargaining or opportunistic hedging. In turn, these fairness perceptions can improve competitive advantages through concrete cooperative behaviors (Chen et al., 2022). Perceived distributive  fairness  reduces  costly  bargaining  and  defensive  monitoring, thereby lowering transaction costs. It can also motivate partners to reciprocate through stronger cooperation in operations, such as giving the focal firm priority access during shortages or being more willing to accommodate temporary adjustments in allocation. Through lower relational frictions and more reliable partner support, distributive justice  helps  translate  responsible  AI  into  stronger  cost  efficiency  and market responsiveness, which are reflected in superior competitive advantages (Branco and Rodrigues, 2006).

Second, responsible AI acts as a powerful driver of procedural justice. Our findings suggest that ethical AI practices operationalize Leventhal's (1980) core justice criteria, specifically consistency, unbiasedness, and correctability,  into  supply  chain  governance.  By  replacing  opaque manual  decisions  with  transparent  algorithms  (explainable  AI)  and providing mechanisms for feedback (voice), responsible AI signals that the decision-making structure is legitimate and respectful (Arrieta et al., 2020). Unlike favorable outcomes, which may fluctuate, a fair process reduces uncertainty and creates willingness to rely on the partner. This willingness is strategically consequential because it enables cooperation beyond  contractual  minimums.  When  partners  believe  that  decision rules  are  consistent  and  unbiased,  they  are  less  likely  to  engage  in opportunistic behaviors or conflict, reducing the need for costly safeguards and lowering transaction costs. At the same time, fair procedures encourage  knowledge  sharing,  relationship-specific  investment,  and voluntary cooperation, which improve product quality and help firms respond more agilely to market changes (Huo et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2012). These behaviors improve forecast accuracy, reduce the costs of coordination  and  conflict  resolution,  and  support  innovation  and adaptability.  Over  time,  such  relational  assets  become  difficult  for competitors to imitate, thereby converting procedural justice into sustainable  competitive  advantages  and,  ultimately,  stronger  relative financial performance.

Finally, our study uncovers a critical asymmetry in how SCC affects these  two  justice  pathways.  As  hypothesized,  high  SCC  weakens  the mediating  role  of  distributive  justice.  In  multitier,  information-poor networks, the social comparison process needed to assess distributive equity  is  disrupted  by  attributional  ambiguity  (Wang  et  al.,  2022). Partners simply cannot see the full picture to verify whether their slice of the pie is fair. However, contrary to the expectation that SCC would universally degrade perceptions of fairness, we found that perceptions of procedural justice remain robust. This finding aligns with the uncertainty management perspective in the justice literature (Lind, 2001), which suggests that under conditions of high uncertainty (e.g., SCC), where outcomes are hard to measure, actors rely heavily on procedural information as a cognitive anchor. In this case, SCC increases the need for heuristics. Because the features of responsible AI (e.g., transparency and  explanation  protocols)  are  directly  experienced  by  the  partner regardless  of  network  noise  (Wagner  and  Neshat,  2010),  procedural justice retains its effectiveness. This suggests that in complex environments,  the  fairness  of  the  process  becomes  the  dominant  signal  of trustworthiness, overriding the ambiguity of outcomes.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

Our study offers three distinct contributions to the literature on AIenabled  supply  chain  management  and  interorganizational  justice. First, we extend the antecedents of justice by conceptualizing responsible AI as a novel governance design. Prior literature has largely focused on traditional governance mechanisms such as formal contract complexity (Poppo and Zhou, 2014), relational norms (Brito and Miguel, 2017), and power dynamics (J. Hoppner et al., 2014) as drivers of justice perceptions.  We  identify  responsible  AI  as  a  form  of  algorithmic governance that operationalizes justice criteria. Specifically, we demonstrate  how  technical  attributes  such  as  bias  mitigation  and explainability function as the digital equivalents of Leventhal's (1980) rules of unbiasedness and accuracy. This bridges the gap between AI ethics and supply chain governance, showing that ethical algorithms not only ensure compliance but actively construct the psychosocial infrastructure  (e.g.,  partners ' psychological  safety  and  reduced  fear  of exploitation) necessary for deep collaboration.

Second,  we  answer  the  call  to  link  interorganizational  justice  to strategic performance outcomes. As noted by Narasimhan et al. (2013) and Alghababsheh et al. (2023), existing research has primarily focused on  attitudinal  outcomes  (e.g.,  satisfaction),  with  limited  evidence  of tangible performance. Our study empirically supports the justice -competitive advantage link and clarifies why justice has strategic value in AI-enabled supply chains. Specifically, the value of distributive and procedural justice lies not only in more favorable partner evaluations  but  also  in  the  cooperative  behaviors  that  follow  from  those evaluations.  Distributive  justice  can  reduce  costly  bargaining  and defensive monitoring and encourage stronger partner support in times of scarcity, whereas procedural justice can reduce opportunistic behaviors and conflict while promoting knowledge sharing, relationship-specific investment, and voluntary cooperation. In this way, justice helps firms lower transaction costs, improve responsiveness, and strengthen longer-term collaborative advantages. This distinguishes responsible AI from a purely efficiency-oriented view of AI adoption by showing that part of its value is relational and is realized through fair supply chain exchanges.

Third, we refine the boundary conditions of justice by revealing the enduring value of procedural justice under conditions of complexity. Previous studies on environmental uncertainty have yielded inconsistent results regarding the efficacy of justice (Cheng et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 1995). Our findings resolve some of these tensions by highlighting the asymmetric sensitivity of justice dimensions. We show that while SCC acts as a barrier to outcomes-based (distributive) fairness because of information asymmetry, it does not diminish the value of process-based

(procedural) fairness. This pattern is consistent with fairness heuristic theory (Lind, 2001), which suggests that when outcomes are difficult to observe  or  evaluate,  actors  rely  on  procedural  cues  as  a  practical shortcut for judging whether another party can be trusted. In AI-enabled supply chains, this means that transparent, consistent, and explainable procedures may carry greater weight in complex networks. Importantly, this logic also points to a potential boundary of procedural justice in algorithmic  governance:  under  high  complexity,  partners  may  place substantial weight on visible procedural features of AI even when they have limited ability to verify whether allocation outcomes are equally favorable. Thus, our findings suggest that procedural justice is especially robust under complexity, but they also imply that future research should examine when AI-based process transparency is aligned with distributive fairness and when the two may diverge.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our findings offer actionable guidance for managers navigating the intersection  between  AI  governance  and  supply  chain  relationship management. Critically, our results suggest that the competitive value of responsible AI stems not only from its technical deployment but also from  its  ability  to  shape  partners ' perceptions  of  justice.  Therefore, managers should shift their focus from AI implementation to managing justice perceptions.

First, managers should ensure that fairness is visible to ensure it is perceived  by  partners.  Our  study  confirms  that  responsible  AI  only fosters  competitive  advantages  when  it  successfully  translates  into perceived  distributive  and  procedural  justice.  A  technically  unbiased algorithm that operates as a black box fails to contribute to this value. Therefore,  managers  should  implement  visible  justice  mechanisms. Instead  of  just  optimizing  backend  allocation,  firms  should  provide partners  with  explanatory  algorithmic  reports.  For  example,  if  an  AI system reduces a supplier's order volume, the system should automatically generate a plain language explanation with objective data. Moreover, managers could establish AI complaint channels through which partners can flag potential errors in AI judgments. This operationalizes the  correctability  criterion  of  procedural  justice,  turning  a  passive technological  transaction  into  an  interactive,  respectful  governance process.

Our results also offer guidance for adopting scenario-specific strategies based on SCC. Recognizing that SCC acts as a boundary condition, we propose a differentiated approach for managing AI governance in simple versus complex networks. In low-complexity environments (e.g., direct  Tier  1  relationships),  managers  should  pursue  both  equitable outcomes (distributive) and transparent processes (procedural) because these  are  easily  verified  by  partners.  In  this  case,  AI  systems  should provide detailed data showing how a partner's treatment compares with the  market  average,  reinforcing  the  perception  of  equity.  In  highcomplexity environments (e.g., multitier, global networks), instead of endeavoring to prove that every allocation is fair, managers should pivot strategically to ensuring the consistency, accessibility, and transparency of processes. For example, implementing a unified, blockchain-backed AI interface that guarantees that the same evaluation rules are applied to a number of suppliers is more effective than trying to justify individual order splits. By anchoring partners' trust in the process (which remains visible) rather than the outcome (which is obscure), firms can maintain collaboration, even in chaotic environments. At the same time, managers should not treat procedural transparency as a substitute for substantive  fairness.  In  complex  networks,  explainability  should  be paired with auditability, appeal mechanisms, and periodic reviews of allocation  outcomes.  This  strategic  pivot  is  particularly  relevant  for large-scale manufacturing firms that manage extensive supplier bases, where verifying individual outcomes is costly. In contrast, for smaller IT service  providers,  where  relationships  are  more  direct,  emphasizing outcome equity remains a viable and effective strategy.

## 7. Conclusion

Drawing on OJT, we empirically examined the relational mechanisms linking responsible AI to competitive advantages. Our analysis of survey data from 218 Chinese firms reveals that responsible AI is positively associated with both distributive and procedural justice, which subsequently  serve  as  key  mediators  in  fostering  competitive  advantages. Crucially, our findings highlight a divergence in how SCC conditions  these  effects:  while  high  SCC  weakens  the  mediating  role  of distributive  justice,  the  mediating  role  of  procedural  justice  remains robust.  Practically,  this  suggests  that  in  complex  supply  networks, managers  should prioritize the transparency and consistency of decision-making processes because fair procedures offer a more stable mechanism  for  maintaining  cooperation  than  outcome-based  equity alone.

Our study has several limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, our cross-sectional design could not capture the temporal evolution of justice perceptions; future researchers could use longitudinal designs to validate causal directions. Second, although our theorizing suggests that supply chain justice improves competitive advantages through cooperative behaviors between partners, we did not directly  measure  these  intermediate  behaviors  in  our  survey.  Future research could incorporate more fine-grained measures of interorganizational behaviors to uncover how justice perceptions are translated into competitive advantages. Third, while we adapted established scales, our items for supply chain justice were relatively general. Future researchers could develop context-specific scales that explicitly reference AI-driven tasks  (e.g.,  algorithmic  inventory  allocation)  to  capture  finer-grained fairness perceptions. Similarly, our theoretical  model prioritized distributive  and  procedural  justice  as  the  primary  structural  mechanisms.  However,  given  the  multidimensional  nature  of  supply  chain justice,  future  researchers  could  extend  this  framework  to  include interactional and informational justice to capture the interpersonal and communicative  differences  of  AI-enabled  collaboration.  Additionally, future works could broaden the responsible AI construct by explicitly incorporating accountability and complement our complexity measures with objective structural metrics. Finally, the use of qualitative methods such as case studies would provide deeper insights into the organizational barriers and contextual differences of responsible AI adoption that quantitative surveys may overlook.
