## ABSTRACT

The semiconductor industry is central to global technological progress but increasingly faces the challenge of balancing financial performance with rising environmental, social, and governance (ESG) expectations. While ESG and artificial intelligence (AI) are widely regarded as strategic priorities, limited empirical research has investigated their combined influence on firm-  level efficiency  in  high-  tech,  capital-  intensive  sectors.  This  study  addresses  this  gap  by  applying  a  three-  stage  Data  Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to evaluate the performance of 33 publicly listed semiconductor firms in Taiwan across profitability, sustainability, and market value. The model reflects the sequential value-  creation process: the Profit stage measures how R&D and labor are transformed into innovation output (intangible assets and income); the Sustainability stage assesses how governance and environmental inputs shape ESG scores; and the Market stage examines how ESG performance and capital investment affect market valuation.  Stage-  specific  efficiency  is  measured  using  ESG  and  structural  variables  aligned  with  the  semiconductor  sector's vertically disintegrated and interdependent value chain. AI-  driven innovation is proxied by patent activity. Results reveal performance disparities across stages: Market stage efficiency ranks highest (0.9664), followed by Profit stage, while Sustainability stage lags-highlighting an ESG implementation gap in otherwise competitive firms. Tobit regression analysis shows that AIdriven innovation enhances sustainability efficiency, whereas firm size and leverage are more closely linked to profitability. This study proposes a multi-  stage framework integrating ESG and AI to evaluate sustainable innovation in high-  tech sectors, offering insights into how firms can balance competitiveness with long-  term sustainability.

## 1   |   Introduction

The  global  semiconductor  industry,  valued  at  approximately USD  503 billion  in  2023  and  projected  to  reach  USD  980 billion  by  2029  (Statista  2023),  constitutes  a  foundational  pillar of  technological  progress  in  domains  such  as  artificial  intelligence  (AI),  computing,  and  communications.  Taiwan's  central position within this global ecosystem is underscored by its substantial contributions, accounting for 77.9% of global wafer foundry output, 21.3% of integrated circuit (IC) design, and 52%

Nevertheless, the sector's rapid  expansion  has  exacerbated  its  environmental  footprint and energy intensity, underscoring the need to implement ESG frameworks  to  mitigate  risks  associated  with  unsustainable growth. central role in the global supply chain-renders it both a global leader  and  a  focal  point  for  ESG  accountability.  Against  the backdrop of intensifying  international  and  domestic  demands for corporate sustainability, the industry faces mounting pressure to align with environmental, social, and governance (ESG) standards, necessitating a strategic equilibrium between global competitiveness and sustainable development.

Recent policy developments, including Taiwan's Climate Change Response Act and the upcoming 2025 carbon fee, underscore the urgency of adopting more integrated ESG strategies within the sector. Nonetheless, the implementation of ESG principles  remains  inconsistent  across  firms,  particularly  in relation to standardized sustainability reporting, carbon emissions mitigation, and supply-  chain transparency. For small and medium-  sized enterprises (SMEs), the adoption of ESG frameworks often lags due to limited resources and technical capacity. Despite these challenges, the potential of AI technologies to support ESG performance-through applications such as realtime emissions monitoring, energy optimization, and automated ESG scoring-has been increasingly recognized (Ozkan 2024; Svensson 2022).

While this rapid growth highlights the industry's importance, it  also  magnifies  its  environmental  footprint  and  governance complexity, particularly in resource- and energy-  intensive production processes. The sector's decarbonization demands have shifted  ESG  integration  from  a  reputational  concern  into  a strategic imperative. Nevertheless, empirical research remains limited on how ESG strategies and AI innovation can be jointly leveraged  to  improve  performance  in  high-  impact  industries. Consequently,  there  is  a  critical  need  to  develop  analytical frameworks that capture how firms operationalize sustainability alongside technological advancement and how these efforts influence multidimensional efficiency.

Although the theoretical complementarity between AI and ESG has been conceptually acknowledged, empirical studies examining  their  joint  impact  on  firm  performance  remain  scarce. Existing  research  often  evaluates  ESG  outcomes  in  isolation from efficiency metrics or focuses narrowly on environmental disclosures,  without  accounting  for  technological  enablers  or industry-  specific  constraints.  Moreover,  there  is  limited  understanding  of  how  ESG  and  AI  interact  within  capital-  and resource-  intensive  industries  such  as  semiconductors,  where sustainability  goals  may  conflict  with  high-  performance  production demands.

This study addresses these gaps by examining how ESG practices and AI-  driven innovation jointly influence firm-  level efficiency in Taiwan's semiconductor industry. Taiwan provides an ideal case study due to its global dominance in wafer foundry, IC design, packaging, and a vertically integrated supply chain, as well as advanced technological capabilities, rendering it highly competitive and subject to heightened ESG scrutiny. To capture this complexity, we apply a three-  dimensional efficiency framework based on Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), which evaluates  firm  performance  across  the  Profit  stage,  Sustainability stage,  and  Market  stage.  DEA  is  particularly  suitable  in  this context  as  it  simultaneously  accounts  for  multiple  inputs  and outputs. It offers a robust analytical tool to assess the interplay between  innovation  and  ESG  practices  within  a  capital-  and energy-  intensive sector.

Building on the DEA results, a Tobit regression model is employed to identify the determinants of efficiency across the three dimensions,  with  a  specific  focus  on  the  enabling  role  of  AI. The Tobit model is appropriate given that efficiency scores are bounded between 0 and 1, and it yields statistically consistent estimates that ordinary least squares (OLS) cannot provide. This integrated approach enables the linkage of operational assessment with explanatory analysis, thereby elucidating the mechanisms through which AI-  driven innovation and firm-  specific attributes shape efficiency trajectories.

This study makes three key contributions: (1) it introduces an innovative  three-  stage  efficiency  analysis  framework  that  integrates  ESG  dimensions  to  evaluate  corporate  performance comprehensively;  (2)  it  quantifies  the  impact  of  AI-  driven  innovation, measured through patent activity, on efficiency using Tobit regression analysis; and (3) it provides actionable insights into  the  characteristics  of  high-  ,  medium-  ,  and  low-  efficiency firms,  offering  evidence-  based  recommendations  for  achieving  the  United  Nations'  (UN)  Sustainable  Development  Goals (SDGs) and enhancing market competitiveness. These findings contribute  to  the  broader  understanding  of  how  ESG  and  AI integration can drive efficiency and sustainability in a globally critical industry.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the relevant literature, Section 3 outlines the methodology, Section 4 presents the results, and Section 5 concludes with key findings and implications.

## 2   |   Literature Review

## 2.1   |   ESG and Corporate Efficiency: A Multi-  Dimensional Perspective

Although prior research has examined ESG performance and AI capabilities as separate constructs, few studies have investigated their synergistic impact on corporate efficiency within capitaland energy-  intensive industries (Di Vaio et al. 2020). Existing scholarship  has  largely  focused  on  overarching  sustainability frameworks and the SDGs, with limited attention to the direct empirical relationship between ESG adoption and firm-  level efficiency outcomes (Singh et al. 2024). This study addresses this gap by integrating ESG and AI perspectives within a multi-  stage efficiency evaluation framework tailored to the semiconductor sector. The ESG framework provides a comprehensive lens for assessing corporate efficiency beyond traditional financial metrics,  encompassing  environmental  indicators  such  as  carbon emissions and energy consumption, social dimensions including labor conditions and workforce diversity, and governance attributes such as board independence and disclosure transparency. These ESG components directly influence firms' resilience, innovation capacity, and long-  term competitiveness, particularly in  resource-  intensive  sectors  like  semiconductors  (Chen  and Delmas 2011; Li et al. 2021). Accordingly, this section delineates the operational relevance of each ESG pillar and its implications for semiconductor efficiency performance.

[S. 3]

The environmental dimension is particularly salient due to the high carbon emissions, energy consumption, and water usage inherent  in  semiconductor  manufacturing.  Agrawal  (2023) identified  that  inefficiencies  in  semiconductor  wafer  fabrication-particularly  equipment  underutilization  and  scheduling  fragmentation-are  closely  linked  to  increased  energy consumption and operational costs, emphasizing the need for process optimization. Metrics such as the Environmental score (E-  score)  assess  environmental  practices,  including  emissions reduction and renewable energy adoption. Empirical evidence suggests that firms with higher E-  scores often exhibit superior operational efficiency (Chen and Delmas 2011).

In Taiwan, regulatory developments such as the 2023 Climate Change  Response  Act  and  the  forthcoming  2025  carbon  fee have significantly elevated the strategic importance of environmental performance. These policies incentivize the adoption of cleaner technologies and ESG reporting systems (Sueyoshi and Ryu 2020), reinforcing the role of traceability and transparency in  low-  carbon  transitions  (De  Villiers  et  al.  2021).  Recent  reviews by Di Vaio et al. (2025) and Di Vaio and Ali (2025) further highlight the critical role of disclosure and structured accountability-particularly relevant in emission-  intensive sectors like semiconductors.

Beyond environmental sustainability, the social dimension is essential for maintaining organizational efficiency through workforce  stability,  innovation  capacity,  and  reputation.  Indicators such as labor relations, diversity, and workplace safety directly affect productivity and long-  term human capital development. Lu et al. (2013) found that US semiconductor firms engaging in socially responsible practices achieved higher operational performance. In Taiwan, growing public scrutiny of working conditions and gender equity in STEM fields has further heightened expectations for social responsibility. The Social score (S-  score) captures factors such as employee rights, equal opportunity, and supply chain responsibility, reflecting a firm's ethical standards and operational resilience.

Complementing the environmental and social pillars, the governance dimension serves as the institutional backbone for ESG implementation.  Effective  governance  structures-characterized by board independence, diversity, and audit transparencyenhance oversight,  strategic  coherence,  and  capital  efficiency. Strong  governance  also  reduces  agency  costs  and  strengthens long-  term  sustainability  orientation.  Li  et  al.  (2021)  demonstrated  that  robust  governance  practices  improve  ESG  alignment  and  performance  outcomes.  Moreover,  sector-  specific ESG research underscores the need for customized metrics and governance frameworks, particularly in high-  technology sectors where general indicators may fail to capture process-  specific nuances (Delmas and Toffel 2014; Baumgartner 2014).

## 2.2   |   AI and Corporate Efficiency: Opportunities and Challenges

Building on the ESG framework discussed above, this section examines  how  AI  influences  corporate  efficiency-both  as  a technological enabler and a potential disruptor. 'AI-  driven innovation' refers to firm-  level technological advancements enabled by AI systems, particularly those that enhance process control, product  yield,  and  sustainability  analytics  in  semiconductor manufacturing. Examples include machine learning models for equipment optimization, defect detection algorithms, and automated ESG reporting tools. This conceptualization aligns with the operational characteristics of innovation in high-  tech manufacturing, where AI functions as both a production enabler and a decision-  support mechanism.

AI has become a transformative driver of corporate efficiency, with  wide-  ranging  applications  across  productivity  optimization,  innovation  enhancement,  and  sustainability  integration.  AI  applications  in  firms  can  be  broadly  categorized  into operational  optimization,  ESG  performance  evaluation,  and strategic innovation (Zhang et al. 2023). In the semiconductor industry-marked  by  rapid  technological  cycles  and  complex manufacturing demands-AI is increasingly leveraged for predictive  analytics,  process  automation,  and  real-  time  decision support (Shi et al. 2023). However, the efficiency implications of AI adoption are multifaceted, and recent studies call for more balanced  evaluations  that  account  for  both  opportunities  and systemic risks.

Empirical  evidence  shows  that  AI  enhances  firm  efficiency by  improving  throughput  and  optimizing  resource  allocation. In  China's  manufacturing sector, Luo et al. (2024) found that AI-  based technological innovation significantly improved total factor  productivity,  particularly  in  regions  with  strong  digital  infrastructure  and  marketization,  underscoring  the  role  of enabling  environments.  In  ESG  contexts,  AI  supports  emissions tracking, energy management, and ESG rating analytics (Capelle-  Blancard and Petit 2022; Pan et al. 2024), aligning with Di Vaio et al. (2020), who emphasized AI's role in sustainable business models aligned with the UN's SDGs. AI also contributes to green manufacturing through real-  time monitoring, process optimization, and regulatory compliance.

Di Vaio et al. (2020) highlighted AI's dual role in process innovation and sustainability integration, especially in high-  tech sectors. Frank (2021) noted that AI-  enabled ESG practices vary by geography and product, while Liu et al. (2022) found that AI significantly improved energy efficiency in China. In the semiconductor context, Tassel et al. (2023) showed that self-  supervised learning  optimized  fab  scheduling,  reducing  energy  use  and boosting  throughput-demonstrating  AI's  transformative  potential within the industry.

In Taiwan's semiconductor sector, AI-  driven innovation is proxied by patent activity, indicating firms' capabilities in automation and advanced production. Firms with higher AI intensity exhibit stronger  innovation  and  competitiveness  (Rammer et al. 2021), and AI complements existing IT infrastructure to enhance  efficiency  (Zhang  et  al.  2023).  Applications  include real-  time monitoring, fab scheduling, energy optimization, and predictive maintenance (Tassel et al. 2023), all of which contribute to improved operational and environmental performance.

However, notable operational and ethical risks remain. While AI holds promise for enhancing ESG performance, its deployment may also introduce unintended consequences that threaten longterm sustainability and social equity. Huang (2024) cautioned that  AI  adoption  may  exacerbate  labor  market  inequality  by disproportionately displacing mid-  skilled occupations that are vulnerable to automation. Singh et al. (2024) highlighted ethical and  inclusiveness  concerns,  while  Larsson  and  Heintz  (2020) pointed to algorithmic opacity and bias. These risks are particularly salient in technology-  intensive sectors, where AI adoption may outpace regulatory and organizational safeguards. For example, in Taiwan, rising AI adoption has increased demand for algorithm engineers while leaving mid-  skilled technician roles stagnant, creating reskilling challenges (Yang 2022).

[S. 4]

Nevertheless, when aligned with sustainability goals and governed by ethical frameworks, AI can serve as a catalyst for ESGoriented innovation (Ozkan 2024). These dynamics underscore AI's dual nature-both an enabler and a disruptor-requiring firms to develop strategic governance mechanisms that ensure responsible deployment and accountability.

## 2.3   |   ESG, AI, and the Semiconductor Industry: Toward an Integrated Framework

To  synthesize  the  preceding  discussions  on  ESG  and  AI,  this section proposes an integrated conceptual framework tailored to  the  semiconductor  industry.  Based  on  the  creating  shared value (CSV) perspective (Porter and Kramer 2011), sustainability is framed as a compliance requirement and a strategic driver of  competitive  advantage.  Unlike  traditional  corporate  social responsibility  (CSR),  which  often  emphasizes  compliance  or philanthropy, CSV embeds environmental and social objectives directly  into  the  value-  creation  process,  positioning  them  as sources of productivity and innovation. Within this framework, environmental and social goals are aligned with firm-  level efficiency  and  innovation,  increasingly  facilitated  by  AI  applications  that  support  real-  time  optimization,  traceability,  and data-  driven decision-  making.

Although  ESG  and  AI  have  each  been  widely  studied  as  independent  performance  drivers,  few  empirical  investigations have examined their combined effects on firm-  level efficiency, particularly in capital- and energy-  intensive industries such as semiconductors. This research gap is especially pronounced in Taiwan,  where  semiconductor  firms  operate  within  globally integrated supply chains and face intensifying regulatory, environmental, and geopolitical challenges. A sector-  specific framework  is  therefore  essential  to  capture  the  interplay  between ESG implementation,  AI-  driven  innovation,  and  performance outcomes.

The semiconductor industry presents unique ESG-  related challenges.  From  an  environmental  perspective,  chip  fabrication requires  ultrapure  water,  substantial  energy  input,  and  the management  of  hazardous  substances,  making  resource  efficiency and decarbonization both urgent and difficult to achieve. Socially,  the  sector  is  heavily  dependent  on  skilled  labor  yet faces persistent concerns regarding worker burnout, gender disparities, and labor rights-issues particularly salient in Taiwan's export-  oriented,  high-  tech  economy.  Regarding  governance, concentrated ownership structures and family-  controlled boards may impede transparency unless formal oversight mechanisms are instituted (Lu et al. 2013). Recent research (Sai and Yamada  2025)  suggests  that  passive  ownership  is  associated with stronger board independence, greater diversity among outside directors, and improved disclosure, whereas concentrated control  may  erode  stakeholder  trust.  In  semiconductor  M&A, tightening regulatory scrutiny-especially in cross-  border deals-underscores the need for governance safeguards to mitigate information asymmetries and ensure ESG accountability.

These sector-  specific dynamics highlight the need for tailored ESG  metrics  and  implementation  frameworks.  For  example, TSMC's ESG scorecard includes industry-  calibrated indicators such as water consumption per wafer and greenhouse gas emissions  per  process  node,  which  are  embedded  within  supplier audit systems. Such practices illustrate a shift toward contextualized ESG benchmarking in the semiconductor sector.

However, ESG benchmarking alone is insufficient. In the absence of real-  time data and adaptive modeling, firms risk stagnation or superficial compliance. AI offers a powerful complement to  ESG strategy,  enabling  tools  such  as  emissions  forecasting, anomaly detection, unstructured ESG data extraction, and risk modeling-thereby facilitating dynamic, large-  scale monitoring and execution (Capelle-  Blancard and Petit 2022; Pan et al. 2024).

The theoretical basis for this convergence is grounded in CSV and integrated reporting frameworks (Eccles et al. 2012), which emphasize value co-  creation,  transparency,  and  long-  term  accountability.  Furthermore,  AI-  enhanced  ESG  systems  align with stakeholder theory, which positions firms as accountable not only to shareholders but also to a broader network of stakeholders. In this light, AI functions both as a technological enabler and a governance mechanism capable of reinforcing ESG integration.

Recent regulatory developments-such as Taiwan's 2023 Climate Change Response Act and the impending 2025 carbon fee-underscore the urgency for ESG-AI alignment. These instruments  impose  financial  accountability  for  emissions  and incentivize the adoption of AI-  enabled compliance tools for carbon risk modeling, energy optimization, and ESG reporting. The coevolution of digital technologies and sustainability regulation is thus reshaping ESG capabilities in high-  tech industries.

Nevertheless, integration is not without risks. Issues related to equity,  transparency,  and  algorithmic  accountability  persist. Scholars  caution  that  unchecked  AI  adoption  may  reinforce labor displacement, amplify bias, and reduce interpretability in decision systems (Singh et al. 2024; Huang 2024). As such, effective ESG-AI integration necessitates robust ethical oversight and institutional safeguards.

This study proposes a three-  dimensional efficiency evaluation model  that  integrates  ESG  indicators,  AI-  driven  innovation (proxied  by  patent  activity),  and  firm-  specific  characteristics  to  address  these  conceptual  and  empirical  gaps.  Patent activity is adopted as a proxy for AI-  driven innovation, as it reflects a firm's capacity to codify, protect, and commercialize technological  advancements-particularly  those  about  automation, predictive analytics, and process optimization, which are  foundational  to  AI  deployment  in  semiconductor  manufacturing. This model captures both the reinforcing synergies and mutual constraints between sustainability strategies and technological  capabilities,  offering  a  comprehensive  framework  to  assess  performance  in  the  semiconductor  industry. While ESG and AI have been extensively examined as separate determinants of firm performance, their joint influence on efficiency-especially in capital- and knowledge-  intensive sectors-remains  underexplored.  This  study  aims  to  bridge this gap by proposing an integrated ESG-AI efficiency model tailored to the semiconductor industry.

## 3   |   Data and Methodology

## 3.1   |   Data Sources and Variable Descriptions

This  study  evaluates  the  efficiency  of  33  domestically  listed semiconductor  companies,  as  detailed  in  Table  1,  which  provides  information  on  the  proportions  of  their  main  products (as of December 2022). Each company is treated as a decisionmaking unit (DMU). Financial statement data spanning 5 years (January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022) was obtained from the Taiwan Economic Journal (TEJ) database.

This study adopts a two-  phase analytical framework, applying  DEA  in  the  first  stage  and  Tobit  regression  in  the  second. First, a dynamic three-  stage network Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) model is employed to assess firm performance across  three  interrelated  dimensions:  profitability,  sustainability, and market value. Based on Tone and Tsutsui (2010), this model incorporates intermediate outputs and carry-  over variables,  making  it  well-  suited  to  the  temporal  and  structural  characteristics  of  innovation  and  ESG  processes.  The vertically  disintegrated  and  innovation-  intensive  nature  of Taiwan's  semiconductor  industry  further  supports  the  model's relevance, as demonstrated in previous DEA applications to  the  sector  (Lu  and  Hung  2010;  Lu  et  al.  2022).  Similar multi-  stage DEA structures have also been effectively applied in  other  manufacturing  sectors,  underscoring  their  empirical  robustness  for  capturing  dynamic  and  multidimensional performance (Azadi, Moghaddas, et al. 2023; Azadi, Yousefi, et al. 2023).

Second, a Tobit model analyzes how firm-  specific attributessuch  as  innovation  output,  size,  and  capital  structure-affect stage-  specific  efficiency  scores.  Given  that  the  DEA  scores are  bounded  between  0  and  1,  the  Tobit  model  offers  consistent  parameter estimates, unlike ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.

Combining DEA with Tobit analysis facilitates a two-  stage explanatory  framework.  Efficiency  scores  from  each  DEA  stage serve  as  dependent  variables,  enabling  the  identification  of structural drivers behind performance differences. This design bridges operational assessment with strategic interpretation by linking firm capabilities in innovation, ESG engagement, and capital decisions to their efficiency trajectories.

The  DEA  model  decomposes  firm  performance  into  three sequential  stages  over  time,  as  shown  in  Figure  1.  In  Stage  1 (Profit),  inputs  include  R&D  expenditure  and  the  number  of employees, capturing technological and labor resources (Fried et al. 2008). Outputs-operating income and intangible assetsreflect  returns  on  innovative  investment.  Operating  income also serves as an intermediate output to Stage 2 (Sustainability), transmitting operational efficiency to ESG performance (Tone and Tsutsui 2010).

Stage 2 (Sustainability) focuses on ESG efficiency. Inputs such as executive compensation, employee benefits, and greenhouse gas scores represent governance, labor welfare, and environmental responsibility (Li et al. 2019). Outputs include E-  score (environmental), S-  score (social), and G-  score (governance), which feed into Stage 3, linking sustainability practices to stakeholder perception and firm value.

Stage 3 (Market) uses market value as the sole output, reflecting overall investor confidence and long-  term valuation performance. Fixed assets are introduced as a carry-  over variable from period t to t +  1, capturing the lasting effect of capital investment and  aligning  with  the  logic  of  multi-  period  DEA  (Tone  and Tsutsui 2010; Lu and Hung 2010; Lu et al. 2022).

Figure 1 depicts this dynamic structure, where stages operate within  each  period  but  are  interlinked  through  intermediate and  carry-  over  variables.  The  model  represents  a  continuous process of value creation, integrating operational, sustainability, and market-  based perspectives.

This  integrated  framework  provides  a  conceptually  rigorous and empirically grounded platform to assess how ESG engagement  and  innovation  capabilities  jointly  influence  the  efficiency trajectories  of  semiconductor firms-particularly in an industry where intangible assets and long-  term strategies drive competitiveness.

The second part of this study uses regression analysis to examine the relationship between company characteristics and efficiency at each stage, investigating the mechanisms and extent of these variables' impacts. As a core indicator of innovation capabilities, the number of patents is crucial in evaluating technological innovation. Svensson (2022) highlights that the number of patents effectively measures innovation performance and technological innovation quality, particularly regarding its impact on subsequent technological development.

Company size is another key characteristic affecting corporate performance. Ahmed et al. (2023) identify a significant relationship  between  company  size,  ESG  performance,  and  financial performance,  noting  that  larger  companies  often  invest  more in environmental and social responsibility initiatives and have competitive advantages in the market. Parrao et al. (2024) emphasize that company size significantly influences innovation sustainability  and  job  growth,  particularly  in  resource-  rich emerging markets. Thus, company size is included in the regression model to analyze its role in efficiency performance across stages.

Company age and internationalization are also important variables. Clauss et al. (2019) argue that company age affects environmental dynamism and innovation differently, with younger companies  being  more  innovative  while  mature  companies excel in resource integration and stability. ## 4.3   |   Comparison and Discussion of Efficiency Results

Based on the overall stage efficiency data, the 33 DMUs were categorized  into  three  groups:  High  Efficiency  (7  DMUs), Medium Efficiency (15 DMUs), and Low Efficiency (11 DMUs) (Table A2).

The  High  Efficiency  group  showed  balanced  resource  allocation across all stages, integrating intangible assets, profitability, and ESG performance to sustain leadership. Companies such as TSMC, EMC, and Aspeed achieved complete efficiency throughout the study period (Bagna et al. 2021).

The Medium Efficiency group performed strongly in the Market stage but showed room for improvement in resource utilization and ESG performance, particularly in the Sustainability stage. Companies  such  as  PXI  and  GMT  maintained  steady  performance  in  the  Profit  and  Market  stages  but  underperformed in  sustainability,  underscoring  the  need  for  enhanced  ESG investment.

The Low Efficiency group struggled in the Sustainability stage, with an average efficiency of 0.6844, while performing better in the  Profit  (0.9268)  and  Market  stages  (0.8992).  A  strong  focus on profitability came at the expense of sustainable development, negatively affecting long-  term objectives such as brand reputation and investor confidence.

A comparison in Table 8 highlights a substantial gap in intangible  asset  transformation  between the High Efficiency group (TFE  0.8634)  and  the  Low  Efficiency  group  (TFE  0.6234), largely driven by weak Sustainability stage performance in the latter.  High-  performing  companies  balanced  innovation,  profitability,  and  sustainability,  whereas  low  performers  focused disproportionately on specific stages, resulting in inefficiencies. Additionally, inadequate management of intangible assets and external pressures-such as regulatory constraints and market demand fluctuations-further impeded the performance of the Low Efficiency group.

## 5   |   Integrated Findings, Implications, and Future Directions

## 5.1   |   Summary of Key Findings and Discussion

This  study  adopts  a  three-  stage  Data  Envelopment  Analysis (DEA)  framework,  integrated  with  Tobit  regression,  to  investigate  the  influence  of  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  and firm-  specific  characteristics  on  operational,  sustainability (ESG), and market efficiency within Taiwan's semiconductor industry.  The  empirical  results  reveal  notable  performance asymmetries across the three stages: while efficiency in the Profit and Market stages is generally high, the Sustainability stage  consistently  underperforms.  This  pattern  aligns  with prior DEA-  based research in capital-  intensive and high-  tech sectors.

[S. 20]

For  instance,  Chen  et  al.  (2024)  developed  a  cross-  efficiency DEA  model  incorporating  prospect  theory  and  distance  entropy  to  account  for  decision-  makers'  subjective  preferences under  risk.  Their  empirical  analysis  of  high-  tech  industries across  29  Chinese  provinces  revealed  substantial  disparities between sustainability-  related and financial efficiency metrics. Similarly, Azadi, Moghaddas, et al. (2023) and Azadi, Yousefi, et al. (2023) proposed a multiplier network analytics model with a  standard  set  of  weights  to  evaluate  green  supply  chain  performance. Applied to the wire and cable industry, their model demonstrated that sustainability dimensions-particularly environmental cost efficiency-often lag operational benchmarks, even under integrated network DEA frameworks. Furthermore, Badiezadeh et al. (2018) introduced a double frontier network DEA approach to assess supply chain sustainability, incorporating undesired outputs and dual-  role factors. Their findings confirmed that environmental and social performance frequently trail operational efficiency, highlighting the limitations of conventional DEA models in capturing multidimensional sustainability outcomes.

These comparative benchmarks underscore the persistent structural  challenges  associated  with  ESG  integration  in  resourceintensive contexts, even when advanced DEA methodologies are employed. The observed gap reflects the inherent difficulty of aligning digital innovation with ESG imperatives, particularly in capital- and energy-  intensive industries. Contributing factors may include  insufficient  allocation  of  ESG-  specific  resources, regulatory ambiguity, and the complexity of embedding sustainability metrics within existing performance evaluation systems.

Patent-  based  AI  innovation  significantly  contributes  to  sustainability and market stage efficiency, reinforcing its dual role in  economic  performance  and  environmental  advancement. While patent counts are widely used as proxies for AI-  driven innovation,  they  may  not  fully  capture  non-  patented  process innovations or reflect individual patents' quality and strategic relevance.  To  enhance  methodological  transparency,  future studies may consider alternative AI intensity metrics-such as AI-  related R&D expenditure, AI workforce share, or firm-  level AI adoption surveys-which offer complementary perspectives on  technological  capacity  and  strategic  orientation  (Cockburn et al. 2019; Brynjolfsson and McElheran 2019). 1

These  results  support  the  work  of  Rammer  et  al.  (2021)  and Svensson (2022), who highlight AI's transformative potential in green manufacturing. Capelle-  Blancard and Petit (2022) further argue that AI-  enabled ESG analytics can enhance sustainability transparency, but only under robust institutional design. The relatively modest impact of AI on Profit stage efficiency, along with its uneven adoption across firms, suggests that technological  capacity  alone  is  insufficient.  One  possible  explanation  is that immediate financial returns in the semiconductor industry often depend on established production processes, cost control mechanisms, and market positioning, which may not be directly influenced by AI-  driven innovation in the short term. Echoing Cheng et al. (2014), effective ESG integration requires not only innovation but also strong internal governance mechanisms and strategic coherence.

Firm size and capital intensity exhibit divergent effects across efficiency dimensions. Larger firms benefit from economies of scale in profit generation; however, their operational complexity may reduce responsiveness to ESG imperatives. This finding aligns with Siev and Qadan (2022), who emphasize performance trade-  offs in mature firms. Capital intensity positively influences Market stage efficiency (Qiao and Lu 2024) but may constrain short-  term profitability. Financial leverage improves Profit stage efficiency (Ahmed et al. 2023), but its limited association with ESG and Market stage outcomes reflects the disconnection between economic structure and sustainability objectives.

While  AI  demonstrates  potential  in  enhancing  Sustainability stage outcomes, its systemic impact on ESG transformation remains  constrained  by  organizational  and  institutional  limitations.  These  constraints  include  fragmented  ESG  regulations, inconsistent  data  quality,  and  limited  inter-  organizational  coordination, which hinder the effective deployment of AI-  driven sustainability  solutions.  This  is  particularly  evident  among small and medium-  sized enterprises (SMEs), which often face resource and capacity barriers. Moreover, as Ozkan (2024) and Svensson  (2022)  caution,  deploying  AI  introduces  social  and ethical concerns-such as algorithmic opacity, labor displacement, and digital inequality-that may undermine broader ESG governance objectives.

The  role  of  AI  in  advancing  corporate  governance  remains a  complex  and  underexplored  dimension.  As  Larsson  and Heintz (2020) note, algorithmic transparency and explainability  are  critical  for  integrating  AI  into  governance  structures that uphold accountability, stakeholder inclusiveness, and ethical oversight. AI has the potential to enhance ESG governance functions-such as real-  time risk detection, audit automation, and  compliance  monitoring-but  only  when  supported  by strong  institutional  frameworks.  Without  clear  safeguards,  AI adoption  may  exacerbate  information  asymmetries  and  erode stakeholder trust.

The  performance  implications  of  AI  innovation  are  not  uniform  but  are  conditioned  by  a  firm's  ESG  readiness-that  is, the maturity of its ESG frameworks and internal alignment. As Cheng et al. (2014) suggest, firms with robust ESG governance are better positioned to translate technological investments into sustainable outcomes. In this study, the limited integration of AI  into  ESG  functions  among  low-  efficiency  firms  reflects  a mismatch  between  innovation  potential  and  absorptive  ESG capacity. These findings underscore the importance of organizational preparedness-not merely for adopting AI, but for embedding it meaningfully into sustainability-  oriented strategies. Future  research  could  explore  how  specific  ESG  governance mechanisms,  such  as  dedicated  sustainability  committees  or ESG-  linked KPIs, mediate the relationship between AI adoption and performance.

By  embedding  AI  and  ESG  within  a  multi-  stage  efficiency framework, this study contributes to the emerging literature on digital  sustainability,  demonstrating  that  AI's  impact  on  ESG outcomes is conditional rather than automatic. Specifically, the findings advance the theoretical understanding that AI-  driven innovation must be coupled with absorptive ESG capacity and governance maturity to yield meaningful sustainability benefits. This sector-  specific insight offers a conceptual bridge between technological innovation and institutional readiness, highlighting the organizational mechanisms through which ESG value is realized in complex production systems.

Overall, the findings emphasize the need for targeted policy interventions, internal governance reforms, and cross-  firm learning strategies to bridge the gap between technological potential and realized ESG outcomes.

## 5.2   |   Policy and Managerial Implications

The  analysis  reveals  that  while  Profit  and  Market  stage  efficiency  are  generally  strong,  Sustainability  stage  performance lags, particularly among SMEs. Although AI-  driven innovation contributes positively to sustainability, its adoption remains uneven.  Based  on  these  findings,  several  targeted  measures  are recommended:

Promote scalable ESG tools for SMEs: Support the adoption of AI-  based systems-such as emissions tracking and energy  optimization-through  cost-  sharing  schemes  or technical  assistance,  enabling  smaller  firms  to  improve sustainability performance.

Focus R&D incentives on ESG-  oriented innovation: Direct public funding toward AI applications with demonstrable environmental  benefits,  particularly  in  cleanroom  efficiency and water resource management.

Strengthen internal ESG capabilities: Encourage firms to establish  internal  governance  structures,  data  systems, and ESG execution frameworks aligned with operational objectives to close  the  gap  between  profitability  and sustainability.

Foster cross-  firm learning mechanisms: Facilitate structured knowledge exchange between highand low-  efficiency firms, particularly in ESG-  AI integration.

Adapt ESG metrics to industry context: Develop benchmarking  indicators tailored to semiconductor operations-for  example,  energy  use  per  process  node  or AI-  assisted resource recovery-to ensure relevance and comparability.

Implementing these evidence-  based policy  measures can help Taiwan  reinforce  its  leadership  in  the  semiconductor  sector while  advancing  global  sustainability  objectives.  Collectively, these strategies promote holistic progress across economic, social, and environmental dimensions, strengthening both industrial competitiveness and ESG leadership.

## 5.3   |   Research Limitations and Future Directions

This study focuses on 33 publicly listed semiconductor firms in Taiwan from 2018 to 2022. While this provides insights into a critical  national  industry,  the  findings  may  not  fully  generalize  to  firms  operating  in  other  institutional  contexts.  Future research could expand the sample to include firms from other semiconductor-  intensive  regions,  such  as  the  United  States, Japan, and South Korea, and differentiate firms by value chain positions (e.g., design, manufacturing, packaging).

The  analysis  relies  on  secondary  quantitative  data.  Future studies may incorporate qualitative approaches-such as interviews  or  case  studies-to  capture  organizational  dynamics underlying ESG-  AI integration more effectively. Including real-  time  regulatory  and  environmental  indicators,  such  as carbon pricing or stakeholder ratings, could also enhance contextual relevance.

Longitudinal  designs  are  recommended  to  assess  the  delayed impacts of innovation on ESG outcomes. Future research could examine how ESG-  linked performance incentives mediate the relationship between AI adoption and Sustainability stage performance. Additionally, social externalities of AI-such as labor displacement, algorithmic fairness, and stakeholder trust-warrant further investigation, particularly within ESG governance frameworks.

## 5.4   |   Concluding Remarks

This study proposes a three-  dimensional efficiency framework grounded in the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) method, integrating ESG and AI indicators to evaluate multi-  dimensional performance  in  Taiwan's  semiconductor  industry.  Combining network-  based  efficiency  assessment  with  regression  analysis reveals  a  persistent  lag  in  Sustainability  stage  performance, in  contrast  to  consistently  strong  outcomes  in  the  Profit  and Market  stages.  This  asymmetry  highlights  the  structural  and institutional  challenges  of  embedding  ESG  objectives  within innovation-  driven, capital-  intensive sectors.

The findings highlight AI's enabling role in ESG performance while emphasizing the importance of internal governance and firm-  specific  readiness.  These  insights  contribute  to  the  academic literature on sustainable innovation and inform policy efforts aimed at ESG transformation in high-  tech sectors. Future research is encouraged to expand this framework to other regions and incorporate dynamic ESG mechanisms for deeper insight  into  the  interplay  between  technological  innovation  and sustainability performance.
