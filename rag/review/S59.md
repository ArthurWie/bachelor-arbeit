## 1. Introduction

Mergers and acquisitions (M & A) remains a pivotal strategic instrument for firm renewal and resource reallocation (Beretta et al., 2025; David, 2021; Levine et al., 2020; Masulis et al., 2007). This significance is underscored by 2025 market trends, where global deal values surged 25% year-over-year to exceed $2 trillion (Mergermarket, 2025; PwC, 2025). Such momentum is particularly pronounced in China; domestic firms  announced  over  $54  billion  in  deals  during  Q1  2025 -a  52% annual increase. 2

Furthermore, Artificial Intelligence (AI) is fundamentally reshaping the business landscape, profoundly influencing strategic decisions like M & A. While AI ' s benefits in product development (Cooper & McCausland,  2024)  and  innovation  (Kopka & Fornahl,  2024)  are  wellestablished;  these  analytical  strengths  are  increasingly  pivotal  in  the high-stakes M & A arena. ## A B S T R A C T

Mergers  and  acquisitions  (M & A)  are  pivotal  strategic  decisions  that  shape  firm  evolution.  Drawing  on  the resource based view, this study uses data from publicly listed firms in China from 2007 to 2024 to examine how artificial intelligence (AI) adoption influences M & A activity. We find that AI adoption significantly increases a firm ' s  likelihood  of  pursuing  M & A,  with  the  effect  especially  pronounced  among  firms  with  abundant  cash holdings  and  those  operating  in  less developed  market  environments.  The  relationship  is  also  stronger  for privately owned enterprises, and AI adoption is associated with improved long term firm value. By linking technological capability with strategic outcomes, this study deepens our understanding of modern M & A drivers, highlighting the critical interplay between internal resources and institutional contexts.

Prior  studies  identify  diverse  M & A  determinants,  ranging  from leadership traits and rural experiences (Xiao et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) to team stability and ESG alignment (Cai et al., 2025; Tang, Xu, et  al.,  2024b). This study asks: How does a firm ' s adoption of AI influence its M & A behaviors? Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV), we argue that AI adoption constitutes a dynamic capability -valuable, rare, and inimitable (Barney, 1991). As a complex integration of data; algorithms; and talent  (Bharadwaj,  2000;  Petcu  et  al.,  2024),  AI  generates  firm-level heterogeneity that is difficult to replicate (Wang et al., 2023). Firms thus use AI-driven insights to identify and acquire complementary assets.  However,  the  effectiveness  of  AI  depends  on  both  internal  and external conditions. Internally, cash flow serves as an enabling resource for capital-intensive deals. Externally, the level of marketization negatively moderates this relationship, as higher information efficiency in developed markets may diminish the relative advantage of using M & A to capitalize on an AI-driven informational edge.

Using data from Chinese listed firms, this study makes several contributions. First, we extend the RBV by conceptualizing AI as a contingent  dynamic  capability  (Kraaijenbrink  et  al.,  2010).  While  prior research  emphasizes  traditional  assets  or  executive  traits  (Guizani & Larabi, 2025; Xiao et al., 2024), we suggest that the strategic role of AI depends  on  the  interplay  between  internal  financial  resources  and external  institutional  environments  (Zhang  et  al.,  2025).  Second;  we broaden M & A literature by shifting focus from market power (Bonaime & Wang, 2024) to technology-based strategic capabilities (Liang et al., 2024). By reversing the typical causality -modeling AI as a driver rather than an outcome -we complement work on digital M & A and innovation (Tang,  Liang, & Fang,  2024a) and technological diversification  (Arts et al., 2025; Zhou, Liang, et al., 2025a). Finally, we deepen the resource orchestration  literature  by  showing  how  AI  and  financial  resources jointly enable M & A (Li et al., 2024).

## 2. Literature review and research hypothesis

## 2.1. RBV and AI adoption

This paper employs the Resource-Based View (RBV) to explain how AI adoption shapes M & A strategies (Estensoro et al., 2022; Lockett et al., 2009). The RBV posits that sustainable competitive advantage stems from unique bundles of valuable, scarce, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable resources (Lockett et al., 2009); provided these assets are managed effectively (Estensoro et al., 2022; Sirmon et al., 2011). As a transformative  asset  (Elia  et  al.,  2021);  AI  adoption  is  a  strategic initiative tied to firm-specific attributes (Proksch et al., 2024), creating capabilities that are difficult for rivals to replicate.

Recent literature applies the RBV to analyze AI as a critical organizational asset. Krakowski et al. (2023) link competitive advantage to unique resource endowments; highlighting the substitution and complementation  dynamics  generated  by  AI.  Building  on  this;  Fosso Wamba et al. (2024) categorized AI assets into three types to investigate their synergistic impacts on firm performance. Furthermore; Li, Zhang, and Um (2025b) frame AI as a crucial resource for advancing positive ESG outcomes.

While  early  scholarship  recognized  information  technology  as  a strategic asset (Bharadwaj, 2000; Lioukas et al., 2016), AI differs from conventional  technologies  like  IoT  by  integrating  vast  datasets  with machine learning to detect latent patterns. Its predictive strength generates  new  tacit  knowledge  (Prior & Marcos-Cuevas,  2025);  such  as personalized services based on historical customer data (Liu, Wang, & Zhu,  2025b).  This  deep  operational  embedding  makes  AI-driven  advantages  difficult  to  imitate  (Lichtenthaler,  2019).  Consequently;  AI capabilities  fit  squarely  within  the  RBV  (Petcu  et  al.,  2024).  Beyond mere deployment; AI adoption necessitates reconfiguring operational routines and decision making systems (Mishra et al., 2022), creating a forward looking value proposition that reshapes the firm ' s performance.

## 2.2. Influence of AI adoption on M & A

Firms frequently operate under resource constraints (Kalatzis et al., 2025); motivating them to leverage AI to enhance their resource portfolios (Ren et al., 2024). Grounded in the RBV, we argue that AI adoption  provides  distinct  advantages  that  drive  M & A behaviors through three primary mechanisms: reduced labor costs, improved management efficiency, and enhanced knowledge spillovers within industrial clusters.

First,  AI  improves  operational  efficiency  by  automating  core  processes, such as manufacturing and logistics, thereby substituting manual labor and reducing costs (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020). These savings expand available capital; increasing the firm's capacity for M & A (Fan & Yang, 2025; Neary, 2007). Second, AI optimizes organizational management by overcoming traditional information bottlenecks and coordination difficulties (Haefner et al., 2021). By integrating large-scale data in real time; AI enhances decision accuracy and lowers managerial costs (Cho & Lee, 2019; Li, Wu, et al., 2025a), allowing firms to better coordinate the acquisition of strategic resources (Kushermanto & Rohman,  2024).  Third;  AI  strengthens  absorptive  capacity;  enabling firms  to  more  effectively  convert  external  knowledge  spillovers  into proprietary innovation (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Rezaei et al., 2024). Consequently, AI-driven innovation engines motivate firms to pursue acquisitions of complementary technologies or teams. Based on these mechanisms, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1 . AI adoption is positively associated with firm M & A.

## 2.3. The moderating role of marketization

Marketization reflects the degree to which resource allocation relies on market mechanisms over state planning (Du et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2025). Measured across five dimensions -including government -market relations, nonstate economy development, and legal sophistication -the marketization index (Fan et al., 2001) captures the maturity  of  the  institutional  environment.  According  to  the ' institutional substitution ' hypothesis; well-functioning markets provide effective  substitutes  for  a  firm's  internal  capabilities  (Nayyar & Luiz, 2024). In highly marketized regions; firms can readily purchase professional due diligence; industry analysis; and valuation models from external providers (Wang et al., 2025). These services replicate core preM & A  functions  of  internal  AI;  such  as  target  screening  (Song  et  al., 2024), thereby eroding the rarity and non-substitutability of a firm's proprietary technology. As competitors gain access to similar decisionsupport through market channels, the asymmetric information advantage of internal AI diminishes, diluting its unique strategic value and marginal contribution to M & A.

Institutional theory suggests that highly marketized environments increase isomorphic pressures, compelling firms to conform to established decision-making norms (Liu, Zhou, & Wu, 2024a). Because M & A transactions are high-risk and highly visible; managers in mature markets often avoid unconventional opportunities identified by AI -such as high-potential  but  low-profit  startups -to  mitigate  reputational  or valuation risks (Grashof & Kopka, 2023; Krause et al., 2019; Rao et al., 2023). Consequently, even when AI reveals strategically valuable but non-consensus targets, the need to preserve organizational legitimacy may lead managers to reject these insights. This suggests that advanced institutional environments may hinder the translation of AI capabilities into actual M & A choices. Based on this logic, we propose:

H2 . Marketization negatively moderates the relationship between AI adoption and firm M & A, weakening the positive effect of AI on M & A in highly marketized regions.

## 4.3. Endogeneity issues and tests

## 4.3.1. Instrumental variable regression

Following  Goldsmith-Pinkham  et  al.  (2020),  we  construct  an instrumental variable ( AI_iv ) using the shift-share (share -move) method to address endogeneity. Specifically, we multiply each firm ' s lagged AI adoption level by the sample-wide annual growth rate of AI adoption, excluding the focal firm from the growth calculation to ensure the instrument reflects  aggregate  technological  evolution  rather  than  firmspecific shocks. This approach simulates firm-level AI growth based on historical exposure to aggregate trends, providing a cleaner identification of the association between AI and M & A behavior. Table 5 reports the two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimation results.

In Model 6, AI is significantly associated with the instrumental variable at the 1% significance level, indicating that the instrumental variable has strong explanatory power. The Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistic is 784.720,  which  significantly  rejects  the  null  hypothesis  of  underidentification at the 1% level, suggesting that the model is not underidentified. Furthermore, the Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F statistic reaches 2246.144, which far exceeds the Stock-Yogo critical threshold of 16.38 for weak instrument diagnosis, thereby ruling out concerns about instrument weakness. In Model 7, the coefficient of AI remains significantly positive at the 5% level ( β = 0.3366, p < 0.05), providing further empirical support for the argument that AI adoption promotes M & A behavior. In addition, we conduct the Durbin-Wu-Hausman test on AI . The results show that the Hausman statistic is 7.7480 and the p-value is 0.0054, indicating that there is an endogeneity issue with the independent variable AI in the benchmark regression model.

## 4.3.2. Heckman two-step estimation

To  address  potential  self-selection  bias,  we  employ  the  Heckman two-step method. In the first step, a Probit model estimates the likelihood of M & A participation using executive overseas experience ( Oversea ) as the identifying variable. Prior research indicates that international exposure broadens managerial cognition and significantly increases  the  propensity  to  initiate  M & A  decisions  (Agcayazi  et  al., 2024; Ding et al., 2021; Liu & Kang, 2025).As shown in Model 8 of Table 6, AI and Oversea are both positively and significantly associated with the likelihood of M & A behavior ( β = 0.0331, p < 0.01; β = 0.1420, p < 0.01),  which  is  consistent  with  the  relevance  of  the  selection equation.

Based on the first-stage estimation, the inverse Mills ratio (IMR) is computed and introduced into the second-stage outcome equation to correct for selection bias. In Model 9, the coefficient of IMR is significantly  positive  ( β = 74.0815, p < 0.01),  suggesting that  unobserved factors affecting selection into M & A are also correlated with firm ' s M & A behavior. After controlling for sample selection, the regression coefficient of AI remains positive and significant ( β = 2.0293, p < 0.01). The above results are consistent with the robustness of the findings after accounting for potential selection bias.

## 4.3.3. Propensity score matching

To address endogeneity stemming from the non-random nature of AI adoption,  we  employ  Propensity  Score  Matching  (PSM).  Firms  are divided into treatment and control groups based on the presence of AIrelated  keywords.  We  perform  1:1  nearest-neighbor  matching  with replacement  using  the  previously  defined  control  variables.  As  illustrated in Fig. 3, the balance test confirms that the matching procedure effectively mitigates selection bias: pre-matching covariate imbalances are significantly reduced, with post-matching standardized differences falling below 10% and t -tests showing no significant differences between the groups. These results ensure that systematic differences in observable characteristics are minimized for the empirical analysis.

## 5.3. Changing the measurement of the moderator variable

To further verify robustness, we recalculate cash holdings using a broader definition. Following standard practice, we define Cashhold1 as the ratio of cash and cash equivalents (including cash on hand and bank deposits  from  the  cash  flow  statement)  to  total  assets.  As  shown  in Table 12, the regression results remain consistent, providing additional support for our research hypotheses.

## 5.4. Changing the sample period

Table 13 reports regression results excluding the 2020-2022 period to  account  for  pandemic-related  distortions.  The  coefficients  for AI remain significantly positive, reaffirming H1. However, the interaction term AI × Market loses statistical significance, likely  due  to  postpandemic policy uncertainties that weakened regional institutional advantages. Conversely, the interaction AI × Cashhold remains significantly positive (Models 30 and 31), further validating H3. This suggests that firms with higher liquidity are better positioned to leverage AI in supporting  their  M & A  strategies,  even  when  controlling  for  exogenous shocks.

## 5.5. Changing the fixed effects specification

To further verify robustness, we re-estimate the model by including province and year fixed effects. The results are highly consistent with the baseline  estimates,  suggesting  that  our  findings  remain  stable  under alternative fixed-effect structures (see Table 14).

To  enhance  robustness,  we  incorporate  province  fixed  effects alongside the original firm, year, and industry fixed effects to account for  time-invariant regional heterogeneity. ## 6.3. Analyst attention heterogeneity

Table  21 presents  the  heterogeneity  analysis  based  on  analyst attention (proxied by the log of analyst followers). The results reveal differentiated associations of AI adoption: For Low Analyst Coverage, the  AI  coefficient  is  significantly  positive  (5%  level). For High Analyst Coverage, the AI coefficient  is  statistically  insignificant. ## 6.4. Geographic location heterogeneity

Table  22 examines  the  association  between  AI  adoption  and geographical  regions,  revealing  notable  regional  disparities.  In  the Eastern region, the coefficient is significantly positive at the 1% level, suggesting  that  firms  in  economically  advanced  areas -supported by superior digital infrastructure and institutional environments -are better positioned to leverage AI for M & A activities.

Conversely, in the Western and Central regions, the coefficients for AI adoption are positive but insignificant. This indicates that the strategic  role  of  AI  in  shaping  M & A  behavior  is  constrained  in  lessdeveloped areas, likely due to weaker digital ecosystems and institutional  support.  These  findings  underscore  how  regional  development disparities determine the effectiveness of digital transformation.

## 6.5. Further analysis: economic consequences

To assess the economic implications of AI-driven M & A,  Table  23 examines its association with firm value (Tobin ' s Q). This metric captures market expectations of a firm ' s growth potential relative to the replacement cost of its assets. Across both Models 66 and 67, the coefficients for Masize are significantly positive, indicating that firms with more active M & A engagement exhibit higher market valuations.

These results suggest that M & A behavior contributes positively to forward-looking performance in the capital market. The findings imply that AI-driven M & A activities do more than just reshape organizational boundaries; they serve as a favorable signal to investors regarding a firm ' s  strategic  agility  and  market  positioning,  ultimately  translating into enhanced market value.

## 7. Conclusions

As AI becomes increasingly integrated into firm operations (Dong et al., 2024; Hruby, 2025; Liu, Wang, et al., 2024b; Liu, Zhang, & Qi, 2025c),  it  exerts  a  profound  influence  on  firm  financial  behavior, particularly M & A. Our results show that AI adoption significantly drives M & A strategies. As a critical case study, our findings within China ' s unique framework provide a strategic benchmark for other emerging markets undergoing digital transformations. While prior research often views digital technology as a passive acquisition target or focuses on ' AI M & A '-where firms  acquire  AI-related  assets  to  enhance  innovation (Tang,  Liang, & Fang,  2024a)  or  diversification  (Zhou,  Liang,  et  al., 2025a) -our study identifies internal AI adoption as a proactive ex ante determinant of acquisition propensity. This distinction shifts the focus from buying AI to using AI as a strategic enabler to execute deals. We find this catalytic effect is heterogeneous: it is strongest in firms with substantial cash holdings, suggesting that financial slack is essential to convert  technological  capability  into  strategic  action.  Furthermore, marketization negatively moderates this relationship; in less-marketized regions,  AI  serves  as  a  critical  tool  for  overcoming  the  information asymmetries that otherwise inhibit deal-making.

This study offers two main managerial implications bridging technology and finance. First, firms should recognize AI as a strategic asset for corporate control decisions. Senior management should integrate AI directly into the M & A workflow -using it for market scanning, datadriven due diligence, and forecasting postmerger synergies. Treating AI  as  part  of  the  financial  infrastructure  enables  firms  to  build  a competitive advantage in the market for corporate control. Second, our findings on cash holdings and market conditions provide guidance for capital allocation. For cash rich firms, investing in AI assisted decision systems  is  essential  for  deploying  capital  efficiently  and  maximizing shareholder returns. For firms expanding into opaque or less developed markets,  AI  serves  as  a  navigation  tool  that  mitigates  risk  and  compensates for underdeveloped market intermediaries.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the generalizability of our findings may be bounded by the specific institutional context of China. As a leading emerging economy with rapid digital infrastructure growth and unique capital market characteristics, China provides a critical laboratory for observing the association between AI and its outcomes; however,  the  mechanisms  identified  here -particularly those tied to marketization  levels -may  manifest  differently  in  economies  with different  regulatory  frameworks  or  more  mature  information  intermediaries. Like any study, this work has limitations that present opportunities  for  future  research.  First,  different  departments -such  as marketing and finance -may deploy AI in distinct ways. Although we identify  an  aggregate  effect,  we  cannot  determine  which  functional applications most strongly influence M & A outcomes. Future research could  use  granular  operational  data  or  case  studies  to  explore  how specific AI uses interact with human capital reallocation, a key source of  diversification  synergies  (Tate & Yang,  2024).
