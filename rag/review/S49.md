## 1. Introduction

The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly reshaped the global economy, positioning AI as a transformative general-purpose technology with far-reaching implications for firms, industries, and markets. Governments worldwide actively promote AI adoption, with China ' s ' New-generation Artificial Intelligence Pilot Zone ' policy serving as a major initiative to foster AI-driven growth. Against this backdrop, assessing the economic consequences of corporate AI investment has become both urgent and policy-relevant. Existing research has mainly focused on the real-side effects of AI, showing that adoption enhances productivity, drives innovation, and reshapes labor markets through both job displacement and creation (Cockburn et al., 2019; Acemoglu et al., 2022; Yang, 2022; Damioli et al., 2023; Babina et al., 2024). However, less is known about its capital market consequences, especially how AI investment influences investor risks and stock price stability.

Theoretically, the impact of AI investment on stock price crash risk is ambiguous. On the positive side, AI can improve information processing, enhance efficiency, and create credible growth prospects, which may reduce managers ' incentives to conceal bad news and thus lower crash risk. On the negative side, the uncertainty and complexity of AI projects may amplify managerial optimism and information asymmetry, encouraging managers to withhold unfavorable information and increasing the likelihood of crashes when

## A B S T R A C T

This pre-registered study executes the empirical design approved in the associated pre-registered report (Bai and Zhao, 2025) to investigate the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) investment on stock price crash risk. Using China ' s ' New-generation Artificial Intelligence Polit Zone Policy ' as a quasi-natural experiment, we find robust evidence that AI investment significantly increases firms ' stock price crash risk, mainly due to reduced information transparency and heightened managerial optimism. The effect is more pronounced for firms with lower levels of information transparency and tighter resource constraints. Furthermore, we also find the policy boosts firm value, suggesting that market optimism may drive short-term valuation gains at the cost of longterm stability. Overall, our findings highlight the unintended downside risks associated with AI investment, emphasizing the importance of transparency and governance in mitigating potential adverse outcomes.

We focus on the relationship between corporate AI investment and stock price crash risk in China for two reasons. First, China offers a nice institutional setting for a quasi-natural experiment. According to the Stanford AI Index Report (2025), China ranks among the global leaders in AI investment and patents. The implementation of the ' New-generation Artificial Intelligence Pilot Zone Policy ' represents a significant institutional initiative aimed at accelerating AI development. Its staggered rollout across regions and over time introduces exogenous variation in firms ' AI exposure, enabling a more credible identification of the causal effects of AI investment on firm-level outcomes such as stock price crash risk and firm valuation. Second, the information environment and capital market are relatively weak in China compared with those of developed countries (Piotroski and Wong, 2012), which may amplify managerial tendencies to withhold bad news, making stock price crash risk more pronounced. These institutional characteristics offer a sharper lens through which to examine the unintended consequences of corporate AI investment.

This study makes three key contributions. First, we extend the focus from the positive role of AI to its potential downside. While previous research has explored the positive impact of AI investment on firms ' productivity (Mihet and Philippon, 2019; Cockburn et al., 2019; Yang, 2022), labor income share (Fan et al., 2025), and innovation outcomes (Rammer et al., 2022; Babina et al., 2024), our study sheds light on the dark side of AI investment by investigating its effect on stock price crash risk, which is an important indicator of financial market stability. This perspective provides a more balanced view of the economic consequences of AI adoption. Second, we enrich the understanding of underlying mechanisms by exploring whether AI investment influences crash risk through the information transparency and managerial expectation management. This mechanism analysis deepens our insight into how technological innovation interacts with corporate disclosure behavior and market reactions, contributing to the literature on both AI and capital market dynamics. Third, we offer some insights into the policy implementation. By identifying the conditions under which AI investment may amplify downside risks, we provide evidence to help regulators design more balanced and forward-looking policies -encouraging innovation while ensuring adequate safeguards to protect market integrity and investor confidence.

## 2. Pre-registered report articulation

In executing this study, the empirical design outlined in the approved pre-registered report (Bai and Zhao, 2025) was followed. Please refer to the pre-registered report for full details on the hypothesis development, data, tool and model specification. In summary, we employ China ' s ' New-generation Artificial Intelligence Pilot Zone Policy ' as the quasi-natural experiment to conduct a differencein-difference (DID) analysis to test our hypotheses.

To measure the stock price crash risk, following Hutton et al. (2009) and He et al. (2025), we measure stock price crash risk by the negative skewness of weekly stock returns ( NCSKEW ) and the down-to-up volatility ratio ( DUVOL ), both calculated on a yearly basis. To analyze the impact of AI investment and crash risk on firm value, we follow prior studies (Wong et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2024) and use the market-to-book ratio and Tobin ' s Q as proxies for firm value.

The DID research design requires the identification of treatment and control groups, corresponding to firms that are, or are not, exposed to the exogenous policy shock. Accordingly, our treatment group consists of firms headquartered in cities that located in AI pilot zones, while firms in non-pilot cities serve as the control group. We construct a treatment indicator, Treatt , which takes a value of 1 if a firm belongs to the treatment group at year t , and 0 otherwise. We also define the variable of Posti as a time indicator that equals 1 for the post-policy period and 0 for the pre-policy period.

In addition, we include the control variables in the paper, following prior research (He et al., 2025). These variables are firm size ( Sizei,t ),  financial  leverage  ( Levi,t ),  profitability  ( ROAi,t ),  proportion  of  fixed  assets  ( PFAi,t ),  state  ownership  ( SOEi,t ),  the  largest shareholder ' s ownership proportion ( Topone i,t ), managers ' stock holdings ( Msharei,t ), CEO-chair( wo )man duality ( Dualityi,t ), separation of ownership and control( Separationi,t ), capital intensity( Intensityi,t ), book-to-market ratio ( BMi,t ), the average weekly stock returns ( RETi,t ), the volatility of weekly stock returns ( Sigmai,t )and share turnover ( Turnoveri,t ). Moreover, since the dependent variable in this study reflects the crash risk in the subsequent period, we follow Wang et al. (2024) and include the current period ' s stock price crash risk as control variable. Definitions for these variables are detailed in Appendix. Additionally, we incorporate year, industry and city levels of fixed effects to control unobserved factors that might influence our results. Standard errors are clustered at the firm level. 3.2.2.2. Placebo tests. Following previous studies (Alder et al., 2016; He et al., 2025), we conduct a series of placebo tests to examine whether our baseline regression results are driven by random factors or omitted variables. Specifically, we use NCSKEW and DUVOL as the dependent variables and design three types of placebo tests: in-time, in-space, and mixed.

First, we perform in-time placebo tests by artificially moving the AI pilot policy forward by 1 -10 years and re-estimating the DID models using NCSKEW and DUVOL . As shown in Fig. 2-a and 2-b, all placebo estimates fluctuate around zero and are statistically insignificant, indicating that our results are not driven by pre-existing time trends.

Second, we conduct in-space placebo tests by randomly assigning a subset of cities as a pseudo-treatment group and repeating the DID estimation 500 times. As shown in Fig. 2-c and 2-d, the estimated treatment effect lies in the right tail of the placebo distribution, and both two-sided and right-tailed p -values are statistically significant at the 1 % level, strongly rejecting the null hypothesis of no treatment effect.

yyFinally, we implement mix-placebo tests, in which both treatment status and treatment timing are randomly reassigned across firms and years. This procedure is repeated 500 times. Fig. 2-e and Fig. 2-f show that the actual estimated treatment effect lies in the right tail of the placebo distribution, and the corresponding p-values are significant at the 1 % level, confirming the robustness of our main results.

3.2.2.3. PSM-DID. To further address potential endogeneity arising from systematic differences between the treatment and control groups, we implement propensity score matching (PSM) and use the post-matched sample to run our DID regression. The matching procedure aims to construct a control group that closely resembles the treatment group in the AI pilot zones, based on key covariates, including firm size ( Size ), financial leverage ( Lev ), profitability ( ROA ), proportion of fixed assets ( PFA ), sales grow ( Grow ), and book-tomarket ratio ( BM ). Propensity scores are estimated using a logit model, and the baseline DID model is then applied to the matched sample.

Appendix A.3 reports the PSM-DID results. Columns (1) and (2) present estimates based on the nearest neighbor matching, where each treated firm is matched to control firms with the closest propensity scores. Columns (3) and (4) show results based on radius matching, which pairs treated firms with all control firms within a specified caliper of the propensity score. Columns (5) and (6) present estimates using kernel matching, which constructs a weight average of control firms for each treated firm. Across all matching methods, the coefficients on Treat × Post remain positive and statistically significant, further confirming the robustness of our findings.

## 3.3. Additional exploratory analysis (unregistered)

To further explore the broader economic consequences of the AI pilot policy, we investigate its impact on firms ' cost structures, financing strategies, and innovation activities. Specifically, we examine changes in administrative expenses, reliance on supply chain finance, and R & D investment following the policy ' s implementation. Administrative expenses ( ADM_Fee ) are measured as the ratio of administrative expenses to operating revenue, supply chain finance ( Sup_Fin ) is proxied by the sum of notes payable, accounts payable, and advances from customers scaled by total assets, and R & D investment ( RD ) is calculated as the ratio of R & D expenditures to operating revenue.

The regression results are presented in Table 8. The coefficients of Treat × Post are negative across all columns, indicating that following  the  implementation  of  the  AI  pilot  policy,  firms  experience  a  significant  reduction  in  administrative  expenses  and  a decreased reliance on supply chain finance. One possible explanation is that the inclusion in the pilot zones enables firms to improve firms ' internal management efficiency and access alternative financing channels -such as policy loans, government funds, or tax incentives -which in turn  alleviates  the  need  for  supply  chain-based  financing.  However,  we  also  find  that  R & D investment is significantly reduced, suggesting a potential resource crowding-out effect, where firms reallocate resources from long-term innovation activities toward AI-related expenditures or other short-term strategic adjustments. These findings underscore the nuanced economic consequences of the AI policy, which may simultaneously enhance financial flexibility while dampening firms ' incentives to engage in innovation-driven growth.

## 4. Discussion

The baseline results provide evidence that firms subject to the AI pilot zone policy experience a higher stock price crash risk compared with those unaffected, providing support for H1b. The robustness of our findings is confirmed through a series of tests, including parallel trend analysis, placebo tests, and PSM-DID.

The mechanism analysis indicates that AI investment increases stock price crash risk by reducing information transparency and amplifying managerial optimism. Additional tests show that firm value increases following the AI pilot zone policy, reflecting an environment of excessive optimism in which markets initially overreact to the perceived benefits of AI adoption, thereby inflating valuations. Cross-sectional analyses further reveal that the positive impact of the AI policy on stock price crash risk is more pronounced among firms with lower information transparency and greater resource constraints.

## 5. Conclusion

In this study, we employ a DID model based on the ' New-generation Artificial Intelligence Pilot Zone ' policy launched by the Chinese government, to investigate the impact of AI investment on stock price crash risk and firm value. Our findings indicate that AI investment significantly increases stock price crash risk by reducing information transparency and amplifying managerial optimism. Additionally, we find that firm value rises following the implementation of the AI pilot policy, reflecting an environment of excessive optimism in which markets initially overreact to the perceived benefits of AI adoption. Cross-sectional analyses further reveal that the positive effect of AI investment on crash risk is more pronounced among firms with lower information transparency and tighter resource constraints. Our main findings remain robust across a series of checks.

Our findings highlight the risk-enhancing effect of AI investment and underscore the importance of prudent policy design when promoting AI-driven development. In this regard, policymakers should exercise caution when promoting large-scale AI initiatives, as the heightened information opacity and managerial overconfidence associated with AI adoption may elevate stock price crash risk and threaten financial stability. In addition, firms should strengthen internal governance and improve disclosure quality to reduce the accumulation of hidden losses and mitigate extreme downside events. Furthermore, our evidence on the coexistence of short-term valuation gains and long-term crash risk provides important implications for investors and regulators. Moreover, the coexistence of short-term valuation gains and long-term crash risk points to an important trade-off for investors and regulators. While AI-related policies may temporarily boost firm value due to market optimism, such gains can mask underlying vulnerabilities. Incorporating this trade-off into investment decisions and regulatory oversight is essential to maintain long-term financial stability.
