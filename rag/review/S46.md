## 1. Introduction

Tourism,  travel, and  hospitality (TTH)  firms  are  increasingly deploying  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  to  improve  forecasting,  pricing, capacity planning, personalization, and front-line service support. These applications  are  particularly  consequential  in  service  settings  where value is co-produced with customers and performance depends on reliable  coordination  across  people,  processes,  and  information  at  scale (Buhalis & Law, 2008; Gretzel et al., 2015; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Tussyadiah, 2020; Zhao et al., 2025). As a result, competitive advantage is increasingly shaped not only by access to AI tools, but also by the organisational capability to integrate AI into day-to-day service operations.

Despite  the  visibility  of  AI  deployments  in  practice,  tourism  and hospitality scholarship still offers limited large-sample, firm-level evidence on whether AI capability translates into market-based value creation. Much prior work has been conceptual, case-based, or focused on traveller perceptions and adoption rather than on organisational outcomes such as firm value (Morosan & Dursun-Cengizci, 2024; Wang & Zhang, 2025). ## A B S T R A C T

Grounded in resource-based view and tournament theory, this study examines how AI human capital (AIHC) is associated with wage inequality and firm value in tourism, travel, and hospitality (TTH). Using an unbalanced panel of 1206 UK listed TTH firms comprising 7539 firm year observations from 2018 to 2024, we estimate internal instrument two step system GMM models with Windmeijer corrected standard errors. Findings indicate that AIHC is positively associated with firm value, negatively associated with wage inequality, and that wage inequality is negatively associated with firm value. Mediation analyses suggest that wage inequality partially channels the AIHC and firm value association. Moderation analyses show stronger AIHC and firm value associations in more dynamic and complex industries, whereas munificence shows no systematic moderating effect. Sectoral analyses reveal stronger value associations in travel and stronger inequality compression associations in hospitality. To address these gaps, we examine whether building AI capability through the workforce is associated with both firm performance and pay structures in tourism, travel, and hospitality. We compile a firm year dataset of 1206 UK listed TTH firms covering 2018 to 2024 (7539 observations),  and  we  focus  on  the  UK  because  listed  firms  disclose remuneration and financial information in a comparatively standardized way, making it a particularly informative setting for studying both wage inequality  and  market value (Department for Business, Energy & Industrial Strategy, 2018; UK Endorsement Board, 2021). We capture AI human capital intensity using a straightforward measure: the percentage of a firm's workforce with AI-related roles/skills in the prior year, which strengthens temporal ordering between AI talent investment and subsequent outcomes. We assess wage inequality using a widely adopted vertical pay measure, defined as the ratio of average top management compensation  to  estimated  average  employee  pay.  Firm  value  is measured using Tobin's Q, which is a standard market-based indicator. We also examine whether these relationships vary across industry conditions, including dynamism and complexity. By focusing on who holds AI skills and how broadly these skills are embedded within the organisation, our approach moves beyond binary notions of ' AI adoption ' and offers evidence on whether AI talent investment is associated with value creation and more equitable pay structures in labour-intensive service industries.

## 2. Literature review

This  section  synthesizes  prior  research  that  informs  the  study's research questions. We first review work on AI human capital intensity as  a  firm-level  capability,  with  particular  attention  to  how  it  is conceptualized,  measured,  and  expected  to  affect  value  creation  in service settings. We then review evidence on wage inequality and pay dispersion, focusing on mechanisms through which technology-related skill investments may reshape within-firm pay structures. Across both subsections, we highlight points of convergence in the literature, clarify unresolved issues, and delineate how these gaps motivate the empirical questions examined in this study.

## 2.1. AI human capital

AI human capital intensity refers to the resources firms devote to attracting, developing, and retaining employees with AI-relevant competencies,  such  as  machine  learning,  data  science,  and  human-AI orchestration,  and  to  embedding  these  competencies  in  complementary processes and governance (Liu & Zhang, 2025). Recent large-sample  studies  operationalize  this  construct  by  using  matched job-posting and resume data to identify AI-related skills and to compute the share of AI workers at the firm-year level (Babina et al., 2023, 2024). This measurement approach moves attention from whether a firm uses AI in a general sense to the extent to which AI capabilities are embodied in the workforce. Theory suggests that the performance implications of AI human capital intensity depend on how AI-related skills complement the  task  structure  of  production.  In  services,  AI  capabilities  can  be mapped to different task intelligences, including mechanical, analytical, intuitive, and empathetic dimensions, implying that the returns to AI skills  vary with the nature of service work and the degree of human interaction  (Huang & Rust,  2018).  A  task-based  perspective  further predicts that AI investments can reallocate task content by automating some activities while creating new, higher-skill tasks, thereby changing workforce  composition  and  returns  to  skills  (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019).  However,  documented  productivity  paradoxes  caution  that benefits are not automatic. Value creation often depends on complementary assets, such as data availability, digital architecture, and work design, and may materialize only after diffusion and implementation lags (Brynjolfsson et al., 2017; Innocenti & Golin, 2022).

Within tourism, travel, and hospitality, discussions of AI capability have evolved from eTourism to smart tourism ecosystems, emphasizing digitally enabled coordination across firms, technologies, and customers (Buhalis & Law, 2008; Gretzel et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2026). TTH is characterized  by  high  contact  intensity,  volatile  demand,  and  operational standardization, conditions under which AI-skilled employees can support both frontstage and backstage applications, including service robots and conversational agents as well as demand forecasting, pricing, and personalization (Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Li et al., 2025). Existing evidence also indicates that the objectives guiding automation, such as cost reduction versus service enhancement, shape customer responses and highlight the importance of aligning technical capability with service design (Belanche et al., 2021; Stylos et al., 2025). Adoption outcomes further depend on the interaction context, with acceptance shaped by perceived interaction quality, creepiness versus coolness, and perceived health  risks  during  crises  (Kim  et  al.,  2021;  Tussyadiah & Park, 2018).

To reflect current scholarly conversations in Tourism Management, we further position the study alongside recent work on generative AI, AI supported job crafting, AI chatbots, and AI based service failure recovery in tourism and hospitality (Li et al., 2025; Stylos et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). These studies clarify that AI now  affects  both  customer  facing  service  experiences  and  employee work design, whereas our study extends this stream by examining firm year variation in AI human capital, wage inequality, and market value.

Despite this progress, much of the TTH literature remains focused on technology presence and customer perceptions rather than treating AI human  capital  as  a  firm-level  resource  observable  in  workforce composition (Li et al., 2024). This limits inference about whether and how differences in AI skill shares are associated with firm value and with internal distributional outcomes. By employing labor-based, firm-year measures of AI human capital, the present study extends prior work by G. Agag et al.

## 2.2. Wage inequality

Wage inequality, understood as differences in pay within and between firms, has re-emerged as a central feature of contemporary labor markets  (Marsland,  2015).  Two  bodies  of  evidence  are  particularly relevant  for  firm-level  research.  First,  matched  employer-employee studies show that rising wage dispersion reflects both within-firm gaps and, to a substantial extent, widening differences between firms in the average wages they pay (Card et al., 2013; Song et al., 2019). Second, organizational  research  links  pay  dispersion  to  performance  through competing  mechanisms.  Tournament  theory  suggests  that  larger  pay spreads can motivate effort when promotion prospects and prize differentials are credible (Lazear & Rosen, 1981), while other work highlights potential costs through reduced collaboration, perceived unfairness,  and  higher  turnover  (Bloom,  1999;  Shaw  et  al.,  2002). Consequently, the relationship between inequality and performance is context dependent and likely to vary with task interdependence and skill complementarities (Guidi & Guardiancich, 2025).

In the UK, pay-ratio reporting underscores the prevalence of steep vertical pay structures. For 2023/24, the median chief executive earned 52 times the median employee salary across FTSE 350 firms (High Pay Centre,  2025).  While  pay  ratios  do  not  capture  all  dimensions  of inequality, they signal differences between top management compensation and typical employee pay that may affect engagement, organizational cohesion, and governance concerns. These considerations are salient  for  research  on AI-related skill  investments because AI-driven work redesign can shift relative bargaining positions and the demand for  complementary  roles,  potentially  influencing  within-firm  wage structures.

These issues are especially pronounced in TTH, where employment is labor intensive, standardized, and demand volatile (Xu et al., 2025). Industry statistics show a persistent concentration of low pay in hospitality. In 2024, 53.6 percent of jobs in accommodation and food services were paid below the UK real Living Wage, representing the highest industry share for the thirteenth consecutive year (Living  Wage Foundation,  2025).  By  comparison,  8.9  percent  of  all  UK  employee jobs were classified as low paid in 2023 using the OECD definition (ONS, 2023). Although pay growth accelerated in 2024 and accommodation and food services recorded the fastest year-on-year increase in median weekly earnings (9.8 percent), the sector remains characterized by low pay and variable hours (ONS, 2024). Vertical inequality is also evident at the top of the distribution, with pay-ratio disclosures indicating wide spreads in some travel-and-leisure firms, such as a CEO-to-median-employee ratio of 303:1 for Compass Group in 2023/24 (High Pay Centre, 2025). Research further documents gender pay differentials in hospitality, with estimates for European hospitality management roles ranging from 5.1 percent to 23.8 percent and links these gaps  to  occupational  segregation  and  precarious  work  (de  Castro Romero,  Barroso, & Santero-S ´ anchez,  2025;  Kronenberg & Fuchs, 2021).

Taken together, the literature indicates that TTH combines structural conditions that sustain unequal pay outcomes with ongoing technological and organizational change. However, existing work rarely connects firm-level  investments  in  AI-related  human  capital  to  distributional outcomes within firms. This gap motivates our second research question on  whether  AI  human  capital  intensity  is  associated  with  wage inequality  in  TTH  firms,  complementing  the  performance-focused question reviewed above.

## 2.3. Overarching theory

Resource-based view (RBV) (Barney, 1991, 1996) and tournament theory (Bognanno, 2001; Lazear, 1989) provide complementary lenses for explaining how AI human capital investment, wage inequality, and firm value are linked. RBV holds that heterogeneous, difficult-to-imitate resources  and  capabilities  to  orchestrate  them  underpin  advantage (Barney,  1991,  1996;  Peteraf,  1993;  Teece  et  al.,  1997;  Wernerfelt, 1984).  Human  capital,  particularly  specialized  and  firm  embedded expertise, is a central intangible resource that can meet VRIN conditions when  combined  with  idiosyncratic  routines  and  path  dependence (Lepak & Snell,  1999;  Ployhart & Moliterno,  2011).  In  AI-intensive contexts,  the  relevant  resource  is  not  merely  the  presence  of  algorithms but the stock and renewal of AI talent that designs, integrates, and scales models with data, architecture, and domain knowledge (Li et al., 2024). Under RBV, increasing the share of AI-skilled employees can raise value creation via innovation, efficiency, and analytics-driven decisions, elevating Tobin's Q (Bin-Nashwan & Li, 2025). Yet RBV also stresses appropriation hazards: when value creation depends on mobile experts, rents may be dissipated through bargaining and turnover unless governance and pay systems support knowledge integration and reduce hold-up risks (Coff, 1997; Fosso Wamba et al., 2024; Sirmon et al., 2007; Teece, 2007).

Tournament theory models compensation as prizes that elicit effort in  rank-order  contests  (Lazear & Rosen,  1981;  Rosen,  1986).  Larger spreads  between  organizational  tiers  can  motivate  contestants  when output is noisy and monitoring is difficult. However, research on pay dispersion shows that, beyond modest spreads, inequality can impair cooperation,  undermine  fairness  perceptions,  and  raise  coordination and turnover costs effects that are salient where tasks are interdependent (Bloom, 1999; Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993; Shaw et al., 2002). Accumulated evidence suggests that performance consequences  of  dispersion  hinge  on  context  (Connelly  et  al.,  2014; Wade et al., 2006). For top management teams (TMTs), extreme vertical pay gaps damage collaboration with the broader workforce, offsetting incentives.

Integrating  these  theories  clarifies  our  study's  relationships.  First, RBV predicts that building a larger, denser stock of AI talent creates a rarer resource base and stronger capabilities for sensing, seizing, and transforming  opportunities  in  data-rich,  dynamic  markets  (Teece, 2007). Second, as firms broaden AI expertise beyond the apex, value creation becomes more distributed across technical and cross-functional roles (Hughes et al., 2025). Organizations that depend on collaborative AI pipelines (i.e., data engineering, MLOps, product, operations) recalibrate pay to recruit and retain scarce mid-tier specialists, narrowing TMT-to-employee ratios (Yuan et al., 2025). RBV's appropriation logic implies that diffusing specialized knowledge can reduce bargaining-power  asymmetries,  attenuating  extreme  prize  spreads. Third, tournament theory implies potential incentive benefits, but in AI-enabled service organizations, where innovation and service quality hinge on knowledge sharing, excessive dispersion depresses cooperation and learning, harming value capture (Bamberger et al., 2021). AI human capital therefore enhances value directly (via capabilities) and indirectly by reshaping rewards in ways that improve collaboration and reduce coordination  frictions  (Liu & Zhang,  2025).  Sirmon  et  al.  (2007) emphasize  that  value realization requires resource orchestration, structuring, bundling, and leveraging human assets. Pay systems play a critical  role.  When  wage  dispersion  aligns  with  levels  conducive  to interdependence, firms are better able to translate AI knowledge into scalable routines, thereby enhancing Tobin's Q. In turbulent environments, the returns to AI talent and collaborative capacity are amplified, thereby  strengthening  the  AI -value  relationship  in  line  with  the resource-based view and dynamic capabilities perspective.

## 3. Conceptual framework and hypotheses development

Guided by the resource-based view and tournament theory (Barney, 1991, 1996; Bognanno, 2001; Lazear, 1989), we developed a model that links  firms ' AI  human  capital  intensity  to  wage  inequality  and  firm value. RBV motivates the core pathway: AI human capital constitutes a firm-specific,  hard-to-imitate  resource  that  enhances  innovation,  process  efficiency,  and  decision  quality,  thereby  generating  an  expected positive  association with firm value. Tournament theory clarifies the incentive and coordination consequences of the pay structure: excessive vertical pay  dispersion  undermines  collaboration  and  knowledge sharing, depressing value creation. Integrating these lenses, our model posits (i) a direct positive association between AI human capital and firm value; (ii) a negative association between AI human capital and wage inequality; and (iii) a negative association between wage inequality and firm  value,  yielding  partial  mediation  of  the  AI -value  link  through inequality. We further contend that environmental dynamism, complexity, and munificence shape the returns to AI human capital.

## 3.1. AI human capital intensity and firm value

Investments in artificial intelligence human capital, reflected in an expansion of the share of employees with AI-relevant skills, constitute a strategic resource with the potential to enhance firm value when effectively embedded in complementary organisational assets (Liu & Zhang, 2025).  Drawing  explicitly  on  the  resource-based  view,  AI-related expertise  in  areas  such  as  data  engineering,  machine  learning,  and human -AI  orchestration  can  satisfy  the  conditions  of  value,  rarity, imperfect  imitability,  and  non-substitutability  when  combined  with firm-specific data, architectures, and domain knowledge (Barney, 1991; Peteraf, 1993). In this sense, AI human capital represents a firm-level capability rather than a standalone technology input.

Dynamic capabilities theory further clarifies how AI human capital is translated  into  value  creation.  By  enhancing  firms'  abilities  to  sense opportunities, seize them through analytics-enabled decision making, and transform service and operational processes, AI skilled employees may  help  organizations  convert  technical  potential  into  competitive advantages that are reflected in market valuations such as Tobin's Q (Teece, 2007; Teece et al., 1997). Recent measurement advances identify  AI  workers  through  matched  job  postings  and  worker  profiles, allowing the construction of firm-year shares of AI talent that have been shown to predict innovation and growth outcomes (Babina et al., 2024). The primary value-creation channels are both productand process-based, as AI human capital is expected to support predictive analytics, personalization, and revenue management while also streamlining  operations,  thereby  aligning  with  higher  expected  cash flows and lower uncertainty (Shollo et al., 2022).

However, the resource-based view also emphasizes that returns to AI human capital are conditional rather than automatic. First, value realization  depends  on  complementarities  with  organisational  assets  and often unfolds with diffusion and implementation lags, consistent with the productivity paradox associated with advanced digital technologies (Brynjolfsson  et  al.,  2017).  Second,  appropriation  challenges  arise because AI-skilled employees are relatively mobile and their contributions are difficult to fully specify ex ante. Without effective resource orchestration  and  incentive  alignment,  value  may  dissipate  through turnover  or  bargaining  dynamics  (Coff,  1997;  Sirmon  et  al.,  2007). Third, misalignment between AI capabilities and work design can undermine  coordination, collaboration, and organisational learning, thereby attenuating performance gains (Adler & Borys, 1996; Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011).

These  mechanisms are  particularly  salient  in  tourism,  travel,  and hospitality, where demand volatility, high contact intensity, and operational  interdependence  amplify  the  importance  of  analytics-enabled decision making and service design. AI human capital with expertise in  data  engineering,  machine  learning,  and  human -AI  orchestration supports revenue management, personalization, and service operations under such conditions, thereby enhancing firm value when embedded in complementary routines and data infrastructures (Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Mariani & Borghi, 2021; Tussyadiah, 2020). Empirical evidence in hospitality and tourism shows that AI adoption improves forecasting accuracy, pricing strategies, and guest experiences, but that performance gains are contingent on complementary capabilities and organisational  redesign  (Buhalis & Law,  2008;  Gretzel  et  al.,  2015; Huang & Rust, 2018; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Li et al., 2025; Stylos et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Consistent with a human-capital-based explanation, recent labour-based measures indicate that higher firm-level shares of AI-skilled workers are associated with  innovation  and  growth  outcomes  (Babina  et  al.,  2024).  Taken together,  these  arguments  suggest  that  AI  human  capital  intensity operates  as  a  capability-building  mechanism  that  is  expected  to  be associated  with  firms ' ability  to  create  value  in  data-intensive  and service-oriented contexts. Accordingly, we propose.

H1 . AI human capital intensity is positively associated with firm value.

## 3.2. AI human capital intensity and wage inequality

Investments in artificial intelligence human capital, reflected in a growing share of employees with expertise in data, machine learning, and human -AI orchestration, can reshape firms' internal pay structures (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2019; Mo et al., 2024; Tambe et al., 2020). This  study's  distributional  predictions  are  grounded  in  tournament theory, which emphasizes how vertical pay differentials affect coordination, incentives, and performance within firms.

Tournament  theory  suggests  that  while  steep  pay  spreads  may motivate effort in settings with independent tasks, they can undermine cooperation, learning, and retention when production is highly interdependent (Bloom, 1999; Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993;  Shaw  et  al.,  2002).  These  coordination  costs  are  especially salient in AI-intensive service contexts, where effective deployment relies  on  tightly  coupled  pipelines  spanning  data  engineering,  model development, operations, and frontline service delivery (Huang & Rust, 2018). As firms expand their AI-skilled workforce, the bargaining power of mid-tier technical and cross-functional specialists increases, heightening the need for compensation systems that support collaboration and limit appropriation hazards (Coff, 1997; Li et al., 2019).

Empirical evidence using labour-based measures of AI skills shows that higher firm-level shares of AI-skilled employees are associated with changes in workforce composition that support innovation and growth (Babina et al., 2024). From a tournament-theoretic perspective, maintaining excessive vertical pay dispersion in such contexts weakens coordination and value capture, whereas more compressed pay structures facilitate effective orchestration of AI-related capabilities (Acemoglu & Restrepo,  2022;  Bloom,  1999;  Shaw  et  al.,  2002;  Wu  et  al.,  2024). Accordingly, we propose.

H2 . AI  human  capital  intensity  is  negatively  associated  with  wage inequality within firms.

## 3.3. Wage inequality and firm value

Wage inequality between top managers and employees has long been debated in relation to firm value. Tournament theory argues that wider pay  spreads  can  motivate  effort  and  ambition  by  strengthening advancement incentives, thereby enhancing performance when rewards are perceived as attainable (Bidwell et al., 2013; Lazear & Rosen, 1981; Rosen, 1986). However, organizational research highlights substantial costs associated with excessive pay dispersion. Large pay gaps can undermine perceptions of fairness, erode trust, and weaken collaboration, particularly in interdependent production settings (Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993). Consistent with this evidence,  meta-analyses  suggest  that  while  modest  inequality  may enhance  incentives,  its  marginal  benefits  diminish  and  eventually reverse as dispersion increases (Bamberger et al., 2021; Connelly et al., 2014).

These trade-offs are particularly salient in tourism, travel, and hospitality, where value creation depends on service quality, coordination, and  knowledge  sharing  across  organizational  levels.  Excessive  wage inequality can erode employee morale and cooperative climates that are essential for delivering integrated guest experiences (Cheng & Wong, 2015).  In  addition,  high  pay  ratios  expose  firms  to  reputational  and legitimacy  risks,  as  stakeholders  may  perceive  extreme  executive compensation as misaligned with good governance, thereby weakening brand trust and market valuation (Bebchuk & Fried, 2004; Fiss et al., 2012). In such contexts, wage inequality functions both as an internal coordination challenge and as an external legitimacy liability that constrains firms ' ability to translate resources into sustainable value creation. Accordingly, we propose.

H3 . Wage inequality is negatively associated with firm value.

## 3.4. The mediating role of wage inequality

Wage inequality can operate as a transmission mechanism linking AI human capital intensity to firm value by shaping internal coordination and incentive alignment rather than by directly generating capabilities. As AI-related work is organised around interdependent roles spanning data  engineering,  machine  learning,  and  operational  deployment, effective collaboration becomes critical for converting technical inputs into organisational outcomes (Huang & Rust, 2018). In such settings, steep vertical  pay differentials  can impede  integration  by weakening cooperation and increasing retention risks among pivotal non-executive specialists. Firms that expand their AI-skilled workforce therefore face incentives  to  recalibrate  compensation  structures  toward  these  roles, reducing excessive top-to-employee pay gaps in order to sustain coordination  and  limit  appropriation  hazards  (Barney,  1991;  Coff,  1997; Peteraf, 1993).

Tournament theory further clarifies why such adjustments matter for value  creation.  While  modest  pay  dispersion  can  motivate  effort, excessive inequality undermines perceptions of fairness, erodes trust, and  weakens  coordination,  thereby  increasing  turnover  and  agency costs and depressing value capture (Bloom, 1999; Cowherd & Levine, 1992;  Pfeffer & Langton,  1993;  Shaw  et  al.,  2002).  Although  a skill-biased  technological  change  perspective  predicts  that  scarce  AI skills  may  widen  wage  gaps  (Acemoglu & Restrepo,  2019),  recent firm-level evidence shows that higher shares of AI workers are associated with workforce shifts that support innovation and growth, consistent with broader diffusion of value creation beyond the apex (Babina et al., 2024).

These  mechanisms  are  especially  salient  in  tourism,  travel,  and hospitality, where value creation relies on tightly coupled frontstage and backstage processes and where coordination costs of high pay dispersion are particularly pronounced. By compressing excessive wage inequality, firms  can  improve  cooperation,  knowledge  sharing,  and  retention, thereby enhancing their ability to translate AI-related capabilities into superior market performance (Connelly et al., 2014; Wade et al., 2006). Taken together, these arguments suggest that AI human capital intensity reduces wage inequality, which in turn enhances firm value, implying a mediating relationship.

H4 . Wage  inequality  mediates  the  relationship  between  AI  human capital intensity and firm value.

## 3.5. The moderating role of industry environment

Industry environment conditions (i.e., dynamism, munificence, and complexity) are contingencies that shape how firms capture value from AI  human  capital  investments  (Mostafiz  et  al.,  2025).  Contingency theory  and  the  strategic  management  literature  argue  that  resource -performance relationships depend on external turbulence, slack, and information-processing  demands  (Dess & Beard,  1984;  Lawrence & Lorsch,  1967).  In  dynamic  environments,  marked  by  unpredictable changes in demand and technology, the marginal returns to AI talent rise because analytics, experimentation, and adaptive routines enable superior  sensing  and  seizing  opportunities  (Teece,  2007).  Complexity likewise increases the payoff to AI human capital by raising the value of data integration and cross-functional coordination, so AI-rich firms can process more information and orchestrate responses more effectively (Keats & Hitt, 1988; Miller & Friesen, 1983). Munificence is theoretically more ambiguous. Resource abundance can ease complementary investments and capability reconfiguration, particularly in interdependent TTH service systems (Barney, 1991; Huang & Rust, 2018; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Peteraf, 1993; Teece et al., 1997), yet slack can also weaken discipline, slow diffusion, and buffer coordination failures (Castrogiovanni, 1991).

Hospitality research similarly suggests that capability -performance links  strengthen  most  clearly  when  uncertainty  and coordination demands are high (Harrington, 2001a, 2001b; Jogaratnam & Tse, 2006; Tajeddini, Ratten, & Denisa, 2020). We therefore expect dynamism and complexity to amplify the AIHC -value relationship, while any positive role  of  munificence is more likely to emerge when resource richness facilitates  complementary  investment  under  sufficiently  dynamic  or complex conditions. This RBV -contingency view clarifies why similar AI investments  yield  heterogeneous  value  across  TTH  firms  (Lalicic & Weismayer, 2021). AI-enabled forecasting and personalization become more valuable as uncertainty rises (Kumar et al., 2019). Therefore, we suggest that.

H5a . The positive relationship between AI human capital intensity and firm value is stronger when industry dynamism is higher.

H5b . The relationship between AI human capital intensity and firm value  is  expected  to  become  more  positive  under  higher  industry munificence, particularly  when  munificence supports  complementary investment under dynamic or complex conditions.

H5c . The positive relationship between AI human capital intensity and firm value is stronger when industry complexity is higher.

## 4.2. Measures

Our  study  employed  several  measures  to  operationalize  the  core constructs  (i.e.,  AI  human  capital  investment,  wage  inequality,  firm value,  and  industry  environment).  These  variables  were  carefully defined and computed to ensure construct validity, comparability across firms,  and  theoretical  alignment.  To  ensure  alignment  between  our empirical design and the research questions, we selected measures that directly map onto the study's core constructs. Our independent variable captures the extent to which firms build AI capability through workforce composition, which is central to examining whether AI human capital intensity  relates  to  subsequent  firm  value  and  wage  inequality  outcomes.  Our  dependent  variables  capture,  respectively,  market-based value creation and within-firm vertical pay structure, which together reflect  the  study's  focus  on  both  performance  and  distributional implications.

AI Human Capital Investment. AI human capital was proxied by the share of AI-related workers in total employment at the firm-year level. This  measure  is  appropriate  because  it  captures  the  intensity  of  AI capability embedded in the workforce, which is the central mechanism through which the study theorises effects on value creation and wage structures.  Following  and  Babina  et  al.  (2024) and  Liu  and  Zhang (2025), we operationalize AI human capital as the firm-year share of employees  with  AI-relevant  roles  or  skills,  which  we  interpret  as  AI human capital intensity  in  the  workforce.  We  use  this  measure  as  a revealed indicator of firms' investment outcomes in recruiting, developing,  and  retaining  AI-relevant  talent,  rather  than  as  a  direct  accounting measure of AI spending. Importantly, this operationalization does not presume that AI is fully embedded in frontline service delivery across all tourism, travel, and hospitality firms. Even when firms rely on externally developed systems, effective deployment in AI-enabled services  requires  internal  capabilities  for  data  and  integration  work, analytics-enabled work design, governance, and human -AI orchestration, particularly given task interdependence in service settings (Huang & Rust, 2018). This perspective is consistent with the sector's evolution from eTourism toward smart tourism ecosystems, in which value creation increasingly depends on firm-level digital and analytics capabilities spanning  frontstage  and  backstage  processes  (Buhalis & Law,  2008; Gretzel et al., 2015; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Li et al., 2025; Mariani & Borghi, 2021; Stylos et al., 2025; Tussyadiah, 2020; Zhang et al., 2026).

To measure AI human capital intensity (AIHC), we identify AI-skilled workers by matching AI-related skill tags in worker profiles and job postings  to  individuals ' employment  records.  AI-related  skills  are defined using a curated dictionary of AI and machine-learning competencies, including core methods (e.g., machine learning, deep learning, neural  networks,  reinforcement  learning),  application  domains  (e.g., natural language processing, computer vision, recommender systems), and  common  AI  toolkits  and  deployment  skills  (e.g.,  TensorFlow, PyTorch, Keras, scikit-learn,  and  MLOps-related  tools).  Generic  skills that are not specific to AI (e.g., spreadsheet skills or programming languages without an AI/ML context) are not sufficient for AI classification. Skill extraction is applied consistently across the full dataset using the same dictionary and rules, after which profiles and postings are linked to firms through standardized employer identifiers and aggregated to the firm-year level to compute AIHC as the percentage share of AI-skilled workers in total employment. To strengthen face validity, Appendix A supplements this procedure with role-title crosswalk and role-evolution examples that translate the captured skill tags into concrete TTH job families and use cases. ## 4.3. Generalized method of moments (GMM) approach

Consistent with prior work on organizational outcomes (Lam et al., 2016;  Mukherji et  al.,  2011;  Zhu  et  al.,  2021),  we  adopt  a  dynamic panel data (DPD) approach in which the one-period lag of the dependent variable  enters  the  model  as  a  regressor.  This  term  captures  path dependence (i.e., performance in t 1 influencing performance in t) and improves the fidelity of short-run adjustment dynamics. In addition, we align  the  timing  of  covariates  and  outcomes  to  preserve  temporal ordering. Key predictors, including AI human capital investment, are measured at year t, whereas firm value (Tobin's Q) is observed at t + 1. Accordingly,  each  specification  includes  both  the  lagged  dependent variable and a one-year lead of the outcome relative to the predictors.

This design raises three econometric concerns. First, including the lagged dependent variable alongside firm fixed effects induces correlation between regressors and the composite error term, producing the well-known dynamic panel bias (Nickell, 1981). Second, our panel is unbalanced because some firm-years lack inputs required to construct Tobin's  Q  or  wage  inequality,  a  common  feature  of  disclosure-based datasets.  Third,  potential  endogeneity  affects  key  regressors  (i.e.,  AI human capital may shape wage inequality and firm value) but feedback from organizational performance to strategic choices (e.g., investment in AI capabilities) is also plausible (Bardhan et al., 2013; Lam et al., 2016).  Ignoring  such  reverse  causality  and  simultaneity  would  risk overstating the impact of AI human capital on the outcomes of interest.

To address these issues, we developed the models using the system generalized method of moments (system GMM) estimator of Arellano and Bover (1995) and Blundell and Bond (1998). System GMM jointly exploits a differenced equation (instrumented with lagged levels) and a levels equation (instrumented with lagged differences), generating internal  instruments  that  are  orthogonal  to  the  fixed  effects  and  thus mitigating dynamic panel bias (Roodman, 2009). The estimator is well suited to unbalanced panels with endogenous regressors and has been characterized as one of the most robust technologies for such contexts (Flannery & Hankins, 2013). In our application, potentially endogenous variables  (e.g.,  AI  human  capital,  wage  inequality)  are  treated  as endogenous or predetermined and instrumented with their appropriate lags,  while  strictly  exogenous  controls  enter  uninstrumented.  We emphasize  that  this  is  an  internal-instrument  strategy  rather  than  a claim of a separately identified external-IV design for AIHC; because no exclusion restriction appeared sufficiently defensible in this setting, we avoid forcing a weak external instrument and instead triangulate the dynamic estimates with static firm fixed-effects benchmarks reported in Appendix C. We implement instrument-set management (e.g., collapsing the instrument matrix and limiting lag depth) to curb proliferation and safeguard  the  reliability  of  over-identification  tests,  following  best practice (Roodman, 2009).

In the reported specifications, the GMM style instrument window for the lagged dependent variable, AIHC, WAGE, and interaction terms is restricted to lags t minus 2 and t minus 3 in the differenced equation. The corresponding levels equation uses lagged first differences as internal instruments, while year and industry indicators are entered as standard instruments. The instrument matrix is collapsed and lag depth is capped in all main models, yielding 120 to 130 instruments for the Tobin Q specifications and 115 instruments for the WAGE specification, which is far  below  the  1206  firm  groups.  Strictly  exogenous  controls  are  not expanded  as  GMM  style  instruments.  Appendix  B,  Table  B1 and Appendix C, Table C6 report the instrument counts, alternative lag caps, and proliferation diagnostics.

All  estimations  are  conducted  in  Stata  16  using  two-step  system GMM with finite-sample, robust standard errors. The two-step estimator improves efficiency and is robust to heteroskedasticity; we apply the Windmeijer  finite-sample  correction  to  adjust  the  downward  bias  in two-step standard errors (Roodman, 2009). As diagnostic checks, we report the Arellano -Bond tests for serial correlation in the first-differenced  residuals  and  the  Hansen  test  of  over-identifying  restrictions and acceptable p-values on these statistics provide evidence consistent  with  valid  instruments  and  correct  model  specification. Collectively, this estimation strategy aligns the identification approach with  the  theoretical  timing  of  effects,  while  directly  confronting  dynamic  bias,  unbalanced  panels,  and  endogeneity  with  an  estimator designed for such settings.

## 4.5. Endogeneity

Endogeneity is a first-order concern in our setting because the focal outcomes and strategic choices are jointly determined over time, the data are observational, and several regressors may be measured with error. Following established econometric guidance (Angrist & Pischke, 2009; Wooldridge, 2010), we consider three primary sources: (i) dynamic persistence (past firm value influencing current outcomes), (ii) omitted time-varying firm- and industry-level factors, and (iii) simultaneity and reverse causality, whereby firms adjust AI human capital investment  and  compensation  structures  in  response  to  performance (Roberts & Whited, 2013; Wintoki et al., 2012). To address these concerns, we adopt an identification strategy that aligns econometric design with the theoretical timing of effects.

First,  we  estimate  dynamic  panel  models  that  include  the  lagged dependent variable to capture persistence. Because this term is correlated  with  unobserved  firm  effects,  standard  pooled  OLS  and  fixedeffects estimators are biased in short panels (Nickell, 1981). We therefore  employ  the  system  GMM  estimator  (Arellano & Bover,  1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998), which combines equations in differences and levels  and  uses  internal  lagged  instruments.  This  approach  mitigates dynamic  panel  bias  while  improving  efficiency  through  additional moment conditions (Bond, 2002; Roodman, 2009).

Second, we treat AI human capital intensity and wage inequality as endogenous (or predetermined) and instrument them using deeper lags within the system GMM framework. Firm-level controls are treated as predetermined, while year and industry fixed effects absorb common shocks and time-invariant heterogeneity. Regressors are lagged to preserve temporal ordering, and internal instruments are constructed using deeper lags (e.g., t 2, t 3). Importantly, we do not rely on an external instrumental variable for AIHC. A credible external IV that satisfies both relevance and exclusion restrictions is difficult to justify in this context, as most plausible instruments would directly affect firm value. Instead, identification relies on temporal ordering, internal instrumentation, and diagnostic validation, which are more defensible in this setting. Specifically, AIHC, WAGE, the lagged dependent variable, and interaction terms are instrumented using the collapsed lag window t minus 2 to t minus 3. Firm size, age, and profitability enter with one-year lags as controls, and year and industry effects enter as standard instruments. This specification is reported consistently in the notes to Table 3 and in Appendix B.

Third, to limit finite-sample bias and instrument proliferation, we follow  best  practices  in  system  GMM  implementation  (Flannery & Hankins, 2013; Roodman, 2009). Specifically, we collapse the instrument matrix, restrict lag depth, avoid instrumenting strictly exogenous variables,  and  ensure  that  the  number  of  instruments  remains  well below  the  number  of  firms.  Estimation  is  conducted  using  two-step system GMM with Windmeijer-corrected standard errors.

We assess model validity using standard diagnostics, including Arellano -Bond tests  for  serial  correlation  and  Hansen  and  difference-inHansen tests of over-identifying restrictions. Across specifications, AR (2) tests fail to reject the null of no second-order serial correlation, and Hansen tests yield acceptable p-values, supporting instrument validity. Additional robustness checks vary lag structures, instrument sets, and sample composition, supporting the view that results are not artifacts of outliers or specific time periods. Moreover, we complement the dynamic analysis with static panel estimations (pooled OLS, random effects, and firm fixed effects) reported in Appendix E (Table E1). These models yield directionally consistent results, although coefficients are more conservative in magnitude, reflecting the absence of dynamic adjustment and explicit treatment of endogeneity. The comparison highlights that part of the cross-sectional association reflects persistent firm heterogeneity, reinforcing the need for dynamic modeling. Additional supporting evidence is provided through placebo lead tests and alternative lag specifications (Appendix E, Table E2), which show that future AIHC does not predict  current  outcomes  and  that  lagged  effects  remain  stable  but attenuated. Together, these results are consistent with a temporally ordered relationship rather than reverse causality. Taken together, while no observational design can fully eliminate endogeneity concerns, the combination of dynamic specification, internal instrumentation, diagnostic testing, and benchmark static models substantially strengthens identification. Accordingly, the reported relationships should be interpreted as robust evidence consistent with the theorized mechanisms, while acknowledging the usual limitations of observational firm-level research.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Key findings

Our dynamic panel analysis yields four principal findings. First, AI human capital intensity exhibits a positive association with firm value (Tobin's Q), net of persistence and controls. This result is consistent with the resource-based view. AIHC is a scarce, firm-embedded capability that,  when  combined  with  data  assets  and  complementary  routines, generates rents observable in market valuations (Barney, 1991; Peteraf, 1993). It also accords with recent evidence that AI intensity is associated with  growth  and  innovation  (Babina  et  al.,  2024)  and  with  service scholarship on AI-enabled productivity and customer experience (Huang & Rust, 2018). Within tourism, travel, and hospitality, prior work documents  value  creation  through  analytics-driven  personalization  and operational  automation  (Buhalis & Law,  2008;  Gretzel  et  al.,  2015; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b).

Second, AIHC is associated with lower wage inequality (WAGE), and WAGE, in turn, is negatively related to Tobin's Q, yielding a statistically significant  partial  mediation.  The  WAGE → value  link  aligns  with research showing that wide pay dispersion undermines collaboration, quality, and retention (Bloom, 1999; Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993). At the same time, our evidence contrasts with a strict tournament-theory expectation that higher vertical dispersion should enhance incentives and performance (Bamberger et al., 2021; Connelly et al., 2014). We contend that the service-system interdependence that typifies TTH moderates these incentive benefits: where task coupling and  knowledge  sharing  are  high,  dispersion  harms  team  production (Shaw et al., 2002), making equity effects salient. AIHC may compress inequality by raising the bargaining power and contribution of technical and mid-tier  employees and by pushing firms to redesign workflows around human -AI collaboration mechanisms consistent with complementarity arguments in the technology -human  capital literature (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019).

Third, the AIHC -value relationship strengthens with environmental dynamism and complexity, while the average linear munificence interaction is not supported. These interactions cohere with contingency and dynamic-capabilities logics: under turbulence and heterogeneity, analytics,  experimentation,  and  cross-functional  orchestration  expand AIHC ' s marginal return (Dess & Beard, 1984; Keats & Hitt, 1988; Teece, 2007). By contrast, the null average munificence effect mirrors arguments that slack can relax selection pressures and allow slower diffusion of best practices (Castrogiovanni, 1991). Our exploratory appendix analyses suggest that munificence becomes more supportive only when it coexists with high dynamism or complexity, which helps reconcile why slack does not systematically translate into stronger AI returns on its own. Finally, sectoral analyses reveal meaningful heterogeneity. AIHC ' s association with Tobin ' s Q is largest in Travel, which faces volatile demand and multi-sided distribution, and remains positive in Hospitality and Tourism; by contrast, AIHC ' s inequality-reducing effect is strongest in Hospitality, a labour-intensive, service-rich context. These patterns are compatible with theory in which travel firms exploit AIHC for agile pricing, routing, and capacity decisions amid high uncertainty, whereas hospitality firms realize equity and coordination gains from diffusing AI skills across interdependent frontline roles. Across sectors, however, the core mechanisms are invariant. AIHC is associated with higher value both  directly  and  indirectly  through  lower  inequality,  and  environmental turbulence is associated with larger payoffs.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

Our findings advance theory at the intersection of the resource-based view (RBV), pay dispersion and tournament logics, and contingency/ dynamic capabilities in service industries. First, we reconceptualize AI human capital, the firm-embedded stock of employees with AI-related skills as an RBV microfoundation. AIHC is valuable because it enables data-driven problem solving, experimentation, and human -AI orchestration.  It  is  rare  and  imperfectly  tradable  because  skills  are  cospecialized with firm data, workflows, and domain knowledge and it is difficult to imitate because benefits hinge on complementarities with routines  and  governance  (Barney,  1991;  Clark  et  al.,  2026;  Peteraf, 1993;  Sirmon  et  al.,  2007).  The  positive  AIHC -value  association  we document  extends  RBV  by  specifying  the  human-capital  pathway through which AI capability is associated with market valuation in TTH, complementing evidence that AI intensity fuels growth and innovation (Babina et al., 2024) and service research on AI-enabled productivity and experience (Huang & Rust, 2018; Ivanov & Webster, 2019a, 2019b; Marchesani & Testa, 2026; Mostafiz et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025).

Second, by showing that wage inequality (WAGE) partially mediates the AIHC -value link, we integrate RBV with socio-structural accounts of performance  in  services.  Prior  work  documents  that  vertical  pay dispersion  can  erode  collaboration,  knowledge  sharing,  and  quality (Bloom, 1999; Campos-Soria et al., 2009; Casado-Díaz & Sim ´ on, 2016; Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993), counter to strict tournament predictions of stronger incentives (Connelly et al., 2014; de G. Agag et al.

[S. 15]

Castro Romero, Barroso, & Santero-S ´ anchez, 2025; Marfil-Cotilla et al., 2024).  Our  evidence  identifies  boundary  conditions  for  tournament theory,  where task interdependence and co-production are high, dispersion's incentive benefits are dominated by coordination and fairness costs (Shaw et al., 2002). AIHC may compress WAGE by elevating the contribution and bargaining power of technical and mid-tier roles and by  prompting  work  redesign  around  human -AI  complementarities (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019; Guimar ˜ aes & Silva, 2016; Lee & Kang, 1998), improving performance indirectly and directly.

Third,  the  moderating  effects  refine  contingency  and  dynamiccapabilities  arguments.  We  find  that  environmental  dynamism  and complexity amplify the payoff to AIHC, consistent with the view that sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring depend on information processing and  cross-functional  integration  when  uncertainty  and  heterogeneity are high (Dess & Beard, 1984; Keats & Hitt,  1988;  Teece, 2007). By contrast, munificence does not reliably strengthen the AIHC -value link in  the  average  linear  model,  aligning  with  the  idea  that  slack  may attenuate selection pressures and slow diffusion of process innovations (Castrogiovanni, 1991). The exploratory appendix evidence nevertheless  suggests  a  more  conditional  role  for  munificence  when  resource richness is paired with higher dynamism or complexity. These results specify when AIHC is most consequential and caution against assuming resource abundance alone magnifies returns. Fourth, sectoral heterogeneity offers theoretically meaningful boundary conditions for generalization.  AIHC's  association  with  firm  value  is  strongest  in  Travel, where  volatility,  networked  distribution,  and  capacity  coordination heighten the value of predictive and  integrative skills, and  its inequality-reducing effect is greatest in Hospitality, a labour-intensive context  where  team  production  is  central.  The  pattern  suggests  that service  architecture,  customer  contact,  modularity,  and  interdependence condition whether AIHC creates value mainly through market agility or internal equity and coordination.

Finally, our study contributes methodologically to theorizing capabilities  by  operationalizing  AIHC  with  a  labor-based  measure  and embedding mediation and moderation within a dynamic panel framework. Using lag structure and system GMM to address persistence and endogeneity links microfoundations to outcomes without external instruments (Roodman, 2009). Conceptually, positioning wage inequality as a mechanism clarifies how socio-technical transformation translates to value, connecting RBV, pay dispersion, and contingency perspectives in services.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

Building on our dynamic panel results, several actionable implications  emerge  for  senior  managers  in  tourism,  travel,  and  hospitality. First, the positive association between AI human capital and firm value suggests  that  firms  should  treat  AI-related  skills  as  a  strategic  asset rather than a discrete IT purchase. This extends beyond hiring a few data scientists. Hotels and airlines benefit from hybrid roles that combine domain expertise with analytics (e.g., revenue managers who code and operations leaders who interpret models). Hotel groups deploy demandforecasting and price-optimization engines to align rates with booking curves, while airlines use machine-learning systems to adjust fares and ancillary bundles, both rely on staff who translate model outputs into decisions.  Online  travel  intermediaries  likewise  personalize  search through recommenders and experimentation. Success requires a shared analytics vocabulary across product, engineering, and data teams.

Second, because wage inequality (WAGE) is negatively associated with  firm  value  and  partially  mediates  the  AIHC -value  link,  leaders should  anticipate  and  manage  distributional  consequences  as  AI  diffuses. Redesign career ladders and pay bands so that AI-complementary frontline and mid-tier roles gain recognition and progression. Productivity gains from AI-assisted scheduling and service-recovery chat are more durable when paired with upskilling budgets, certification pathways,  and  transparent  bonuses  tied  to  team  outcomes  rather  than individual sales. Tracking an equity dashboard pay ratios, promotion velocity, internal mobility can alert executives to unintended widening of dispersion during transformation.

Third,  our  moderation  results  imply  that  context  matters.  Where demand and product complexity are high, hub-and-spoke airline networks, multi-property hotel portfolios, multi-channel tour operators the returns to AIHC are amplified. Managers in such environments should prioritize use cases that exploit uncertainty (e.g., near-real-time pricing, dynamic capacity allocation, and predictive staffing that flexes housekeeping,  concierge,  and  check-in  resources  by  arrival  pattern).  By contrast, munificent environments do not automatically strengthen AI payoffs,  thus,  slack  can  mask  execution  problems and slow learning. Governance should therefore emphasize disciplined test-and-learn cycles, post-mortems, and clear stage-gates for scaling pilots.

Fourth, sectoral heterogeneity points to distinct roadmaps. In Travel, where volatility and network coordination are first-order constraints, emphasize  AIHC  for  integrated  revenue  management,  disruption  recovery, and irregular-operations decision support. In Hospitality, prioritize  broad  workforce  upskilling  AI-assisted  housekeeping  routing, personalized service scripts, and demand-based labor planning paired with equity safeguards that keep teams cohesive. In Tourism attractions, focus  on  guest-flow  forecasting,  dynamic  ticketing  windows,  and  AIaided crowd management to protect experience quality without overstaffing.

Fifth,  societally,  fairer  pay  structures  and  predictable  operations enhance  worker  wellbeing  and  reduce  waste  and  emissions  through better  forecasting  and  capacity  use.  By  providing  evidence  that  AI human capital is associated with lower wage inequality and higher firm value, our study motivates investments that spread AI skills beyond a small elite, improving dignity at work, career progression, and retention.  More  accurate  demand  prediction  allows  hotels  and  airlines  to schedule staffing, energy, and inventory to actual needs, cutting food waste, idle energy  loads, and  unnecessary  trips.  These  changes strengthen  household  income  stability,  service  reliability,  and  local destination resilience, while advancing inclusive, low-carbon growth.

Finally, sequence implementation: (1) build a minimally sufficient data  foundation  (clean  reservations,  POS,  and  operations  data;  clear ownership);  (2)  appoint  a  cross-functional  AI  steering  group  that  includes HR/compensation; (3) start with two or three high-ROI use cases with  pre-registered  evaluation  plans  and  success  metrics  (RevPAR, ancillary revenue per passenger, guest-satisfaction recovery); (4) institutionalize capability transfer communities  of  practice, code repositories, and bite-size training to avoid dependency on a few experts. These steps translate the study's message into practice: invest in AIHC, redesign work and rewards to sustain equity, tailor to environmental complexity and dynamism, and scale through disciplined learning.

## 6.4. Implications for policymakers

Findings from this study yield several implications for policymakers seeking  to  enhance  productivity,  equity,  and  resilience  in  tourism, travel,  and  hospitality.  First,  because  AI  human  capital  is  positively associated with firm value, skills policy should prioritize human-capital formation alongside digital capital. Targeted training tax credits, apprenticeships in data engineering and revenue analytics, and industry university  programmes can accelerate AIHC accumulation, especially for SMEs facing fixed-cost barriers. Real-world deployments, hotel demand forecasting, airline fare optimization and disruption recovery, and timed-entry management at attractions require hybrid roles blending domain knowledge and analytics and public policy can underwrite these pipelines at scale.

Second,  the  evidence  that  wage  inequality  (WAGE)  is  negatively associated with firm value, and partially mediates the AIHC -value link, suggests  that  inclusive  AI  diffusion  may  be  economically  beneficial. Policymakers should attach training and pay progression conditionalities to AI adoption grants (e.g., certification pathways for frontline and G. Agag et al.

[S. 16]

mid-tier roles using AI tools, team-based bonuses). Requiring firms to track  equity  dashboards,  pay  ratios,  promotion  velocity,  internal mobility can deter widening dispersion. Given pronounced inequality compression in hospitality, hospitality-specific upskilling vouchers and recognition  of  AI-complementary  competencies  are  likely  to  yield outsized returns.

Third, moderation results indicate that dynamism and complexity are associated with larger AIHC returns, whereas munificence does not. Consequently, generic subsidies are less effective than context-sensitive instruments. Policymakers should prioritize high-uncertainty use cases dynamic  pricing, predictive staffing, capacity allocation through competitive challenge funds and regulatory sandboxes. In parallel, investment in data collaboratives linking bookings and POS data would enable smaller operators to participate in AI benefits otherwise limited to large platforms.

Fourth,  algorithmic  governance  must  keep  pace  with  commercial practice.  Consumer-facing  safeguards algorithmic impact  assessments for personalized pricing and external audits for algorithmic collusion can preserve trust while allowing innovation. Agencies should clarify acceptable  data-sharing  and  pricing  conduct  for  OTAs,  airlines,  and hotel consortia to reduce legal uncertainty.

Finally, policy should respect sectoral heterogeneity. In travel, investment in operations-research capability for disruption management has  high  social  returns.  In  hospitality,  where  our  evidence  indicates stronger inequality compression, emphasis should fall on broad workforce upskilling and progression. Tourism attractions benefit from grants for guest-flow analytics and timed-entry systems that protect experience quality. Together, these instruments translate the evidence into a policy portfolio that scales AI value, safeguards equity, and fosters competitive markets.

## 7. Limitations and future research directions

Several  limitations  suggest  caution  in  interpretation  and  point  to fruitful avenues for future inquiry. First, a key limitation of this study is that the evidence is based on UK-listed tourism, travel, and hospitality firms, and the magnitude and form of the observed relationships may depend  on  institutional  features  of  the  UK  setting.  The  UK  context combines comparatively strong disclosure and governance requirements for  listed  firms  and  market-based  valuation  mechanisms,  which  may heighten the  visibility  of  AI  human  capital  effects  in  firm  value  and improve the measurement of executive pay structures, while labourmarket  institutions  and  wage-setting  practices  may  shape  how  AIrelated  skills  translate  into  within-firm  pay  dispersion.  Accordingly, although the underlying mechanisms linking AI human capital intensity, wage inequality, and firm value are theoretically applicable to service economies  more  broadly,  their  empirical  expression  may  differ  in countries  with  more  coordinated  wage  bargaining,  stronger  employment  protection,  or  different  regulatory  and  capital-market  regimes. Future research should therefore replicate and extend our design across institutional contexts and sectors to test boundary conditions and assess whether AI human capital generates similar performance and distributional implications beyond the UK and beyond service-intensive environments. Second, although we  model  dynamics  using  internalinstrument system GMM and benchmark the core relationships against static firm fixed-effects panels, observational data cannot fully resolve remaining  concerns  about  reverse  causality,  measurement  error,  and instrument weakness. We do not claim a separately identified externalIV  design  for  AIHC,  because  any  exclusion  restriction  in  this  setting would be difficult to defend. Dynamic panel estimators are sensitive to instrument  proliferation,  stationarity  assumptions,  and  lag-selection choices.  While  our diagnostics are acceptable, small departures from these assumptions can bias estimates (Roodman, 2009). Future research could complement our approach with designs that exploit quasi-experimental variation (e.g., staggered regulatory shocks, cloud-cost shocks, or instruments based on exogenous exposure to AI

toolsets), as well as with matched difference-in-differences at the subsector  level.  Consistent  with  this  limitation,  the  manuscript  now  interprets  the  estimated  coefficients  as  robust  conditional  associations rather than definitive causal effects. This wording is deliberate because the  system  GMM  design  improves  temporal  ordering  and  addresses several endogeneity concerns, but it cannot fully substitute for exogenous assignment of AI human capital.

Third, measurement constraints warrant further work. Our AI human capital indicator captures the share of AI-skilled workers but not the depth  of  their  expertise,  the  quality  of  human -AI  orchestration,  or complementarities  with  data  assets  and  routines.  Likewise,  wage inequality (WAGE) is constructed from financial disclosures and may miss  within-grade  dispersion  and  non-wage  forms  of  reward.  Richer micro-data  linking  payroll,  task  assignments,  and  skill  taxonomies would illuminate how resources are orchestrated into capabilities and outcomes (Sirmon et al., 2007). Future studies could disentangle distinct AIHC bundles (e.g., data engineering vs. ML operations vs. human-AI interface design) and test how specific complements (data governance, experimentation cadence, frontline autonomy) condition performance and equity. Fourth, external validity is bounded. Our context is UK-listed tourism,  travel,  and  hospitality  firms.  Governance  and  remuneration practices,  labour  institutions,  and  platform  competition  differ  across countries and among small and medium-sized enterprises. Comparative, multi-country panels would enable tests of institutional contingencies and  cultural  moderators.  In  addition,  the  2018 -2024  window  spans pandemic and recovery phases that may alter the returns to AIHC and the salience of wage structures. Replication in more stable periods and in adjacent service domains (e.g., retailing, logistics) would help separate structural effects from shock-specific dynamics. Finally, our moderation tests use standard proxies for dynamism, munificence, and complexity; these may be coarse relative to the fine-grained environmental turbulence faced by multi-channel service firms. Future work could integrate digital-market  metrics (e.g.,  search  volatility,  platform  algorithm  updates) and customer-journey heterogeneity, and model how dynamic capabilities  sensing,  seizing,  reconfiguring  mediate  the  translation  of AIHC into value under varying conditions (Teece, 2007). Longitudinal case -panel hybrids, combining archival data with process tracing inside firms, would clarify the mechanisms linking AIHC, wage structures, and firm value over time.

## Impact statement

This study explains how tourism, travel, and hospitality firms can pursue artificial intelligence without widening organizational inequality. The  findings support investment in AI skills beyond a small technical elite by linking workforce  capability,  fairer  pay  structures,  service  reliability,  and competitiveness. For managers, the results encourage job redesign, AI upskilling,  and  transparent  progression  pathways.
