## 1. Introduction

They also help physicians  to  provide  more  accurate  diagnostics  and  reduce  burnout from  paperwork  and  administrative  tasks. ## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. AI adoption in healthcare

The literature offers several definitions of AI. Some studies view AI as a machine (McCarthy et al., 1955), an agent, or a system (Poole et al., 1998)  that  can  perform  as  human  brains  do.  Others  focus  on  such technologies  as  machine  learning,  deep  learning,  natural  language processing, cognitive computing, neural networks, and similar systems that allow machines to display human intelligence (Agrawal et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020). Most scholars agree that AI can process, analyze, and interpret data to learn to make decisions independently to achieve objectives and tasks (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Paschen, Wilson, & Ferreira, 2020).

In  healthcare  settings,  major  AI  technologies  being  used  include natural  language  processing,  machine  learning,  and  deep  learning. Natural language processing is the ability to analyze human language, which enables computers to recognize, interpret, and manipulate human language (Paschen, Pitt, & Kietzmann, 2020). Healthcare systems use natural language-processing techniques to extract relevant information from narrative texts or images to provide data for clinical decision systems.  Machine  learning  refers  to  a  system ' s  ability  to  continuously improve its capabilities and performance of a specific task by progressively analyzing and acquiring knowledge from data (Murphy, 2012). Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that allows computers to gain knowledge from experience and comprehend the world through a hierarchy of concepts (Goodfellow et al., 2018). Machine learning and deep learning have been used to process patients ' medical records to improve diagnoses and clinical decision-making.

The healthcare operations within an organization encompass activities pertaining to finance, administration, and quality enhancement, all aimed  at  fortifying  fundamental  treatment  functions.  Some  of  the recurring challenges of healthcare services are enhancing patient outcomes and reducing high costs while improving efficiency (Andel et al., 2012; Porter et al., 2021; Weiner et al., 2020). Many healthcare organizations are adopting AI technologies over traditional ones to tackle the above issues thanks to AI ' s distinct ability to gather and process data to produce  a  definite  output  for  the  end  user.  While  AI  technologies, models, and applications in healthcare are developing at an increasing rate and their promising benefits in improving healthcare delivery are evident (Fazal et al., 2018; Hassanzadeh et al., 2019), medical practitioners  are  slow  to  adopt  them  in  current  healthcare  processes  (Pan et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2021). Existing literature highlights numerous challenges impeding the adoption of AI in healthcare. Prominent concerns include factors such as culture and personnel, profitability, technology, and data (Fan et al., 2020; Panch et al., 2019; Sun & Medaglia, 2019).

## 2.2. Determinants of AI adoption in healthcare

Empirical research on AI adoption in healthcare has tried to explore what factors enable users (Alaiad & Zhou, 2014; Saheb, 2020; Shareef et al., 2021) and organizations (Benzidia et al., 2021; Damoah et al., 2021; Leone et al., 2021) to embrace this technology. In terms of users, factors impacting adoption behaviors are different between healthcare professionals (e.g., doctors, nurses, administrators, etc.) and patients. For  healthcare  professionals,  initial  trust,  performance  expectancy, belief, and previous experience in these AI applications (Fan et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2019; Prakash & Das, 2021) are among the factors facilitating favorable opinions of AI. For patients, the major concerns when deciding to use AI technology include personalization, social influence, data risk, and trust (Alaiad & Zhou, 2014; Saheb, 2020; Shareef et al., 2021).

Despite the amount of attention on AI adoption in healthcare at the individual level, empirical research on the factors that shape AI adoption in healthcare organizations is limited (Khanijahani et al., 2022). ## 3. Conceptual framework and hypotheses development

## 3.1. Organizational information processing theory (OIPT)

According  to  the  Organizational  Information  Processing  Theory (OIPT), as an organization expands, it assimilates diverse internal and external  processes,  thereby  inheriting  their  inherent  complexity  and unpredictability (Thompson, 1967). This uncertainty stems from factors such as standard operational procedures, interdependence among subunits, and the dynamic nature of the external environment (Gattiker & Goodhue,  2004).  In  response  to  this  uncertainty,  organizations  may choose  to  enhance  their  information  processing  capacity,  a  strategic investment aimed at proficiently managing information flow (Galbraith, 1974).

Hospitals are a domain that considerably necessitates an augmented information-processing capacity. First, modern hospitals have various interdependent  departments,  each  possessing  distinct  objectives  and priorities. Despite their differences, these departments must collaborate harmoniously to provide an expanding array of services. Second, hospitals face the challenge of addressing the diversity in treatment outcomes due to the varying characteristics,  conditions,  and  severity  of illnesses among patients (Kravitz et al., 2004). Third, a high CMI results in greater uncertainty in clinical decision-making, primarily due to the intricacies of diagnosing and treating patients within a complex case mix. Consequently, the provision of accurate and timely patient clinical information  becomes  imperative  to  alleviate  this  uncertainty  and enhance the quality of clinical decision-making.

As  outlined  by  the  OIPT,  organizations,  if  equipped  with  solid information-processing capabilities, would be able to predict environmental  changes,  operate  efficiently,  and  consequently  attain high  organizational  performance  (Galbraith,  1974).  In  line  with  the OIPT,  AI  in  this  study  is  conceptualized  as  a  tool  that  amplifies  the information-processing  capabilities  of  hospitals  (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019).  Subsequently,  the  integration  of  AI  into  hospitals ' operations would serve to alleviate the intricacies and uncertainties they encounter. Equipped with sophisticated techniques and algorithms, AI systems have the capacity to efficiently handle the vast and intricate medical data of patients. This enables hospitals to derive timely and valuable insights that, in turn, enhance the accuracy of diagnoses and the effectiveness of treatments (Deist et al., 2020). Moreover, the OIPT suggests that hospitals  can  improve  patient  outcomes  by  employing  informationprocessing methods that mitigate ambiguity in clinical environments (Flynn & Flynn, 1999).

## 3.2. Determinants of AI adoption

## 3.2.1. Market share and AI adoption

As inferred from OIPT, hospitals are urged to invest in bolstering their  information-processing capacity to effectively manage the escalating volume and diversity of complexities and uncertainties. In the healthcare industry, sources of uncertainty include the dynamic external environment,  patient  demands,  interconnected  departments,  and  the variability in patient treatments. Hospitals with a larger market share encounter  a  more  extensive  patient  base  and  increased  demand, amplifying the unpredictability of medical activities. Concurrently, the proliferation  of  patient  data  and  the  imperative  to  process  this  data expand  exponentially.  Functioning  within  information-intensive,  dynamic,  and  volatile  settings,  OIPT  recommends  that  these  hospitals consistently gather, analyze, and interpret information adeptly to alleviate uncertainties. By embracing AI, a tool that enhances informationprocessing capability (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019), hospitals can navigate the intricacies of their operating environment, substantially improving their overall information-processing efficiency.

However, AI adoption calls for significant investment in technologies such  as  natural  language  processing,  pattern  recognition,  machine learning, deep learning, cognitive computing, and neural networks, to name a few.  To  successfully  adopt  AI  across  an  entire  organization, hospitals also need to spend a significant amount of time and money to educate and train employees. For these reasons, not all hospitals can afford to dedicate the necessary resources to AI adoption. Hospitals with a  higher  market  share  have  broad  physician  networks  and  inpatient bases as well as better access to capital and human resources (Cutler & Morton, 2013; Wani et al., 2018). Moreover, hospitals with a higher market share treat more patients than hospitals with a lower market share. Therefore, those hospitals receive a larger portion of prospective reimbursements from Medicare and a larger portion of insurance reimbursements from other third-party coverage (Lee et al., 2017). It is expected  that  a  larger  market  share  will  allow  hospitals  to  dedicate sufficient capital and human resources to AI adoption. In addition to better access to capital and human resources, hospitals with a higher market share can be better able to achieve economies of scale. Compared to hospitals with a lower market share, hospitals with a higher market share can spread costs over a larger number of patients and thus lower the fixed costs per patient.

To the extent that hospitals with a larger market share may have more considerable resources to absorb the costs of adopting AI technologies and achieve economies of scale, we expect a positive relationship between market share and AI adoption. Accordingly, we posit the following hypothesis:

H1a: Hospitals with a larger market share are likely to adopt AI.

## 3.2.2. Average length of stay and AI adoption

The  average  length  of  stay  is  one  of  the  hospital  characteristics affecting the possibility of AI adoption. Extended length of stay often arises  due  to  inadequate  coordination,  insufficient  communication P. Pham et al.

among  healthcare  providers,  and  the  unavailability  of  the  patient ' s medical history during the point of care (Bodenheimer, 2008). Consequently, an extended length of stay is linked to elevated occurrences of medical complications, heightened risk of readmissions, and escalated healthcare expenses. Addressing this challenge necessitates improved operational  coordination  that  spans  physical  resources,  services,  and patient flow, given the substantial interdependence among various departments within a hospital.

In line with the OIPT literature, it is noteworthy that the benefits derived from information-processing systems like AI are influenced by the extent of interdependence among sub-units within the organization and the magnitude of uncertainty within various situations (Galbraith, 1974; Wybo & Goodhue, 1995). When dealing with low interdependence, simple coordination modes such as standard operating procedures suffice. However, in scenarios marked by high interdependence, the requirement shifts to a common formalized language and comprehensive,  accurate  information  exchange  among  sub-units  (Thompson, 1967).

According  to  OIPT,  hospitals  are  encouraged  to  enhance  their information-processing capabilities by investing in advanced information systems. This notion leads to the understanding that hospitals with a higher average length of stay can adopt AI solutions, as AI adoption has demonstrated  its  ability  to  elevate  the  quality  of  healthcare  data, thereby enhancing the overall service-delivery quality. For instance, the incorporation of AI ' s text-to-speech and speech-to-text capabilities enables doctors to process documents with enhanced accuracy. Additionally,  the  utilization  of  AI  technologies  empowers hospitals to predict ambulatory hospital visits with precision (Hadavandi et al., 2012). This predictive capacity equips hospitals to anticipate the required human resources,  supplies,  and  interdepartmental  coordination  necessary  to provide effective patient care.

Furthermore, hospitals confronted with a high average length of stay have  the  option  to  implement  telemedicine  solutions,  allowing  for earlier patient discharge and enhancing patient management, particularly in remote areas (Armaignac et al., 2018). The integration of artificial  intelligence  into  remote  doctor-patient  interactions  serves  to modernize communication, deepen patient relationships, and facilitate real-time decision-making. For instance, Medtronic ' s personal diabetes assistant, Suga.IQ, serves as a notable example. It assists patients with diabetes in effectively managing their conditions by alerting them when their blood sugar levels reach dangerous levels, prompting immediate corrective  actions  (Medtronic,  2018).  The  Deloitte  ASSIT  technology demonstrates another instance of communication enhancement in patient care. Leveraging natural language processing and speech recognition, this innovation interprets patients ' requests, prioritizes them, and forwards  them  to  the  appropriate  personnel.  Impressively,  90  %  of surveyed  patients  affirm  the  technology ' s  positive  impact  on  staff responsiveness (Deloitte, 2020).

In scenarios where hospitals are grappling with extended decisionmaking durations, the significance of a dependable solution like AI becomes more pronounced. Natural language-processing techniques and deep learning come into play to extract information from unstructured data sources, including images, clinical notes, medical journals, operative notes, discharge summaries, and diagnostic testing reports. These methods  convert  this  information  into  coherent  and  structured  data (Davenport & Dreyer,  2018).  In  contrast  to  the  unstructured  data employed  in  non-AI  hospitals,  the  structured  data  employed  by  AIdriven hospitals proves to be highly advantageous for functions such as storage, searchability, and analytics. This, in turn, significantly enhances  the  efficiency  of  tasks  and  ultimately  supports  the  decisionmaking process of physicians. Therefore, we expect hospitals facing a high average length of stay will be inclined to implement AI technologies. This inclination arises from the observed positive impact of AI on healthcare delivery quality and the decision-making process. Based on this understanding, we put forth the following hypothesis:

H1b: Hospitals with a higher average length of stay are likely to adopt AI.

## 3.3. Performance outcomes of AI adoption

According  to  OIPT,  hospitals  with  strong  information-processing capabilities tend to operate with greater efficiency and attain elevated levels  of  operational  performance.  The  capacity  for  information  processing not only enriches the quality of patient care but also optimizes the management of hospitals ' operational aspects through knowledge application  and  the  utilization  of  suitable  technology  (Srivastava & Singh, 2021). Through the adoption of AI, hospitals can significantly augment their information-processing capability (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019). This advancement holds the potential to significantly enhance their overall capability for processing and utilizing information.

AI-assisted systems possess the capability to transform continually expanding patient medical records into comprehensible, manageable, and  actionable  information.  This  transformation  serves  to  mitigate healthcare uncertainty, alleviate information ambiguity, and streamline information  dissemination  across  various  caregiver  departments  and healthcare partners. Additionally, the heightened quality of information enhances hospitals ' capacity for demand forecasting, ensuring timely, top-notch  care.  These  systems,  furthermore,  play  a  pivotal  role  in fostering  integration  within  hospitals,  connecting  hospital-patient  interactions,  facilitating  inter-functional  collaboration,  and  establishing seamless  hospital-provider  connections  (Dobrzykowski & Tarafdar, 2015). These integrations collectively contribute to enhancing hospitals ' operational  efficiency  (Yu  et  al.,  2021).  Additionally,  according  to Tortorella, Fogliatto, Esp ˆ osto, et al. (2022), AI functions as a processingactuation technology that translates received information into actionable  decisions.  Their  findings  underscore  that  those  hospitals  extensively  adopting  such  processing-actuation  technologies  exhibit  better operational performance (Tortorella, Fogliatto, Esp ˆ osto, et al., 2022).

AI technologies also help healthcare organizations adapt to shifts in the  environment  and  swiftly  rebound  from  disruptions.  From  the perspective  of  OIPT,  the  availability  of  valuable  insights  regarding changes in markets, patient demands, and requirements is of paramount importance  for  healthcare  organizations.  This  information  is  instrumental in the development of novel products or services, including new drugs and treatment protocols. Organizations that offer diverse services and have multiple revenue streams are better equipped to manage both internal  and  external  disruptions,  thereby  maintaining  their  desired performance  levels.  AI ' s  transformative  influence  on  healthcare  is evident in the introduction of innovative services like healthcare chatbots,  personalized treatments, and post-discharge remote care. These novel service offerings alleviate the strain on constrained resources such as  beds,  medical  professionals,  equipment,  and  more,  all  while  upholding an elevated standard of service during times of disruption. The research by Tortorella et al. (2021) substantiates that AI stands as one of the technologies bolstering hospitals ' resilient capabilities. This includes the ability to monitor, forecast, respond to, and learn from disruptions in order to recover quickly from disruptions and sustain operational and financial outcomes.

As explored in Section 3.2, existing research highlights the transformative impact of AI technologies within hospitals. These technologies streamline patient flow, enhance disease detection, mitigate misinterpretation and diagnostic errors, and bolster the efficiency of medical supply chains and healthcare delivery systems (Kraus et al., 2021; Leone et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2020; Topol, 2019; Damoah et al., 2021; Medtronic, 2018). This, in turn, facilitates the optimization of resources, cost management, and effective healthcare treatment (Leone et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2018). Consequently, the adoption of AI is anticipated to yield improvements in both operational  performance  and  financial  performance for hospitals. Based on this premise, we propose the following hypothesis:

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Sample selection

To compile a preliminary list of hospitals that have implemented AI, we conduct a meticulous manual data collection process. Employing keywords such as ' artificial intelligence, '  ' machine learning, ' ' cognitive computing, '  ' deep learning, ' and ' natural language processing, ' combined  with  the  term ' adoption, ' we  perform  comprehensive searches  across  various  sources.  This  methodology  aligns  with  prior research practices (Choudhury & Asan, 2020; Shinners et al., 2020). Our search entails publicly accessible data repositories, including hospital websites, practitioner journals, academic journals, and annual financial reports. Additionally, we leverage the Factiva database, procured from Dow Jones & Company, Inc., to augment our data collection efforts.

Following the argument advanced by Zhang (2021), we designate a hospital  as  a  non-AI  adopter  if  our  comprehensive  search  across  the aforementioned sources yields no indication of AI adoption within the hospital ' s  context.  We  adopt  a  rigorous  selection  process,  retaining solely those hospitals that furnished ample information to ascertain their initial AI-adoption years. This procedure yields 246 AI hospitals with specific adoption years. We then create a matching sample of 246 hospitals that have not incorporated AI into their operations.

To ensure methodological rigor, our selection criteria include the stipulation that non-AI hospitals be situated within the same state as their AI counterparts. This is intended to minimize the potential influence of state-level regulations on healthcare operations, as highlighted by Radley et al. (2017). Furthermore, we require that the non-AI hospitals provide the same category of services as the AI hospitals. This step is taken to account for the systematic differences in objectives that often arise  among  hospitals  offering  disparate  services,  as  underscored  by Eldenburg et al. (2004). In scenarios where multiple non-AI hospitals satisfy our criteria, our preference is to select the one that most closely aligns with the bed count of the corresponding AI hospital, falling within the range of 90 -110 %. This approach aims to ensure a balanced and equitable comparison between the two sets of hospitals.

For our empirical analysis, we draw upon secondary data sourced from the Healthcare Cost Report Information System (HCRIS), a dataset that has been curated and refined by the RAND Corporation ' s Hospital Data repository. To ensure the consistency of our dataset, we exclude hospitals  that  are  not  within  the  scope  of  the  RAND  Corporation ' s Hospital Data repository. Our ultimate dataset comprises 1,882 observations, collectively reflecting a span of 941 AI-adopting hospital-years and an equivalent count of 941 non-AI-adopting hospital-years. These observations include hospitals situated across 40 distinct states; the data covers the time frame spanning from 2000 to 2020.

## 4.2. Variable measurements

## 4.2.1. Indicator of AI adoption

We create an indicator variable, AI , to denote hospitals that adopted AI in a particular year. The AI indicator has the value of 1 if the hospital adopted AI (i.e., AI hospital), and the value of 0 if the hospital did not adopt AI (i.e., non-AI hospital).

## 4.2.2. Determinants variables

As discussed in section 3.2, we identify two hospital-specific characteristics that are likely to be associated with the decision to adopt AI: market share ( MKTSHR ) and the average length of stay ( ALOS ). We use MKTSHR to measure a hospital ' s market share. It is calculated as the number of patient days for a hospital over the total number of patient days within the market (Lee et al., 2017; Zhang, 2021), which includes all hospitals within a 25 mi (40.23 km) radius (Zhang, 2021). We use ALOS to measure a hospital ' s average length of stay, and it is calculated as the total number of inpatient days divided by the total number of inpatients discharged (Bai, 2013).

## 4.2.3. Performance variables

Following the literature, we use three financial measures and two operational measures to assess hospital performance. The three financial performance  measures  are  outpatient  revenue  ( lnOPREV ),  inpatient revenue ( lnIPREV ), and return on assets ( ROA ) (Lee & Choi, 2016; Maiga & Jacobs,  2008;  Swink & Jacobs,  2012). lnOPREV and lnIPREV are constructed as the natural logarithms of outpatient revenues and inpatient revenues, respectively. ROA is constructed as net income divided by total assets at year-end.

We use two measures of operational performance that are widely used  in  the  literature:  productivity  ( PRODUC )  and  occupancy  ( OCC ) (Bai,  2013;  Chang,  2013;  Ding  et  al.,  2013;  Eldenburg & Krishnan, 2003). PRODUC is used to measure full-time employees ' output and is constructed by dividing the average daily census by the total number of full-time employees. The average daily census is calculated by dividing the total number of patients per year by 365. OCC is used to measure the efficiency in the use of licensed beds and is constructed by dividing the average daily census by the total number of licensed beds.

## 4.2.4. Control variables

Following the studies on healthcare management, we control for a list of factors that have been documented as affecting the decision to adopt AI and performance outcomes. In particular, we control for total expenses ( lnTotalExp ), complexity and severity of patient illness treated ( CMI ),  size  of  the  hospital  ( Size ),  the  proportion  of  Medicaid  and Medicare patients ( Medicare% and Medicaid% ), teaching status ( Teaching ), location ( Metro ), ownership ( FP and NP ), system affiliation ( System) , state regulation variations ( State ), and year fixed effects ( Year ) (Bai et al., 2010; Bai & Krishnan, 2015; Ding, 2014; Eldenburg & Krishnan, 2003, 2008; Shen et al., 2007; Zhang, 2021).

We use lnTotalExp to measure a hospital ' s total expenses; this figure is calculated as the natural logarithm of total expenses (Aletras, 1999). We use CMI to represent the service complexity (Grassetti et al., 2005). CMI captures  the  average  weight  for  the  different  diagnosis-related groups  in  a  hospital.  Hospitals  with  a  higher CMI provide  more complicated treatments and deal with more severe patients compared to hospitals  with  a  lower CMI . ## 5.4. Difference-in-Difference test

To further mitigate endogeneity concerns, we employ a differencein-difference  test  to  identify  the  causality  between  AI  adoption  and performance.

The difference-in-difference approach is based on a parallel trend assumption, which requires that the difference between the treatment and control group is constant over time if there is no treatment. We use the approach outlined in Anand et al. (2021) to examine whether hospital performance between AI and non-AI hospitals exhibits a parallel trend prior to AI adoption. For each sample year, we identify all hospitals that adopted AI in that particular year. We designate the initial year of AI adoption as year 0 and the subsequent (previous) year as year + 1 (-1). Then we gather data for the periods from year 3 to year + 3 and create a trend variable Pre-AI_Period to represent the periods before AI adoption. Specifically, Pre-AI_Period takes the value of 1 for the periods of year 3 to year 1 and 0 otherwise. The interaction term Pre-AI_Period × AI , is used to compare the trend in performance between AI and non-AI hospitals for the pre-AI period. We take our performance measures as the dependent variable and include all the control variables as in Equation (2). The estimation results show that the coefficients of the interaction term are all nonsignificant, which satisfies the parallel trend assumption. 5

To conduct the difference-in-difference test, we start with a univariate analysis. We first compute the change in performance measures for the treatment and control groups over three periods: from year 1 to year + 1, year 1 to year + 2, and year 1 to year + 3. Our treatment group includes all AI hospitals that we identified in each sample year, while the control group includes all non-AI hospitals. Then we compute the differences in performance changes between the two groups. The univariate results are reported in Panel A of Table 7. The t -test results illustrate  that  the  differences  in  all  performance  changes  except  the change in ROA are positive and significantly different from zero, indicating that hospital performance improves after AI adoption.

## 5.5. Additional tests

In addition to Wooldridge ' s instrumental variable approach, we also employ the traditional two-stage least square (2SLS) analysis to test the robustness of our results. The fitted probability from the estimation of Equation (1) is used to replace the endogenous indicator variable, AI , in Equation (2). The results in Table 8 show that the coefficients of FittedProbability using the 2SLS analysis are similar to those of AIFIT as reported in Panel B of Table 6, which provides further support to our core finding that AI adoption is positively related to hospital performance except for ROA .

It is possible that the effect of AI adoption on hospital performance might be delayed because hospitals need time to learn and make effective use of AI. To account for this issue, we investigate the effect of AI adoption  on  future  performance.  Specifically,  we  replace PERFORMANCEit in Equation (3) with PERFORMANCEit + 1 . The untabulated results show that four performance  measures (i.e., lnOPREVi + 1 , lnOPREVi + 1 , PRODUCi + 1 , and OCCi + 1 ) are still significantly positively related to AI adoption.

To mitigate the impact of outliers on regression results, we exclud observations with performance measures of more than three and less than negative three z-scores from the sample. Alternatively, we winsorize  all  continuous  variables  at  the  1st  and  99th  percentiles.  The regression results (untabulated) are similar to those reported in Panel B of Table 6, which confirms that our regression results are not driven by outliers.

## 6. Discussion and future research

## 6.1. Discussion and conclusions

Employing a dataset of 1,882 observations spanning the years 2000 to 2020 and guided by the theoretical framework of the OIPT, this study undertakes a comprehensive examination. It commences by scrutinizing the factors that influence hospitals ' decisions to adopt AI technology. Subsequently, the study delves into the performance outcomes that stem from the adoption of AI within these hospitals. To address the potential endogeneity  concern  regarding  hospitals ' decision  to  adopt  AI,  we investigate  the  impact  of  endogeneity  on  the  relation  between  AI adoption  and  hospital  performance  using  Wooldridge ' s  instrumental variable approach.

Our findings indicate a noteworthy trend: hospitals endowed with a substantial market share are notably more predisposed to embracing AI adoption, in contrast to their counterparts with lower market shares. Thus, our study enriches literature on the determinants of healthcare information technology adoption. Furthermore, our study substantiates that the integration of AI technologies correlates with higher financial and operational performance.

Our  results  point  to  the  compelling  suggestion  that  hospitals endowed with a larger market share should be strongly encouraged to adopt AI technology. These hospitals, given their size and complexity, operate within a dynamic environment, catering to a diverse array of patients and services. Hence, the benefits they stand to gain from AI technology  are  significant.  By  utilizing  AI,  they  can  streamline  data derived from diverse hardware and software systems, alleviate administrative burdens on physicians, optimize the balance between health resources and patient demand, curtail healthcare costs, and ultimately bolster healthcare efficiency.

A surprising initial outcome emerged when our H1b failed to gain support, indicating that hospitals with higher average lengths of stay are not  inherently  more  inclined  to  adopt  AI.  Our  interpretation  of  this outcome revolves around a multifaceted perspective. We postulate that both AI-adopting and non-AI-adopting hospitals share a common aspiration to reduce their average lengths of stay, albeit through divergent means. While non-AI hospitals may opt for other technologies such as lean thinking to achieve this reduction, the overarching objective remains consistent. This convergence in goals could potentially dilute the association  between  the  average  length  of  stay  and  AI  adoption,  accounting  for  the  absence  of  significant  findings.  Another  plausible explanation  revolves  around  the  prioritization  of  AI  technologies.  In many  instances,  the  central  impetus  for  AI  implementation  lies  in enhancing detection and diagnostic accuracy across both inpatient and outpatient care, rather than focusing exclusively on curbing the average length of stay for inpatients. As long as AI serves to augment physicians ' diagnostic accuracy, there exists a compelling rationale for encouraging the adoption of AI across both AI and non-AI hospitals.

The proliferation of advanced AI technologies further accentuates this point. By harnessing AI capabilities, hospitals can optimize operational efficiency and bolster profit margins. This is achieved by utilizing existing  hardware,  software,  and  healthcare  workforce  capacities  to accommodate  more  patients,  intensifying system utilization,  and reducing patient turnover. This dynamic shift emphasizes the potential for both AI and non-AI hospitals to gain from AI adoption, albeit through different avenues.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

As in any research, this study is not without limitations. First, this study exclusively employs an indicator variable to measure the status of AI adoption. This simplified approach may overlook variations in AI techniques utilized and the extent of AI adoption. The diversity could entail differences between adopting AI across an entire hospital versus specific departments, or the frequency of AI usage (e.g., daily adoption vs. as-needed). To comprehensively capture these nuances, future investigations  might  supplement  secondary  data  with  survey-based  or field-based  data,  thereby  enabling  a  more  accurate  assessment  of  AI types and degrees of adoption. Second, due to data limitations, we have been  unable  to  include  several  significant  control  variables  in  our analysis. These variables, including factors such as the number of doctors, doctor capabilities, the count of IT artifacts or systems used in the hospitals, and the extent of specialties or services offered by the hospital, are  essential  for  a  comprehensive  examination.  We  recognize  the importance of these variables and recommend that future research endeavors consider their incorporation to enrich the analytical framework. Third, while we addressed endogeneity concerns through Wooldridge ' s instrumental  variable  approach,  the  possibility  of  self-selection  bias cannot be entirely disregarded (Maiga, 2014). This intriguing aspect warrants  further  exploration,  thus  signifying  an  area  ripe  for  future research. Fourth, our study omits an examination of the performance P. Pham et al.
