## 1. Introduction

The  escalating  momentum  of  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  powered Customer Relationship Management (CRM) has surged in recent years, establishing it as a pivotal cornerstone within the contemporary era of the  Fourth  Industrial  Revolution  (Rahman  et  al.,  2023).  AI-powered CRM is  recognised  as  a  catalyst  for  transformative  advancements  in CRM practices and big data analytical intelligence (Rahman et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020), characterised by its adaptability to dynamic market changes (Chaudhuri et al., 2022). Empirical studies further demonstrate its  potential  in  enhancing  organisations ' capacity to fortify  customer relationships  (Chatterjee  et  al.,  2022),  manage  complex  interactions, and drive service innovation performance (Kumar et al., 2023).

The global market for AI-powered CRM reflects this enthusiasm. In the United States alone, industry revenue reached an estimated USD 41.0 billion in 2024, with projections suggesting expansion to USD 80

The rapid adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI) -powered Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems has exposed a critical gap: despite substantial investment, many organisations fail to derive meaningful business value from these technologies. Recent surveys show that while AI is a strategic priority for executives, only a fraction report significant returns, with adoption challenges particularly acute in customer-facing functions. This study  addresses  this  gap  by  conceptualising  and  empirically  examining  AI-powered  CRM  as  a  higher-order organisational capability. Drawing on the microfoundations of dynamic capability theory, we adopt a threestage research design. First, a systematic scoping review and in-depth interviews with industry experts identify  the  core  dimensions  and  subdimensions  of  AI-powered  CRM  capability.  Second,  we  operationalise  and validate these dimensions within a nomological network. Third, a survey of 205 banking employees in Australia tests the influence of AI-powered CRM capability on marketing ambidexterity and, in turn, on organisational outcomes.  The  quantitative  analysis  confirms  that  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities  positively  shape  marketing ambidexterity, which subsequently enhances profitability and competitive advantage. Theoretically, the findings advance CRM research by introducing a microfoundational capability model that integrates data management, multi-channel integration, and service offerings. Practically, the study provides actionable guidance for managers seeking to close the ' value realisation gap ' by cultivating AI-powered CRM systems as dynamic capabilities that balance exploration and exploitation in volatile markets.

Salesforce, for example, captured more than 21.7 % of the global CRM market share in 2023, outstripping rivals and underscoring its leadership during this era of AI innovation (Schmaier, 2024).

Although AI-powered CRM shares some foundational elements with other AI-powered information systems, such as data management and analytics, its role is fundamentally distinct. Unlike AI applications in operations,  finance,  or  supply  chain  management,  AI-powered  CRM deals directly with consumer data and frontline customer interactions, where errors or biases can have immediate reputational and financial consequences.  For  example,  Salesforce ' s  CRM  platform  leverages  AIdriven  personalisation,  next-best-action  recommendations,  and  automated cross-selling, all of which directly shape customer perceptions, trust, and loyalty. This customer-centric orientation makes AI-powered CRM uniquely consequential compared to other enterprise systems.

Moreover, CRM platforms combine transactional, behavioural, and contextual data at scale to deliver personalised marketing offerings (e.g., cross-selling, dynamic pricing, recommendations). These processes are not  merely  back-end  efficiencies  but  are  embedded  in  the  value  cocreation  process  between  firms  and  customers.  As  such,  AI-powered CRM  must  grapple  with  challenges  that  are  qualitatively  different from those facing other AI-enabled systems: ensuring transparency in algorithmic  decision-making,  mitigating  customer  resistance  to  automated engagement, addressing ethical concerns around data privacy, and balancing personalisation with fairness (Gaczek et al., 2023; Papagiannidis et al., 2023). This distinctiveness underscores why examining AI-powered CRM through the lens of dynamic capabilities (DC) theory is important. While other AI-powered systems may rely on universal capabilities such as data governance or process automation, CRM uniquely requires the integration of these with customer-facing service offerings and multi-channel engagement to generate marketing ambidexterity. In other words, AI-powered CRM is not just ' another ' AI application; it is a higher-order capability that directly mediates between organisational resources and consumer value creation, making its study both timely and necessary.

Despite considerable enthusiasm for AI-powered CRM, many organisations fail to translate investment into meaningful outcomes (Chatterjee et al., 2022). A recent Boston Consulting Group survey reveals that only 25 % of executives report significant business value from AI initiatives, despite 75 % identifying AI as a strategic priority for 2025 (Apotheker et al., 2025). Similarly, McKinsey & Company notes that while  nearly  all  firms  invest  in  AI,  only  1 %  have  achieved  full  AI maturity capable of generating substantial impact (Mayer et al., 2025). This gap is exacerbated by employee scepticism in critical customer-facing functions such as sales and marketing, despite AI ' s high potential in these areas (Mayer et al., 2025). Such findings underline significant  organisational  challenges  in  building  AI-powered  CRM capabilities.

The strategic integration of AI-powered CRM capabilities offers advantages for the financial ecosystem, allowing firms to effectively tackle contemporary challenges through enhanced customer interaction and refined decision-making processes. Financial service providers prioritise leveraging AI-driven customer service systems to achieve cost savings and improve service experiences for their clientele (Xu et al., 2020). The utilisation of AI in the banking sector aims to reconfigure the industry in alignment  with  the  imperatives  of  the  Fourth  Industrial  Revolution, thereby fostering enhanced levels of innovation, agility and resilience (Arya & Tiwary, 2024).

While service organisations are increasingly investing in AI-powered CRM technology,  the  banking  industry  has  observed  a  notable  16 % increase in the average cost of disputes (Agrios, 2024). About 31 % of customer-facing roles in Australia lack proficiency in effectively utilising AI systems, and 34 % struggle to derive value from AI-powered CRM systems. Furthermore, customer preferences highlight the importance of digital  experience  and  customer  service  in  the  financial  sector,  with 53 % of customers expressing willingness to switch providers for a better digital experience and 41 % for improved customer service (Salesforce, 2024). Concerns about financial security have also increased, with 43 % seeking  enhanced  digital  experiences,  while  42 %  express  concerns about  a  decline  in  financial  security  compared  to  the  previous  year (Salesforce,  2024).  Negative  attitudes  toward  AI,  stemming  from perceived  opacity  and  a  lack  of  transparency,  result  in  individuals ' reluctance to share information, thereby limiting the effectiveness of AI-powered CRM systems in supporting decision-making (Gaczek et al., 2023). Additionally, the dark side of AI-integrated processes extends beyond  ethical  decision-making  and  privacy  issues,  contributing  to relationship gaps with adverse consequences for businesses (Papagiannidis et al., 2023). This underlines a critical need to comprehend how AI-powered CRM capabilities can be harnessed within rapidly evolving market dynamics to mitigate such risks and enhance performance.

The extant research underscores the importance of examining AIpowered CRM systems, particularly given their direct role in shaping customer  experiences  and  revenue  growth  (Rahman  et  al.,  2023). However, prior studies often treat CRM generically, focusing on it as one of many enterprise technologies rather than as a uniquely customer-facing and data-intensive platform. As a result, the distinctive microfoundations  through  which  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities  are cultivated and how these capabilities interact with organisational outcomes remain underexplored. For instance, while research has linked data analytics and CRM usage to improved firm performance (Moradi & Dass,  2022;  Rahman  et  al.,  2023),  these  studies  do  not  unpack  how customer-facing service offerings and multi-channel engagement combine with back-end data governance to form a higher-order capability.  This  gap  is  particularly  critical  in  dynamic  markets  where AI-powered CRM adoption is expanding but its value remains uneven, reinforcing  the  need  for  a  framework  that  explains  how  AI-powered CRM can be developed and leveraged as a dynamic capability to achieve ambidexterity and long-term performance gains.

While previous studies have primarily focused on CRM as a general system, gaps remain in understanding its AI-powered capabilities and impact. For instance, prior studies have examined CRM in the context of technology turbulence (Chatterjee et al., 2022), industry dynamism and information and communication technology (ICT) capability (Chatterjee et  al.,  2022),  strategic  intent  (Chaudhuri  et  al.,  2022)  and  customer service flexibility. However, there is a paucity of research on AI-powered CRM capability and its role in establishing marketing ambidexterity as an organisational capability (Hughes et al., 2010) to achieve organisational success (Voss & Voss, 2013). Especially in adapting to rapidly changing environments and fostering innovation (Akter et al., 2023), the role of marketing ambidexterity between AI-powered CRM capabilities and  key  organisational  outcomes  (e.g.,  profitability  and  competitive advantage)  remains  notably  underexplored  (Chatterjee  et  al.,  2022). This study addresses these gaps by applying the microfoundations of the dynamic capabilities framework (Bojesson & Fundin, 2021; Chen et al., 2023;  Teece,  2007)  to  investigate  how  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities develop  and  influence  marketing  ambidexterity,  ultimately  driving profitability and competitive advantage in volatile market environments.

Accordingly, this study seeks to examine:

RQ1 . (Exploratory -Ontological): What are the core dimensions and subdimensions that constitute AI-powered CRM capability?

RQ2 . (Measurement -Operationalisation): How  can  these  dimensions and subdimensions be reliably operationalised and validated within a nomological network?

RQ3 . (Impact -Model Testing): To what extent do AI-powered CRM capabilities influence marketing ambidexterity, and how does this, in turn, shape organisational outcomes such as profitability and competitive advantage?

These research questions are designed as sequential stages of inquiry. RQ1 is exploratory, identifying dimensions and subdimensions through a systematic review and in-depth interviews. RQ2 builds on this foundation  by  operationalising  and  validating  measurement  scales.  RQ3 models the effects of the higher-order capability on key performance outcomes.  By  integrating  exploratory,  measurement,  and  modelling perspectives, this study makes two contributions. Theoretically, it advances  CRM  research  by  introducing  a  microfoundational  capability model of AI-powered CRM, moving beyond fragmented technological accounts.  Practically,  it  provides  banking  managers  with  actionable guidance for harnessing AI- powered CRM systems more effectively to balance  exploration  and  exploitation,  thereby  addressing  the  urgent need to bridge the ' value realisation gap ' that has so far plagued AI adoption in CRM.

The structure of this paper is outlined as follows: Section 2 provides a review of the literature and theoretical underpinnings, with a focus on AI-powered CRM capabilities. Section 3 presents the findings derived from qualitative research, which involved in-depth interviews designed to explore the dimensions of AI-powered CRM capabilities. Section 4 elaborates on the conceptual model and hypotheses derived from the consolidation of insights gathered from the scoping review and in-depth interviews. Methodological considerations are explained in Section 5, followed by a detailed examination of data analysis and a summary of findings in Section 6. Finally, Section 7 highlights the theoretical and managerial contributions of this research and outlines potential avenues for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Significance of AI-powered CRM capabilities

AI-powered CRM refers to the integration of AI algorithms within CRM systems, which enables businesses to identify new revenue opportunities  (Monod  et  al.,  2023).  AI  facilitates  the  automation  of processes to enhance business performance (Baabdullah et al., 2021). By analysing  extensive  customer  data,  AI  aids  organisations  in  their decision-making processes (Chatterjee et al., 2021), leading to a more profound understanding of customer preferences and fostering personalised interactions (Li & Xu, 2022). It not only functions as a system for collecting information but also assists in acquiring, retaining, and analysing customer data. This comprehensive approach enhances customer service satisfaction, fosters long-term customer relationships, and ultimately  improves  organisational  profitability  (Suoniemi  et  al.,  2021). Alnofeli et al. (2023) reveal that AI-powered CRM capabilities constitute a  multidimensional  concept  that  integrates  data  management  across multiple channels to offer innovative service solutions. This approach optimises interactions, adds value to businesses, and provides competitive intelligence that enhances customer satisfaction and business performance (Libai et al., 2020).

## 2.2. Microfoundations of dynamic capabilities (DC)

Numerous theoretical frameworks have been employed to investigate  the  adoption  of  AI-powered  CRM  systems  in  business  contexts including:  Resource-based  view  (RBV)  theory  (e.g.  Chatterjee  et  al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2023; Ledro 2021; Li and Xu 2022; Libai et al. 2020; Suoniemi et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020), Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) Theory (e.g. Baabdullah et  al.  2021),  Technology  Acceptance  Model  (TAM2)  (e.g.  Chatterjee et al. 2021; Gupta et al. 2024), Status quo bias theory (e.g. Chatterjee et al., 2022; Demlehner and Laumer 2024), Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (e.g. Chatterjee et al. 2021; Gupta et al. 2024), Technology threat avoidance theory (TTAT) (e.g. Gaczek et  al.  2023),  Theory  of  Task-Technology  Fit  (TTF)  (e.g.  Gupta  et  al. 2024),  theory  of  productivity  paradox  (e.g.  Kumar  et  al.  2023)  and Dynamic capability (DC) (e.g. Chaudhuri et al., 2022; Kumar et al. 2023; Rahman et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020). Although contributions to this field of research are valued, it has been acknowledged that much of the existing literature presents findings that are fragmented. Consequently, there remains a need to further explore the microfoundations of DC.

The  resource-based  view  (RBV)  theory  constitutes  a  fundamental framework, suggesting that a company ' s long-term competitive advantage comes from its ability to effectively control and utilise resources and capabilities that are valuable, scarce, hard to replicate and cannot be easily replaced by competitors (Barney, 1991; Wang & Feng, 2012). In contrast to RBV, which predominantly emphasises the strategic significance  of  tangible  resources  (Srivastava  et  al.,  2001),  the  DC perspective suggests that broad possession of diverse resources does not always result in superior performance over time (Shamim et al., 2021). Instead, it asserts that sustained competitive advantage arises from the capability  to  adeptly  acquire  and  deploy  a  business ' s  resources  in alignment with the dynamic environmental conditions (Hossain et al., 2020). Teece et al. (1997) introduced the concept of DC, clearly distinguishing it from the RBV, while noting that the DC extends the RBV by emphasising different aspects. DC should be distinguished from operational  capabilities,  which  pertain  to  an  organisation ' s  current  operations, as DC is defined as an organisation ' s ability to integrate, build and reconfigure  internal  and  external  competencies  to  address  rapidly changing environments (Teece et al., 1997), thereby aiming to modify the enterprise ' s resource base (Akter et al., 2021).

Microfoundations of DC refer to the basic, detailed actions or processes at a lower level within the organisation that collectively explain and support broader, higher-level strategies and analyses (Bojesson & Fundin,  2021).  In  the  context  of  DC  theory,  understanding  these micro-level actions is essential for maintaining and enhancing the organisation ' s performance. Existing research explores the microfoundations of DC from diverse perspectives. For instance, Akter et al. (2023) investigate six microfoundations (i.e., analytics climate, cross-functional  integration,  data,  technology,  pattern  spotting,  and ambidexterity) within the emerging data-driven innovation paradigm, specifically discerning complementary and co-specialisation attributes. Teece (2007) suggests that the DC framework aims to uncover the origins of enduring competitive advantage and provide managerial guidance for sustaining profitability through the development and implementation of intricate microfoundations, including strategies to mitigate zero-profit scenarios in highly competitive markets.

To  empirically  investigate  the  research  questions  concerning  AIpowered CRM capabilities, this study adopts the microfoundations of DC theory, which emerged from the DC framework as an extension of RBV (Teece et al., 1997). While RBV emphasises the value of static resources (Barney, 1991; Kristandl & Bontis, 2007; Wernerfelt, 1984), the DC framework focuses on how firms continuously build, integrate and reconfigure  resources  or  microfoundations  to  remain  competitive  in dynamic environments (Teece, 2018; Teece et al., 1997).

The relevance of this approach is supported by Mikalef et al. (2021), who demonstrate how AI facilitates DC in B2B marketing by improving various  microfoundational  processes  such  as  AI-driven  data  analysis, natural language processing, intelligent agents, decision automation and predictive analytics. These microfoundation  processes correspond closely with the sensing, seizing, and transforming activities that characterise  the  broader  DC  framework  (Teece,  2007).  Building  on  this foundation, our study extends this logic to the context of AI-powered CRM,  arguing  that  these  technologies  serve  as  higher-order  capabilities underpinned by microfoundations that are essential for navigating dynamic market conditions. Recognising these micro-level activities is critical  for  maintaining  and  enhancing  organisational  performance (Chen et al., 2023).

Although DC theory has been extensively applied in many studies, as shown in Table 1, these investigations primarily focus on strategic-level routinisation within organisations, neglecting the operational level. This has left  a  gap  in  understanding how routinised and ad-hoc activities interact and influence organisational outcomes. To address this gap and provide new theoretical and practical insights, this study argues that adopting a microfoundations perspective is essential.

## 3. Research design

To address the RQs, we conducted three studies. First, we conducted study 1 (a scoping review) to identify the primary dimensions of AIpowered  CRM.  Second,  we  conducted  Study  2  (in-depth  interviews and  thematic  analysis,  (n = 24)  to  confirm  the  dimensions  and  subdimensions of AI-powered CRM with industry experts. Finally, we conducted an empirical study (n = 205) to test the hypotheses and validate the research model.

## 3.1. Research setting

The study focuses on the major banking institutions in Australia, commonly referred to as the "big four"  banks, namely  the  Commonwealth  Bank  of  Australia  (CBA),  Westpac,  National  Australia  Bank (NAB), and Australia and New Zealand Banking Group (ANZ). These establishments collectively hold a substantial market share, accounting for approximately 75 % of the Australian banking sector. This dominance highlights their significant influence and competitive position  in  integrating  AI-driven  technologies  (Mehra & Leggatt,  2024). Renowned for their prominence within Australia ' s financial landscape, the combined assets of the big four banks exceeded $3.85 trillion by the conclusion of 2023, surpassing twice the nation ' s economic output. The chosen  context  provides  an  ideal  setting  for  exploring  the  research questions driven by several pivotal factors.

## 3.2. Scoping review - Qualitative (Study-1)

A  scoping  review  was  employed  to  develop  synergistic  solutions using qualitative techniques. This method serves as an effective tool for several purposes, including mapping existing literature and identifying common subdimensions within primary dimensions (Arksey & O ' Malley, 2005; Fowler & Thomas, 2023; Munn et al., 2018). Using four key databases (i.e., Scopus, Web of Science, Business Source Complete and ProQuest), we used the following search strings ' artificial intelligence ' AND ' customer relationship management ' , ' AI ' AND ' CRM ' , "technological innovation and CRM", "automation and CRM", "CRM analytics" etc. till December 2023. The search focused on peer-reviewed journals with special attention to Australian Business Deans Council ' s (ABDC) C to A* ranked outlets, level 2 and above in the Association of Business Schools  (ABS)  and  Q3  and  above  in  the  Scimago  Journal  Ranking. Studies  focusing  on  only  CRM  were  excluded,  and  studies  with  the application of AI in CRM were included. We also carefully assessed the citation data of each publication, including research questions, theories, methods, and findings of the selected studies, to evaluate their relevance to our study. Finally, we conducted a thematic analysis of 64 articles on AI-powered  CRM,  which  resulted  in  three  major  dimensions:  governance capability, multi-channel integration capability, and service offerings capability as primary dimensions (refer to Exhibit 1).

## 3.3. In-depth interviews (Study-2)

To address the research question regarding the dimensions of AIpowered  CRM,  the  study  conducted  24  in-depth  interviews  between January and March 2023, utilising both written responses and Zoom recordings.  Participants  were  selected  through  purposive  sampling (Agyei et al., 2022), targeting individuals with a minimum of two years of experience in utilising AI-powered CRM systems. Each interview was allocated  45 -60 min and subsequently transcribed for thematic categorisation. To ensure diversity in sample characteristics, a descriptive analysis was conducted, encompassing variables such as age, gender, industry and management level. Consequently, the demographic profile of  the  respondents  represents  a  variety  of  groups,  as  illustrated  in Table 2.

The  study  subsequently  employed  manual  thematic  analysis  to discern patterns and meanings within the interview dataset, adhering to the  guidelines  established  by  Braun  and  Clarke  (2022).  The  data collection process was discontinued once the researchers were able to anticipate the informants ' responses, indicating that data saturation had been reached. At this stage, minimal new insights were emerging from the  interviews  and  additional  data  collection  was  unlikely  to  yield further valuable information. Data saturation, a key concept in qualitative research, denotes the point at which continued data collection no longer  yields  significant  contributions  to  the  study  (Saunders  et  al., 2018). To ensure the validity and trustworthiness of the findings, the researchers adhered to the guidelines proposed by Denzin and Lincoln (2008), which recommend strategies such as triangulation, peer review, and participant validation to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the research process and outcomes.

## 4. Study 1 & 2 findings and hypotheses development

Based on the qualitative findings from the scoping review, in-depth interviews  and  thematic  analysis,  this  study  has  identified  three  primary dimensions and eight subdimensions of AI-powered CRM capability  (see  Fig.  1).  The  inaugural  dimension  is data  management capability ,  which encompasses three subdimensions: data governance, data analytics and data privacy. The second dimension is multi-channel integration  capability ,  which  comprises  two  subdimensions:  content consistency  and  process  consistency.  Finally,  the  third  dimension  is service  offerings  capability ,  which  encapsulates  three  subdimensions: personalisation, automation and meaningfulness. The conceptual model of AI-powered CRM capabilities in this study is constructed upon the foundational theories of DC and microfoundations. The model posits that  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities,  encompassing  data  management, multi-channel integration, and service offerings, significantly influence organisational outcomes such as sustained profitability and competitive advantage. These effects are hypothesised to be mediated by marketing ambidexterity, which reflects the organisation ' s ability to balance the exploitation  of  existing  capabilities  with  the  exploration  of  new opportunities.

According to Shamim et al. In essence, managing the veracity and value of data through organisational processes and strategic direction is a crucial element in forming organisational dynamic capabilities, focusing on effective data management (Shams & Solima, 2019). It also involves strategic capabilities to enable data monetisation through enhanced business operations  and  decision-making  processes  (Legner  et  al.,  2020).  Data management capability consists of three sub-dimensions: data governance, data intelligence, and data privacy.

Data governance (DG) involves determining the individuals or entities responsible for decision-making regarding an organisation ' s data assets and ensuring effective management and use of data (Khatri & Brown, 2010). It encompasses the strategic implementation and management of policies,  processes,  and  technologies  to  protect  valuable  data  assets throughout the entire organisation (Gregory, 2011). This enables accurate decision-making,  enhancing  overall  firm  competitiveness  and  performance (Rana et al., 2022).

Data  privacy  (DP) involves  regulating  organisations ' handling  of consumer personal data for marketing purposes, including control over access, use, dissemination, and protection (Martin & Murphy, 2017). ' Data privacy policies focusing on data breach is one of the critical building blocks of AI-based CRM. ' (Participant #3, Male, 30)

## 4.2. Multi-channel integration capability

The multi-channel integration capability plays a critical role within AI-powered CRM systems, translating the outputs of business strategy and  value creation processes into value-added  interactions with customers (Abadie et al., 2023; Payne & Frow, 2004). It involves strategically managing diverse communication channels within the organisation to deliver a cohesive, seamless experience for customers across various  touchpoints  (Awasthi & Sangle,  2012;  Frasquet & Miquel, 2017). Multi-channel integration capability encompasses two reflective sub-dimensions: content consistency and process consistency.

Content consistency (CC) refers to the uniformity between the information exchanged with customers across multiple channels and the information  they  receive  (Sousa & Voss,  2006).  Content  consistency through  different  channels  of  interaction  ensures  an  integrated  and reliable service experience (Shen et al., 2018). Consistent content across channels is essential for a seamless experience, minimising friction and transaction  times.  Inconsistency,  on  the  other  hand,  may  lead  to customer  frustration  during  channel  transitions  (Lee  et  al.,  2019).  It ensures that customers receive uniform information and communications  irrespective  of  the  channels  they  select  (Mukhopadhyay  et  al., 2023).

' The AI does a lot, the customer can interact with us through multiple  touch  points  to  multiple  personas  or  multiple  accounts.  AIbased  CRM  will  help  us  unify  customer  accounts,  allowing  us  to see a single view of each customer and then reach out to them in a holistic, integrated manner. ' (Participant #6, Male 35)

Process consistency  (PC) refers  to  the  assurance  of  procedural  and operational consistency within customer-facing operations across diverse communication channels. Examples include the speed of service delivery, imagery, and the overall feel of the channel (Sousa & Voss, 2006). Ozok et al. (2007) contended that consistent processes within automated  CRM  systems  enhance  organisational  capabilities,  potentially leading to increased customer profitability. It involves ensuring that the process and activities carried out in the front-office operations remain unified across various communications (Hossain et al., 2019). Lee et al. (2019) suggest that ensuring a uniform visual appearance and seamless experience across channels is essential for driving meaningful customer engagement, where customers actively interact with the firms ' offerings and activities.

' Regardless of how they have interacted or who they are, they will be visiting our business, whether it be for any account. But for us, we should be able first to create a single view of the customer, and then create  an  integrated  plan  across  channels  for  the  customer  who reached out and across different touchpoints for our customers to use in an automated and intelligent way. ' (Participant #6, Male 35)

The  two  sub-dimensions  within  multi-channel  integration  play  a pivotal role  in orchestrating integrated interactions that enhance the customer experience by ensuring consistent responses across channels and maintaining similarity and consistency in relevant process attributes (Mukhopadhyay et al., 2023; Sousa & Voss, 2006). By integrating these capabilities, organisations can create a seamless multi-channel experience that improves customer engagement and operational efficiency. Therefore, we propose:

H2 . :  Multi-channel  integration  capability  positively  influences  AIpowered CRM capabilities.

## 4.3. Service offerings capability

Providing "good service" remains a primary focus for all service organisations,  with  "good  service"  often  equating  to  more  personalised service. Furthermore, the adaptation of service offerings significantly enhances  employees ' work  performance  and  positively  influences customer evaluations (Surprenant & Solomon, 1987). Service offerings, therefore, are actions taken to meet societal needs and accommodate diverse customer segments, each of which perceives unique experiential levels within the service. This capability has three distinct sub-dimensions encompassing personalisation, automation and meaningfulness,  each  contributing  to  the  overall  effectiveness  and satisfaction of the service provided.

Personalisation (SP) refers to the customisation of services to appear tailored to individual requirements (McCoy & Hargie, 2007), achieved through advanced AI technology that observes customer behaviour and adapts to their preferences and needs over time, thereby avoiding mass production (Chung et al., 2016). Personalisation is critical for gaining a competitive advantage in today ' s business environment, as it involves using personal-level data to tailor customer interactions and enhance marketing effectiveness across all marketing initiatives (Cavdar Aksoy et al., 2021). AI tools are viewed as advantageous for every aspect of the CRM process, as they facilitate personalised goods and services to consumers, thereby enhancing  their experience and  contributing to increased profitability, leading to improved customer satisfaction and financial gains for businesses (Libai et al., 2020). The following statement supports this:

' I believe that AI can be used to better structure and organise our teams to be more engaged with our customers. That provides us with opportunities to target our customer base better and build engagement and better service for our members. ' (Participant #9, Male 40)

Automation (SA) is defined as the utilisation of advanced technology to enhance efficiency and effectiveness through automated, personalised and data analytics-driven activities (Mero et al., 2020). Huang and Rust (2018) characterise automation as mechanical intelligence due to its capacity to autonomously execute routine repetitive tasks, resembling human behaviour while maintaining consistency. Implementing intelligent  automation in  a  CRM  system enables employees to effectively manage their relationships, streamline decision-making processes, and contribute to enhanced employee satisfaction (Chatterjee et al., 2022), as reflected by the following comment:

' We ' re talking about automation; it requires an advanced degree of analytics,  and  then  you ' ll  be  able  to  provide  the  service  to  the customer through different channels because there are many channels that can be used. ' (Participant #13, Male, 52)

Meaningfulness  (SM) encompasses the perceived relevance, appropriateness and usefulness of a new service or product for target customers, reflecting its comparative relevance to competitors (Akter et al., 2023; Siu et al., 2013). The operationalisation of meaningfulness involves assessing the degree to which the new service aligns with customers ' needs (Siu et al.,  2013).  Kumar  et  al.  (2023) argue that the process  of  creating  a  new  service  model  includes  enhancing  existing services by leveraging novel resources, a concept commonly referred to as innovative service offerings. It is reflected by the following comment:

' The system serves a purpose, because you need to put what ' s relevant for the customer and relevant for the company. ' (Participant #11, female, 42)

Therefore, we hypothesise:

H3 . :  Service  offerings  capability  positively  influences  AI-powered CRM capabilities.

## 4.4. Sustained profitability

According to Littleton (1928), profit serves as a measure of accomplishment, an indicator of operating results and a crucial element of indispensable managerial information. While economists and accountants typically define profit as the surplus remaining after covering all costs, including interest and wages, business practitioners view it as a vital metric for evaluating business performance and managerial effectiveness.  This  dual  perspective  highlights  the  multifaceted  nature  of profit, underscoring its importance not only in financial accounting but also in strategic business management. It emphasises that profit is not merely a financial outcome but a comprehensive indicator of organisational health and strategic success.

Profitability (PF) is intrinsically linked to the achievement of financial goals, including returns on sales, business unit performance, and both current and expected returns on investment, as well as profit margins (Akter et al., 2023). Financial performance can also be assessed through managers ' evaluations of their organisation ' s overall financial success relative to competitors (Gabler et al., 2015). These metrics are essential for  understanding  the  effectiveness  of  business  strategies  and  operational efficiencies. Given the paramount importance of profitability as a key metric for evaluating organisational performance, the integration of AI-powered CRM dimensions emerges as a critical capability. Scholars within similar research paradigms have acknowledged that enhancing such systems contributes to superior business value and overall firm performance (Chatterjee, 2022; Li et al., 2023). Thus, it is hypothesised:

H4 . : AI-powered CRM  capabilities positively impact sustained profitability.

## 4.5. Sustained competitive advantage

Sustained competitive advantage (CA) is a strategic approach that involves leveraging a company ' s  unique capabilities and resources (internal strengths) to capitalise on market opportunities (environmental opportunities), while simultaneously managing and mitigating external risks  (external  threats)  and  addressing  internal  deficiencies  (internal weaknesses)  (Barney,  1991).  This  study  aims  to  deepen  the  understanding  of  how  AI-powered  CRM  systems  can  enhance  a  firm ' s competitiveness.  It  employs  the  microfoundations  of  the  dynamic capability view, which focuses on the small-scale, foundational actions taken  by  the  banking  organisation.  The  hypothesis  posits  that  the organisation can achieve a lasting competitive edge by effectively performing specific, detailed actions (Helfat & Peteraf, 2015; Teece, 2007). These everyday activities, when supported by AI-powered CRM capabilities, can lead to a sustained competitive advantage.

Furthermore, the literature provides precedence supporting the link between sustained competitive advantage and sustained profitability. By  strategically  considering  a  company ' s  sustainability  and  resource imitation  forces,  executives  can  forecast  dynamic  pricing  patterns, establish strategic guidelines for organisational learning processes and capture profits from technological innovation (Teece, 1986; Williams, 1992). This strategic foresight and adaptability are essential for maintaining a competitive edge in a rapidly evolving market. Therefore, it is highly plausible to hypothesise that leveraging AI-powered CRM capabilities enhances sustained competitive advantage, subsequently leading to  sustained  profitability.  Accordingly,  the  following  hypothesis  is proposed:

H5 . : AI-powered CRM capabilities have a significant positive effect on sustained competitive advantage.

H6 . :  Sustained competitive advantage of AI-powered CRM capabilities influences sustained profitability.

By integrating AI-powered CRM systems, firms can enhance their microfoundations of DC, enabling them to sense, seize, and transform opportunities  more  effectively  than  their  competitors  (Teece,  2007). This strategic use of AI not only supports immediate competitive gains but  also  fosters  long-term  sustainability,  ensuring  continued  profitability and market leadership.

## 4.6. The mediating role of marketing ambidexterity

Marketing ambidexterity (MA) refers to the ability to retain current customers and exploit existing markets while simultaneously exploring new  ones,  ensuring  sustained  success  through  the  coordination  of innovation, adaptation to environmental changes, and refinement and extension of existing processes and technologies (Voss & Voss, 2013). According to De Luca et al. (2021), companies can maintain competitiveness  by  utilising  data-driven  analytics;  this  is  achieved through marketing ambidexterity, where companies leverage insights from  evolving  customer  needs  and  market  trends.  When  a  company lacks marketing ambidexterity, its resources and capabilities will stagnate, preventing any strategic advantage from being realised. Conversely, ambidexterity facilitates both the exploration of new opportunities and the exploitation of current ones, ensuring the company remains dynamic and competitive in the market (Hughes et al., 2010). Investigating the mediating role of marketing ambidexterity between AI-powered CRM capabilities and organisational outcomes is important for several reasons. It unveils the underlying mechanisms driving these relationships, providing insights essential for developing strategies that fully  leverage  AI-powered  CRM  systems.  Validating  this  mediation provides  strategic  insights  into  balancing  market  exploration  and exploitation,  which  is  crucial  for  maintaining  competitiveness  and long-term  profitability.  Additionally,  it  enhances  decision-making  by aligning  resource  allocation  with  strategies  that  promote  sustained competitive advantages. This evaluation extends beyond direct effects, ensuring  that  CRM  strategies  are  both  technologically  advanced  and strategically  integrated  with  market  dynamics  and  customer  needs, resulting in superior organisational performance. Hence, this research postulates the following hypotheses:

The  strategic importance of marketing ambidexterity lies in its potential to bridge the gap between technological capabilities and market-oriented outcomes. ## 5.1. Measures

This study has utilised both explanatory and exploratory research methodologies. The instrument development process commenced with formulating conceptual definitions for each construct and compiling an initial  list  of  items  that  closely  align  with  the  dimensions  of  these construct  definitions  (Creswell & Creswell,  2023;  MacKenzie  et  al., 2011). Following the recommendations of MacKenzie et al. (2011), this process  meticulously  considered  the  nature  of  the  construct,  encompassing the conceptual domain and the applicable entity. The conceptual definition of  AI-powered CRM,  along  with its dimensions, sub-dimensions, and measurement items, is illustrated in the conceptual model and presented in Table 3. Data governance was measured using the scale developed by Mikalef and Krogstie (2020), while data intelligence was assessed using measures by Ghasemaghaei (2019). The constructs of data privacy were evaluated by Parasuraman et al. (2005). Additionally, content consistency was measured by Sun et al. (2020) and process consistency by Lee et al. (2019). Personalisation and automation constructs were both measured by Lin et al. (2022), and meaningfulness was assessed using the scale developed by Nakata et al. (2018). The mediating variable, marketing ambidexterity, was measured using items adapted  from  the  study  by  Akter  et  al.  (2023).  For  the  outcome constructs,  the  sustained  profitability  was  measured  by  Vorhies  and Morgan (2005), and the sustained competitive advantage was measured by Cao et al. (2019).

To examine the impacts of AI-powered CRM capability and marketing ambidexterity on outcome constructs, this study controlled for demographic  variables,  including  age,  gender,  education  level,  management level, and years of experience. These controls are essential as they may ultimately influence the outcomes. Participants were selected based on a minimum of two years ' experience with AI-powered CRM tools,  ensuring their familiarity and proficiency with the technology. Before testing its validity, it was necessary to evaluate and pre-test the AI-powered CRM scale (Hair et al., 2022; MacKenzie et al., 2011). As part of the pre-testing process, data were collected from 44 respondents to confirm the format, scales, sequence and wording of the questions. Additionally, pilot data were gathered from 60 members of the banking industry to assess the reliability and validity of the overall conceptual model.  Table  3 provides  the  operationalised  scales,  along  with  their corresponding definitions and measurement items. These variables were measured using reliable and well-established scales from prior research to ensure the validity of the findings.

## 5.2. Main study

Data collection was facilitated by a leading marketing research firm in Australia from March to April 2024. The firm is a globally recognised data  insights  organisation  renowned  for  its  industry-leading  online research capabilities and strong relationships with Australia ' s "big four" banks. The firm serves over 750 clients worldwide and offers innovative solutions (Kopsiaftis, 2023). Respondents were selected based on predefined criteria. The purposive selection of participants from the ' big four ' banks was aimed at ensuring optimal representation of the target population (Creswell & Creswell, 2023; Krishnaswamy & Satyaprasad, 2010). Specifically, the study targeted banking employees aged 18 years and above with a minimum of one year of experience using AI-powered CRM systems for banking. This criterion was deliberately chosen to serve as the sampling element for the research. A total of 774 survey responses were attempted. Out of these, 569 responses were excluded for failing to meet  the  eligibility  criteria  based  on  the  specified  questions.  These criteria  were  established  to  ensure  that  only  respondents  with direct experience in utilising AI-powered CRM systems and a sufficient understanding  of  how  banks  employ  data  analytics  for  customer  data analysis  were  included.  Additionally,  responses  exhibiting  flatlining, speeding, missing values, or other suspicious patterns (such as inconsistent or repetitive answers) were manually reviewed and excluded. These  exclusion  rules  were  designed  to  safeguard  the  quality  and integrity  of  the  data,  ensuring  that only knowledgeable and relevant participants  contributed  to  the  analysis.  Reporting  these  details  is crucial for transparency and for ensuring the generalisability and validity of the study ' s findings (Creswell & Creswell, 2023). Consequently, after  applying  the  eligibility  criteria,  205  completed  responses  were retained  for  the  final  analysis. ## 7. Findings, discussion and implications

To address the research question concerning AI-powered CRM capabilities, the study ' s findings identify various microfoundations within the domains of data management, multi-channel integration, and service  offerings  capabilities.  Specifically,  data  governance  ( β = 0.905, p < 0.001), data intelligence ( β = 0.731, p < 0.001) and data privacy ( β = 0.888, p < 0.001) emerge as critical components that constitute data management capability in the context of AI-powered CRM. These results corroborate the assertions of Lokuge et al. (2020) and Gregory (2011), who  emphasise  that  customer  data  is  a  valuable  corporate  asset requiring meticulous protection and active management. The integration of governance, privacy and analytics is highlighted as a significant enhancement to AI-powered CRM systems. Similarly, content consistency  ( β = 0.933,  p < 0.001)  and  process  consistency  ( β = 0.962, p < 0.001)  are  identified  as  significant  dimensions  of  multi-channel integration  capability.  These  results  are  in  line  with  the  findings  of Wilson and Daniel (2007), who advocate for the adoption of CRM systems as essential components in the transformation towards engaging customers through multiple channels more effectively. The AI-powered CRM system significantly  contributes  to  this  transition  by  managing customer data, streamlining processes and enhancing interactions across various channels. Furthermore, these findings confirm the recent study by Abadie et al. (2023), which highlights that AI facilitates cross-channel  process  performance  and  highlights  the  importance  of both tangible and intangible resources. This study also reinforces the role  of  AI  in  overcoming  challenges  associated  with  multi-channel integration.  Finally,  service  offerings  capability  is  explained  by  personalisation ( β = 0.930, p < 0.001), automation ( β = 0.834, p < 0.001), and meaningfulness ( β = 0.921, p < 0.001). Service offering capability appears to be the most significant predictor due to the importance of these subdimensions in serving customer needs in an AI-driven world. Leveraging market orientation outlook, this function plays an instrumental  role  in  attracting  more  prospects,  closing  more  deals,  and strengthening  customer  relationships  through  the  unification  of  AI powered CRM  activities on an integrated platform. While all second-order dimensions are significant and meaningful, service offering capability stands out as the strongest determinant.

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

The study presents several notable theoretical contributions. It diverges from prevailing trends in AI-powered CRM research, which predominantly focuses on strategic management and business success at the macro level (Chatterjee et al., 2022; Chaudhuri et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2023). Our findings advance CRM knowledge by incorporating microfoundational  perspectives  of  data  management,  multi-channel integration  and  service  offering  capabilities  in  developing  an  integrated AI-powered CRM capability model. This consolidative approach not only enhances the comprehension of AI capabilities but also provides a nuanced understanding of their operationalisation. For example, our findings extend  this line of  inquiry  by  identifying  three  microfoundations  of  offering  capabilities:  service  personalisation,  robust automation,  and  the  provision  of  meaningful  offerings  that  are  not compromised by algorithmic  hallucinations  or  biases.  These  offering capabilities operate in tandem with two other critical capabilities, data management (e.g., governance, intelligence, and privacy) and channel integration (e.g., content and process) to form a higher-order, AI-powered CRM capability model.

While prior research has extended the DC theory within CRM investigations  (Chatterjee  et  al.,  2022;  Chaudhuri  et  al.,  2022;  Kumar et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2023), it has predominantly overlooked the microfoundations  inherent  to  the  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities.  This research advances the microfoundational perspective of DC theory to address current challenges by precisely outlining the main attributes of eight microfoundations, both at the individual and higher-order levels, that  are  essential  to  form  an  AI-powered  CRM  capability  model (Magistretti et al., 2021).

Our research advances the theory by identifying the complementary and  cospecialisation  attributes  of  microfoundations  (i.e.,  data  governance,  data  intelligence,  data  privacy,  content  consistency,  process consistency,  personalisation,  automation  and  meaningfulness)  under three  primary  capabilities.  The  discernment  of  both  lower-order  and higher-order capabilities not only enhances the understanding of the concept but also extends knowledge of its holistic impact on marketing ambidexterity and critical outcomes.

For example, our findings build a critical bridge between the theoretical concepts of capability and ambidexterity, thereby shaping the emerging  landscape  of  AI-powered  CRM.  It  clarifies  how  marketing ambidexterity can be achieved by exploiting data governance, intelligence and privacy across multiple channels (email, web, mobile, and  social)  to  explore  the  next-best  actions  and  generate  dynamic, personalised offering. Specifically, the investigation into the mediating role  of  marketing ambidexterity extends the CRM literature by highlighting the strategic importance of balancing exploration (innovation) and exploitation (operational excellence) in an AI environment. This dual  focus  will  empower  firms  to  be  agile  and  innovative  without sacrificing  efficiency,  thereby  achieving  marketing  ambidexterity  by leveraging the microfoundations of AI capabilities.

Our findings offer a refined understanding of how organisations can achieve sustained competitive advantage and profitability using marketing ambidexterity. First, our findings establish the theoretical relationship between ambidexterity and sustained profitability through AIpowered  recommendation  algorithms,  dynamic  personalisation,  and customer retention, which is far more effective than typical CRM-based acquisitions, directly improving long-term profitability. The operational excellence  of  ambidexterity  also  enhances  cross-selling  and  reduces customer churn, driving steady profit growth. At the same time, the ability to innovate constantly makes switching costs high for customers, ensuring sustained competitive advantages. For example, these findings highlight pathways to competitive advantage in the financial industry through fraud detection, personalised financial product offerings, and data-driven customer engagement, enabled by the exploitation capability of strict governance, privacy, and compliance frameworks.

Overall, our findings elucidate how AI-powered CRM capabilities can be developed to address uncertainty through exploitation of data and process and exploration of new offerings. This perspective offers insights into the continuous evolution of microfoundations of DC, emphasising the need for ongoing adaptation and learning within organisations.

## 7.2. Managerial and Policy Implications

This study was conducted to help financial institutions in Australia understand how to implement AI-powered CRM practices successfully. The empirical results provide a comprehensive framework for understanding  the  key  dimensions  necessary  for  effective  implementation. One of the most critical  dimensions  highlighted  is  the  imperative  of effective data management. This includes data governance, systematic analysis and data protection, all of which are essential for informing strategic  decision-making  and  optimising  CRM  processes  (Gregory, 2011). By prioritising robust data governance frameworks and advanced data  analytics,  organisations  can  enhance  operational  efficiency  and safeguard  customer  information.  This  study  emphasises  the  practical importance of developing comprehensive data management strategies to fully leverage the potential of AI-powered CRM systems. Technologies such as AI-powered CRM systems, which leverage intelligent solutions, big data analytics, and the Internet of Things (IoT), provide managers with new insights for developing innovative strategies (Lokuge et al., 2020).

Effective  CRM  across  various  communication  and  sales  channels necessitates the support of a robust AI-powered CRM system. To ensure cohesive and efficient management of these relationships, CRM systems must be accessible  not  only  to  direct  employees  but  also  to  indirect partners,  such  as  third-party  vendors  or  resellers,  who  engage  with customers on behalf of the company (Wilson & Daniel, 2007). This inclusive  approach  ensures  that  all  parties  involved  in  customer  interactions have the necessary tools and information to deliver a seamless and unified customer experience across multiple channels of interaction. The  effective  management  of  customer  relationships  across  diverse channels necessitates the implementation of a robust AI-powered CRM system, with a particular emphasis on enhancing service offerings. The study ' s  findings  highlight  the  ability  of  employees  to  automate  and personalise services, thereby adding meaningful value to customer interactions. However, Wang and Zhang (2025a) found that ethical anxiety about AI digital employees can negatively moderate the technology ' s  impact  on  innovative  service  offerings.  Policymakers should  consider  implementing  ethics  training  programs  for  financial industry  in  Australia  in  rolling  out  AI-powered  CRM  initiatives  for various  service  innovations.  Thus,  time  befitting  AI  ethics  training, knowledge of updated AI ethics policies and establishing industry wide standards could reduce anxiety while empowering the ethical development and deployment of AI powered CRM in the financial industry (Li and Huang, 2020; Hani & Akter, 2024).

There are several additional practical implications relating to strategic resource application, employee training and development, enhanced  customer  insights,  improved  decision-making  processes, innovation and adaptability, and compliance and risk management. For example, by understanding the specific AI-powered CRM capabilities that drive competitive advantage and profitability, managers can make more informed decisions about where to allocate resources. This strategic allocation ensures that investments are directed towards areas with the highest potential for return, enhancing overall organisational efficiency  (Füller  et  al.,  2022).  The  study  highlights  the  importance  of equipping employees with the skills needed to utilise AI-powered CRM systems effectively. Financial institutions should invest in comprehensive  training programs that focus on data analytics, automation, and personalisation to maximise the benefits of AI technologies.

AI-powered  CRM  systems  provide  deep  insights  into  customer behaviour  and  preferences  (Libai,  Bart,  Gensler,  Hofacker,  Kaplan, K ¨ otterheinrich, et al., 2020). Financial institutions can utilise these insights to develop targeted marketing campaigns, refine customer segmentation,  and  refine  their  customer  loyalty  programs,  ultimately leading to increased customer satisfaction and retention. The integration of AI-powered CRM capabilities can enhance decision-making processes by providing real-time data and predictive analytics (Ledro et al., 2023). This allows managers to make proactive decisions that align with market trends and customer needs, ensuring the organisation remains competitive and responsive.

The  study  underscores  the  role  of  marketing  ambidexterity  in leveraging AI-powered CRM systems. Financial institutions should foster a culture of innovation and adaptability, encouraging teams to explore new market opportunities while optimising existing processes. This dual focus can drive sustained growth and innovation. With increasing regulatory scrutiny in the financial sector, AI-powered CRM systems can help  institutions  ensure  compliance  with  data  protection  laws  and manage risks more effectively. Robust data governance frameworks and automated compliance checks can mitigate potential legal and financial risks. For example, our findings can contribute to upgrade data governance  policies  in  Australia  aligning  with  CSIRO  (2019) ' s  ethical framework of AI focusing on eight core principles as follows: generates net-benefits,  do  not  harm,  regulatory  and  legal  compliance,  privacy protection, fairness, transparency and explainability, contestability and accountability.

Microfoundations  within  a  finance  organisation  are  deeply  interconnected, necessitating that managers adopt a holistic perspective on AI-powered CRM capabilities. This approach involves recognising the intertwined nature of these foundational elements and fostering a proactive environment across various organisational functions (Khan et al., 2020). By doing so, managers can design innovative and effective service offerings, leveraging a comprehensive understanding of their organisation ' s dynamic capabilities (Kindstr ¨ om et al., 2013). Furthermore, policymakers can harness the potential of AI-powered CRM capabilities by developing and implementing frameworks that encourage innovative service offerings while ensuring ethical standards. Firstly, establishing regulatory  measures  to  protect  data  privacy  can  help  mitigate  risks associated with AI-powered CRM adoption, ensuring that the benefits are  maximised  while  maintaining  public  confidence  and  compliance with legal standards. Additionally, providing guidelines that promote transparency and accountability in AI algorithms can build trust among users and stakeholders (Gonçalves, 2020). Furthermore, collaboration with industry leaders and academic institutions can foster an environment of continuous improvement and knowledge sharing (Iqbal, 2021). By incentivising research and development (R & D) in AI-powered CRM technologies, policymakers can stimulate advancements that enhance CRM functionalities. Our findings can directly contribute to the safe, consistent and responsible use of AI policies and practices in Australia such as ISO/IEC 23894:2023 Information technology - Artificial intelligence -Guidance on risk management (PM & C, 2023).

Given  the  multifaceted  nature  of  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities within  financial  institutions,  managers  must  integrate  various  organisational functions to assemble actionable insights. These findings underscore the interconnection of microfoundations within the organisational processes, structure and interactions (Magistretti et al., 2021). As such, managers are encouraged to adopt a holistic perspective on AI-powered CRM capabilities, integrating ethical principles to deliver effective service offerings, ultimately contributing to business success. Drawing  on  global  policies,  such  as  OECD  AI  principles  (OECD.AI, 2024), The Global Partnership on AI (GPAI) (GPAI, 2023), Globalpolicy. ai,  a  coalition  of  various  government  Wang  ations  (GlobalPolicy.ai, 2023), AI legal instrument by the Council of Europe to protect human rights (Council of Europe, 2023), UNESCO ' s 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (UNESCO, 2022), our findings suggest embracing an ethical AI framework that will data privacy and security in this evolving landscape.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research Directions

This  study  has  certain  limitations  that  must  be  acknowledged. Despite being carefully designed and executed to address the research questions, the study ' s methodology and focus introduce some constraints.

First, due to the exploratory nature of the study, we initially conducted a scoping review (Study 1) to map the existing literature, identify gaps,  and  explore  the  dimensions  of  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities. Leveraging our findings, future studies can conduct a systematic literature review on each dimension of our research model as this process provides specific, focused and in-depth evidence.

Second, the data collection  was  limited  to  the  Big  Four  banks  in Australia, and the empirical investigation was based on responses from individuals with at least two years of experience using AI-powered CRM technology.  Consequently,  the  findings  derived  from  the  Australian banking industry may not be applicable to other industries or contexts. Moreover, it remains uncertain whether the results would be consistent in different countries. Therefore, it is recommended that future research should test the proposed conceptual framework in diverse settings to enhance the generalisability of the conclusions.

A third limitation of this study is the cross-sectional data collection approach,  which  captures  responses  at  a  single  point  in  time.  This method  inherently  restricts  the  ability  to  observe  variations  in  respondents ' perceptions and understanding over time. Consequently, the results may not accurately reflect changes in concepts and attitudes that could emerge over time. Specifically, these limitations are relevant to our  two  outcome  constructs:  sustained  profitability  and  sustained competitive  advantage.  We  used  cross-sectional  study  to  measure managers ' perceptions on these two aspects. A future study using longitudinal design could adequately measure these constructs as it will help  track  changes  in  response  patterns  over  an  extended  period, thereby  providing  a  more  comprehensive  understanding  of  the  dynamics involved.

[S. 17]

Furthermore,  this  study  did  not  include  organisational  or  digital culture,  which  may  influence  how  AI-powered  CRM  capabilities  are formed. Consulting evidence highlights its importance for AI adoption and performance outcomes, with BCG (2018) noting that companies prioritising digital culture achieve significantly better results (Hemerling et al., 2018). Prior research also shows that digital culture can  act  as  a  critical  moderator  in  digital  transformation  processes, shaping the link between digital adoption, strategic drive, and innovation performance (Wang & Zhang, 2025b). Future studies could therefore  examine  how  cultural  orientations  influence  or  moderate  the integration of AI-powered CRM components.

Finally,  while  this  study  identified  three  dimensions  and  eight microfoundations of AI-powered CRM capability, there may be additional dimensions that warrant exploration. Although some research has examined the impact of new AI-powered CRM systems on organisational performance, there is limited investigation into the microfoundational dynamics of AI-powered CRM and their effects on innovation, profitability and growth. Future studies should consider developing scales to measure marketing capabilities in areas such as communication, security and creative offerings. Additionally, incorporating a dyadic research approach  that  includes  perspectives  from  both  employees  and  consumers  could  provide  a  more  comprehensive  understanding  of  these dynamics. This conceptualised the sequential relationship between AIpowered CRM capability and marketing ambidexterity and examined its impact on sustained profitability and sustained competitive advantage.  However,  further  research  is  needed  to  explore  other  potential mediators and outcome variables. Such investigations could provide a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms and effects associated with AI-powered CRM capabilities, thereby enriching the existing body of knowledge and offering broader insights into organisational performance and strategic outcomes.
