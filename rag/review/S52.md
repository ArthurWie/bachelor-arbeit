## ABSTRACT

Academic Summary: Firms face intensifying pressure to innovate amid escalating US-China trade tensions. This study investigates how they integrate artificial intelligence (AI) to respond to such disruptions. We reconceptualize induced innovation theory as adversity-  induced innovation theory and test hypotheses using data from listed Chinese manufacturing firms, WTO tariff records, and customs data (2015-2022). Results indicate that higher tariff exposure increases AI integration, particularly among firms with stronger customer engagement needs. AI integration improves supply chain efficiency, sales performance, and market value. However, performance benefits follow an inverted U-  shape: extreme tariff exposure diminishes AI's compensatory effects. The study extends induced innovation theory to geopolitical adversities and advances stakeholder engagement theory by identifying customer engagement as a boundary condition for technology adoption. We establish causal relationships between tariff-  induced AI integration and firm performance through instrumental variable analysis and robustness checks. Findings inform how firms can strategically use technological innovation and customer collaboration to strengthen resilience under external shocks. Managers should view trade tensions as catalysts for AI integration rather than merely as obstacles.

Managerial Summary: Firms facing trade tensions should view them as catalysts for strategic AI integration rather than merely as obstacles. Our research shows that firms experiencing higher tariff exposure that invest in AI achieve measurable improvements in supply chain efficiency, sales performance, and market valuations. However, AI's compensatory benefits diminish at extreme tariff levels, revealing an optimal investment threshold that managers should consider. The benefits of AI integration are particularly strong for firms with high customer engagement needs, such as those with concentrated customer bases, substantial international sales, or significant customer-  specific investments. To maximize returns, managers should prioritize AI technologies that strengthen customer relationships by enhancing communication, responsiveness, and service personalization during trade disruptions. Successful implementation requires navigating technical and organizational challenges, including talent acquisition, data quality concerns, and integration complexities. Despite these hurdles, our findings confirm AI's strategic value as a compensatory innovation tool that not only mitigates tariff impacts but also transforms supplier-  customer relationships. By aligning AI initiatives with customer engagement objectives, firms can turn geopolitical adversity into a competitive advantage. Companies that integrate AI strategically can maintain operational continuity, preserve market position, and emerge stronger from trade disruptions.

## 1   |   Introduction

The resurgence of nationalism and protectionism has become a defining feature of today's global economy, fueling trade tensions  and  global  supply  chain  disruptions  (Chor  and  Li  2024; Grossman et al. 2024). Geopolitical flashpoints, including the US-China  trade  dispute  and  conflicts  in  Ukraine  and  Gaza, have forced firms to overhaul supply networks, disrupting the movement of goods, capital, and information on a global scale (Moradlou et al. 2021). Tariffs, as a central instrument of these changes, have undermined the stability and resilience of supply chains (Benguria et al. 2022), contributing to sharp declines in  investment,  production,  R&D,  and  profitability  (Benguria et al. 2022; Chor and Li 2024). In response, firms have begun to diversify suppliers and adopt new technologies to mitigate risk and restore operational continuity (Dong and Kouvelis 2020).

The integration of  Artificial  Intelligence  (AI)  into  global  supply  chain  management  has  become  a  key  strategic  approach for  addressing  geopolitical  disruptions  and  generating  value for diverse stakeholder groups (Haenlein and Kaplan 2019). AI technologies, including machine learning, natural language processing, computer vision, and predictive analytics, enable firms to  analyze  vast  amounts  of  data  from  various  sources  in  real time, providing unprecedented insights and agility (Davenport and Ronanki 2018). AI-  powered innovations could be utilized to enhance risk management, support decision-  making, increase efficiency, improve customer engagement, and foster innovation (Baryannis et al. 2019; Haenlein and Kaplan 2019; Toorajipour et  al.  2021).  However,  the  integration  of  AI  is  not  without  its challenges, which include addressing talent shortages, managing ethical concerns, ensuring data quality, and overcoming integration difficulties (Wamba et al. 2020).

AI integration becomes increasingly important as firms respond to shifts in trade policy and adjust their supply chain structures. However, in light of the implementation challenges, successful adoption often depends not only on internal technological capacity but also on the degree to which firms align these technologies with the expectations of external stakeholders, particularly primary customers. This alignment matters because suppliers' ability to maintain effective engagement with key business customers can significantly influence both the implementation of AI  and  resulting  performance  outcomes  (Brodie  et  al.  2011). Customer  engagement  involves  several  interrelated  elements: the strength of collaboration, mutual understanding, and trust between suppliers and customers (Brodie et al. 2011); the extent to which AI-  enabled systems and offerings are adapted to meet specific customer needs (Tuli et al. 2007); and the integration of customer priorities into suppliers' operational decisions, strategic initiatives, and value delivery processes (Vivek et al. 2012).

According to the induced innovation theory (Acemoglu 2010; Hicks  1932),  changes  in  relative  factor  prices  steer  the  trajectory of technological change. This theory has been empirically supported  across  various  sectors,  including  energy  (Newell et al. 2010; Popp 2002), agriculture (Ruttan 1977; Olmstead and Rhode 1993), and environmental technologies (Ren et al. 2022). Recent  studies  have  extended  its  application  to  explain  how economic challenges drive innovation, such as emissions trading systems stimulating green innovation (Ren et al. 2022) and rising labor costs driving technological advancements in China's secondary industry (Fleisher et al. 2021). Despite this rich body of literature, the existing literature on induced innovation theory  fails  to  adequately  address  its  application  to  suppliers'  responses to trade disruptions, particularly in the context of AI integration.

Current research has not fully explored how sudden changes in trade policy, particularly the introduction of higher tariffs, can push  suppliers  to  quickly  adopt  new  technologies.  Moreover, there is a lack of empirical evidence linking AI integration to firm  performance  in  times  of  trade  disruptions.  Specifically, the  mechanisms  by  which  tariff  hikes  might  induce  AI  integration  in  supply  chain  management  are  not  well  understood (Acemoglu 2015). This gap is particularly problematic given the increasing frequency and impact of trade disruptions in today's global economy (Benguria et al. 2022).

To  address  these  gaps  in  the  innovation  literature,  this  study aims to examine whether firms espouse AI integration as a strategic response to geopolitical disruptions, specifically, the tariff shock, and how the firm's need for engagement with its primary customers plays a role in this response. Extending the induced innovation  theory  to  the  broader  area  of  adversity,  we  argue that in response to U.S. tariff hikes, Chinese suppliers are likely to  turn  to  AI  technological  integration  to  offset  the  increased costs. We hypothesize that firms with higher exposure to tariff increases are more likely to embrace AI integration in an effort to mitigate the impact of trade policy uncertainty on their operations and performance. Moreover, we posit that this relationship is stronger for firms with higher needs for engagement with their primary customers. Finally, we propose that for firms with higher tariff exposure, a higher level of AI integration leads to improved firm performance.

Our study makes several important contributions to the growing literature on AI, stakeholder engagement, and innovation management. First, we extend traditional induced innovation theory to broader adversity-  induced innovation and provide empirical evidence of the strategic value of AI integration in mitigating the negative effects of tariff exposure. Second, we introduce primary customer engagement as a key moderator and explore its role in influencing firms' AI integration responses to such adversity. Finally, the study enriches the literature on AI's strategic value by providing empirical evidence linking AI integration to firm performance during trade disruptions.

## 2   |   Theory and Hypotheses

## 2.1   |   Induced Innovation Theory

Induced innovation theory, first proposed by Hicks (1932) and further developed by Acemoglu (2010), posits that shifts in relative factor prices steer the trajectory of innovation, as firms are incentivized to develop and adopt technologies that reduce their reliance on more expensive inputs (Acemoglu 2015). The theory finds support from various studies. For example, higher energy  prices  have  led  to  increased  innovation  in  energyefficient technologies (Popp 2002), and energy efficiency regulations have induced innovation in air conditioners and water heaters  (Newell  et  al.  2010).  The  theory  has  also  explained technological and institutional changes in agriculture (Asche and Smith 2018; Olmstead and Rhode 1993; Ruttan 1977) and energy sectors (Grubb et al. 2020). Recent applications demonstrate  how  economic  challenges  drive  technological  change: emissions  trading  systems  stimulate  green  innovation  (Ren et  al.  2022),  financial  constraints  enhance  R&D  efficiency in green technologies (Zhang and Jin 2021), and rising labor costs drive innovation in China's secondary industry (Fleisher et al. 2021).

[S. 3]

Building on these findings, we argue that induced innovation theory can be extended to account for a wider range of adversities  beyond  purely  economic  conditions.  Adversity  refers  to circumstances  that  hinder  individuals,  firms,  or  governments in  pursuing their objectives (Asay and Bair 2021; Rosenzweig and Grinstein 2016). The COVID-  19 pandemic illustrates this point:  severe  global  disruption  stimulated  significant  innovation across healthcare, education, and food services, leading to the development of new solutions to address pandemic-  related challenges  (Asay  and  Bair  2021).  Both  the  Western  proverb 'Necessity is the mother of invention' and the Chinese proverb 'In hardship, consider change' reflect this principle, suggesting a  cross-  cultural  recognition  that  difficult  circumstances  often encourage  creative  problem-  solving.  However,  adversity  has boundaries. Extreme or prolonged hardship, such as persistent poverty, may suppress creativity and innovation (Bair 2020), indicating a threshold beyond which adversity becomes detrimental. Access to resources, supportive institutional arrangements, and  organizational capabilities strongly influence  whether adversity  fosters  innovation.  This  broader  view  of  adversityinduced innovation provides opportunities for examining how different  forms  of  challenge  influence  technological  development across sectors and societies.

## 2.2   |   The Rise of AI and Firms' AI Integration

AI has become a key driver of business innovation, transforming how companies create value across industries (Haenlein and Kaplan 2019; Hollebeek et al. 2021; Wetzels 2021). We define AI integration as the adoption, investment in, and strategic implementation of AI and related technologies to address external challenges  and  influence  performance.  This  includes  cognitive  AI,  which  performs  human-  like  perception,  reasoning, and  interaction  through  natural  language  processing,  speech recognition,  and  computer  vision,  and  analytical  AI,  which examines  data  for  insights,  predictions,  and  decision  support using machine learning, deep learning, and predictive analytics. Companies may employ various AI technologies to address trade disruptions and create stakeholder value.

AI  offers  substantial  economic  benefits.  It  analyzes  extensive,  real-  time  data  on  business  processes  and  product  usage (Rammer et al. 2022). Firms can automate tasks, improve operations,  and  enhance  product  performance.  AI  generates  insights, predictions, and automated decision-  making capabilities (Baryannis et al. 2019; Toorajipour et al. 2021). It supports datadriven business models and helps develop new products and services (Lee et al. 2019). AI adoption is linked to revenue growth, particularly when combined with complementary technologies

(Ardito  et  al.  2024).  It  also  transforms  customer  interactions through behavioral insights and personalized marketing (Filieri et al. 2021; Kopalle et al. 2022). Beyond economic gains, AI provides  social  and  sustainability  benefits.  It  helps  firms  address sustainability challenges, manage waste, and facilitate sustainable  transformations  in  production  and  innovation  (Mariani et al. 2023). AI supports cost reduction, enhances productivity, shortens  product  development  cycles,  and  improves  decisionmaking (Verganti et al. 2020).

However, AI integration presents significant challenges. Concerns about job loss from automation (Frey and Osborne  2017),  the  time  required  for  process  adaptation  and worker  reskilling  (Tambe  et  al.  2019),  and  issues  related  to data quality, skill gaps, technological uncertainty, and regulations all hinder adoption (Haefner et al. 2021). To realize AI's benefits  fully,  firms  must  move  beyond  mere  adoption.  They should  integrate  AI  into  strategy  and  operations  (Raisch  and Krakowski  2021).  This  requires  strategic  alignment,  resource allocation, and organizational integration (Haefner et al. 2021), employee  capabilities  (Garbuio  and  Lin  2021),  and  external innovation  partnerships  (Sjödin  et  al.  2021).  Our  conceptualization of AI integration allows for assessment of how organizations leverage AI to address external challenges and how such integration influences performance.

## 2.3   |   Customer Engagement

Supply chain disruptions from trade tensions create unique challenges for firms that depend heavily on their primary customers. How these firms respond to adversity may depend on the strength and nature of their customer relationships. Understanding this connection requires examining customer engagement as a strategic construct. Customer engagement involves resource investments in interactions, activities, and relationships that extend beyond simple exchanges (Vivek et al. 2012). Drawing on stakeholder theory and service-  dominant logic, engagement research recognizes that firms must consider diverse stakeholder interests to create value (Freeman et al. 2010; Hollebeek et al. 2022). Customer engagement specifically includes knowledge sharing (Filieri 2013), cooperation, and collaboration toward mutual objectives (Viglia et al. 2018, 2023). These collaborative processes require  substantive  resource  commitment  from  both  parties (Hollebeek et al. 2023).

In  this  study,  we  focus  on  the  need  for  primary  customer  engagement,  defined  as  the  extent  to  which  a  supplier's  operations, strategies, and value creation processes depend on close collaboration, mutual understanding, and trust with its primary customers (Brodie et al. 2011; Vivek et al. 2012). This need intensifies when suppliers make significant relationship-  specific investments and customize their offerings to meet the distinct requirements of their major customers (Tuli et al. 2007). Suppliers with  high  dependence  on  primary  customer  engagement  face different pressures and opportunities when responding to trade disruptions  compared  with  suppliers  with  more  transactional customer relationships.

The need for primary customer engagement becomes particularly  relevant  in  the  context  of  AI  integration.  Suppliers  with strong  customer  engagement  relationships  may  face  pressure to adopt AI technologies that align with customer expectations or requirements. These suppliers may also have access to richer customer data and collaborative opportunities that enable more effective AI implementation. Conversely, the costs and risks of AI adoption may be higher for suppliers whose primary customers demand stability and proven solutions rather than technological experimentation. Understanding how the need for primary customer engagement influences AI integration decisions under trade adversity is therefore critical.

## 2.4   |   Hypothesis Development

Building on our extended adversity-  induced innovation theory, we propose that Chinese firms with high exposure to tariffs are likely to seek technological solutions to mitigate the impact of increased costs. AI represents a particularly promising avenue for such innovation. These technologies can automate tasks, replace labor, and enhance productivity (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018), enabling  firms  to  offset  tariff  burdens  and  maintain  their competitive position. AI serves as a key tool for achieving sustainable  supply  chain  objectives,  enhancing  environmentally conscious practices and optimizing supplier selection, forecasting,  and  logistics  (Hao  and  Demir  2025).  It  supports  supplier selection through data-  driven decision-  making, transforms procurement via automation (Nissen and Sengupta 2006), improves warehousing and production processes (Yang et al. 2022), and strengthens  demand  forecasting  (Simchi-  Levi  and  Wu  2018). Thus, we hypothesize:

H1. Suppliers  with  higher  exposure  to  tariffs  will  exhibit  a greater degree of AI integration .

Firms with strong primary customer engagement possess both the motivation and the capability to respond strategically to tariff pressures through AI integration. As established in Section 2.3, deep  collaboration  with  key  customers  maintains  communication  channels  and  provides  insights  into  their  requirements (Ganesan  et  al.  2005;  Rindfleisch  and  Moorman  2001).  This engagement  becomes  particularly  valuable  under  tariff  pressure,  enabling  firms  to  interpret  market  signals  and  adjust  to tariff-  induced demand shifts (Tuli et al. 2007). Trust developed through these relationships fosters an environment conducive to focused innovation efforts (Rindfleisch and Moorman 2003).

The  exchange  of  complex  information  through  customer  engagement  (Ganesan  et  al.  2005)  helps  firms  understand  how tariffs affect customer needs, aligning with induced innovation theory's principle that price signals guide technological change. This reduced uncertainty allows firms to concentrate on value creation through AI rather than on negotiation. Deep engagement  establishes  a  foundation  for  joint  problem-  solving,  and firms  leverage  their  understanding  of  customer  needs  (Vivek et al. 2012) to develop AI solutions that address tariff challenges. The combination of tariff-  induced price pressures and the need for  superior  market understanding through primary customer engagement creates a strong impetus for AI integration. Based on these considerations, we hypothesize:

H2. The positive  relationship  between  tariff  exposure  and  AI integration is stronger for supplier firms with a greater need for primary customer engagement .

When facing tariff exposure, firms motivated to adopt AI technologies  must  address  several  implementation  challenges,  including data quality issues (Hazen et al. 2014), privacy concerns (Martin  and  Murphy  2017),  difficulty  acquiring  AI  expertise, organizational resistance, ethical considerations, and high implementation costs (Moktadir et al. 2019). However, induced innovation theory suggests that firms with higher tariff exposure have greater incentives to pursue AI integration, potentially improving performance despite these obstacles.

AI integration can generate measurable benefits across multiple  business  operations.  In  supply  chains,  AI  optimizes  costs by identifying inefficiencies (Gunasekaran et al. 2017) and enhances  forecasting,  inventory  management,  and  production planning (Dubey et al. 2021). In sales, AI provides a comprehensive view of the supply chain and highlights potential risks (Dubey  et  al.  2021).  For  innovation  and  firm  value,  AI  enables  product,  service,  and  process  improvements  (Zhan  and Tan 2020), which can offset the impact of tariffs. Additionally, AI supports decision-  making with real-  time data (Gunasekaran et al. 2017), enhances operational efficiency for improved profitability (Hazen et al. 2016), and mitigates supply chain risks for more stable valuations. Consequently, tariff exposure can act as a catalyst for AI integration, which in turn strengthens firm performance. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3. The higher the degree of exposure to tariffs, the greater the positive  impact  of  AI  technological  integration  on  firm  performance, as measured by ( a ) supply chain performance ,  ( b ) sales performance, and ( c ) firm value .

## 3   |   Methodology

## 3.1   |   Sample Firms and U.S. Tariff Exposure

This  study  uses  a  large  sample  of  manufacturing  firms  listed on China's Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges from 2015 to  2022.  Financial  data  are  obtained  from  the  Chinese  Stock Market  and  Accounting  Research  Database  (CSMAR).  Our analysis period begins March 22, 2018, when the United States announced tariff  increases  on  $50  billion  of  Chinese  imports (Benguria et al. 2022; Chor and Li 2024; Chen et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2023). Tariff data, including U.S. import tariffs on China and  Chinese  retaliatory  tariffs,  are  collected  from  Li  (2018) and Most Favored Nation (MFN) tariffs from the WTO Tariff Download Facility. Firm-  level trade data come from the China Customs Dataset (2013-2016).

## 3.2   |   Firm's Integration of AI

We utilize the CSMAR database on firm digital transformation to construct an index measure for firm AI integration, extracting  both  quantitative  indicators  and  textual  information  from annual reports. The index measures several key factors:

Term frequency analysis of technical vocabulary (AI, machine  learning,  blockchain,  cloud  computing,  big  data) through structured textual examination.

[S. 7]

Capital  allocation  to  AI-  integrated  projects  (laboratories, platforms,  facilities,  equipment,  infrastructure,  information systems, smart interfaces, robotics).

Executive  leadership  structure  (inclusion  of  CIO,  CTO, CDO positions).

Strategic AI priorities in emerging technology, as reflected by  the  frequency  of  AI  and  digital  transformation  keywords in Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) sections.

Our  methodology  follows  established  textual  analysis  techniques for corporate disclosures (Ardito et al. 2024). The design mitigates  endogeneity  through  the  exogenous  nature  of  U.S. tariff policy changes, which are external and unpredictable to firms. Tariff shocks precede firm responses such as AI integration, creating a clear temporal sequence. Year and industry fixed effects control for temporal trends, macroeconomic conditions, and sectoral heterogeneity, addressing reverse causality, simultaneity, and omitted variable concerns.

## 3.3   |   Need for Primary Customer Engagement

We use three proxy variables to measure supplier firms' primary customer importance: overseas sales, asset specificity, and customer concentration.

Overseas  sales ,  measured  as  the  percentage  of  international  sales  to  total  revenue,  indicate  the  global  reach  of a supplier's customer base. Significant international sales reflect geographically diverse primary customers, increasing demand complexity due to varying market conditions (Johanson and Vahlne 2015).

Asset specificity ,  measured  as  R&D  expenses  relative to  annual  sales,  reflects  investments  dedicated  to  serving  specific  customers  and  indicates  the  firm's  commitment to primary customer relationships (Hagedoorn and Duysters 2002; Rindfleisch and Heide 1997).

Customer concentration , measured as the proportion of sales to major customers, indicates reliance on key customers. High concentration indicates that a substantial portion of revenue comes from a few primary customers, suggesting close and dependent relationships (Kale et al. 2002; Patatoukas 2012).

We use Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to combine these three proxies into a single composite measure.

## 3.4   |   Supply Chain Performance

We use several variables, including the Bullwhip ratio, inventory levels, inventory turnover ratio, and inventory days, to assess supply chain performance. The Bullwhip ratio, defined as the volatility of production divided by the volatility of demand, is  widely  used  to  evaluate  supply  chain  efficiency  (Bayraktar et al. 2020). A ratio exceeding 1 indicates production volatility surpasses  demand  fluctuations,  causing  the  bullwhip  effect, where supply and demand mismatches escalate along the supply chain. Chinese exporters may experience heightened bullwhip ratios due to unexpected increases in U.S. import tariffs, which disrupt demand and production alignment.

To ensure the robustness and validity of our findings, we employ several approaches, focusing in particular on reverse causality and omitted variable bias. To address concerns that technologically oriented firms might be more likely to experience higher tariff  exposure,  which  could  create  reverse  causality,  we  first use lagged tariff exposure measures to establish temporal precedence (Table 7, Column 1). The results indicate that lagged U.S. tariff exposure has a positive and significant effect on AI integration ( β =  0.025; p <  0.05), demonstrating that tariff exposure precedes firms' AI integration decisions.

Second, we conduct a subsample analysis comparing high-  tech and non-  tech firms (Table 7, Columns 2-3). The results reveal no significant difference in the tariff exposure effect between these two groups. A t -  test  comparing  the  coefficients  of  the  two  regressions shows no statistical difference (0.376), suggesting that our findings are not driven by differences in firms' technological orientation. These results collectively address concerns regarding reverse causality.

To account for potential omitted variables that may influence both tariff exposure and AI integration, we implement three distinct instrumental variable (IV) approaches.

First, we use the number of pre-  WTO (before 2001) industrial clusters in each province as an instrument (Table 7, Columns 4-5). This instrument relies on the logic that historical industrial distribution impacts current tariff exposure but is unlikely to directly  influence  contemporary AI integration (Lu et al. 2019). The first-  stage result indicates strong instrument relevance ( F -statistic = 25.05), and the second-  stage result confirms the positive and significant relationship between tariff exposure and AI integration. Second, we employ the density of railways in each province in 1933 as an instrumental variable (Table 7, Columns 6-7).  This  historical  infrastructure  measure  predates  current trade tensions, providing exogenous variation in modern trade patterns. The instrument demonstrates strong statistical power ( F -  statistic  =  39.19), and the second-  stage result aligns with our primary findings. Third, we use the distance between an exporting firm's city location and the nearest seaport as an instrument (Table 7, Columns 8-9). This approach leverages geographic location, which affects trade exposure but is plausibly exogenous to AI integration decisions. We calculate the inverse of the distance, where a larger value indicates closer proximity to the seaport. This instrument also proves strong ( F -  statistic  =  25.59) and supports our main findings.

Across all three IV approaches, the second-  stage results consistently show a positive and significant effect of tariff exposure on AI integration, further supporting the validity of our analysis and reinforcing our main findings. ## 5   |   Discussion and Conclusions

This  study  examines  how  firms  integrate  AI  in  response  to trade tariff disruptions and how customer engagement needs influence this response. We extend induced innovation theory to  develop  adversity-  induced  innovation theory, demonstrating  that  geopolitical  disruptions  drive  technological  change beyond  traditional  factor  price  mechanisms.  Our  findings reveal three key insights: tariff exposure induces AI integration as a compensatory innovation strategy, the need for primary customer engagement moderates this relationship, and AI integration improves firm performance up to an optimal threshold beyond which extreme tariff exposure overwhelms technological  adaptation.  These  results  advance  the  understanding of innovation under adversity and establish boundary conditions for when and how firms leverage technology to address external shocks.

## 5.1   |   Theoretical Contributions

This study advances innovation theory by reconceptualizing induced innovation as a response to adversity beyond traditional factor  price  changes.  Three  primary  theoretical  contributions follow  from  our  empirical  findings.  First,  we  extend  induced innovation theory from its traditional focus on factor price signals to encompass geopolitical disruptions as innovation drivers. Traditional induced innovation theory explains how rising factor costs (labor, energy) direct technological change toward economizing on expensive inputs (Acemoglu 2010; Hicks 1932; Popp 2002; Fleisher et al. 2021). Our findings demonstrate that this  logic  extends  to  geopolitical  adversities:  tariff  exposure functions as an economic shock that induces compensatory innovation through AI integration. This represents a theoretical advance because it identifies adversity as a distinct category of innovation inducement, separate from but related to price-  based mechanisms. The theory now explains innovation responses to sudden external shocks (tariffs, trade disruptions) rather than only  gradual  market-  driven  price  adjustments.  We  term  this extension adversity-  induced innovation theory, broadening the scope  of  conditions  under  which  firms  pursue  technological solutions.

Second, our findings identify customer engagement as a critical  boundary  condition  that  determines  when  adversity  induces innovation. Firms with higher customer engagement needs (measured through overseas sales, asset specificity, and customer concentration) respond more strongly to tariff exposure through AI integration. Third, we provide empirical evidence of the performance consequences of adversity-  induced AI integration. Our results demonstrate that tariff-  exposed firms improve supply chain efficiency, sales  performance,  and  market  value  through  AI  integration. This finding contributes to AI adoption literature (Ardito et al. 2024; Rammer et al. 2022) by establishing the conditions under which AI delivers strategic value, moving beyond  adoption  studies  to  performance  outcomes.  The  nonlinear  relationship  suggests  that  adversity-  induced  innovation has  optimal  ranges,  beyond  which  the  compensatory  benefits diminish.

## 5.2   |   Managerial Implications

This  research  provides  actionable  insights  for  businesses  facing external shocks, particularly trade tariffs. First, managers should consider AI as a central tool for strategic realignment and supplier-  customer engagement. Our findings demonstrate that AI integration improves supply chain efficiency, optimizes sales performance,  and  strengthens  stakeholder  relationships  even amid  geopolitical  challenges.  AI  facilitates  value  co-  creation, allowing suppliers and customers to collaborate on innovation and increase the overall value derived from their interactions. These  results  suggest  that  companies  can  use  AI  to  manage tariff-  induced  uncertainty  rather  than  relying  solely  on  traditional reactive measures.

Second, managers should recognize adversity as an opportunity for compensatory innovation. In the face of challenges such as trade  wars,  companies  can  identify  opportunities  to  advance technologies, refine resource allocation, and adjust production techniques. Implementing AI in operations enables these compensatory innovations, allowing firms not only to survive but to strengthen their competitive position during external shocks.

Third,  companies  should  prioritize  AI-  driven  initiatives  that address  stakeholder  needs,  particularly  customers  affected  by external shocks. AI-  enabled customer insights can support the development  of  personalized  products  and  services,  fostering deeper  supplier-customer  engagement.  Rather  than  treating AI as a temporary solution, firms should integrate AI into core business processes to achieve sustainable benefits, emphasizing stakeholder-  oriented  value  co-  creation.  The  study  highlights that  AI  is  a  strategic  resource  capable  of  driving  innovation, engagement, and resilience, and organizations should actively embed AI in their strategies to maximize these benefits.

## 5.3   |   Limitations and Future Research

Our  research  has  several  limitations  that  suggest  directions for  future  studies.  First,  our  focus  on  customer  engagement excludes  other  stakeholder  groups.  Future  research  could  examine how AI strategies affect government bodies, employees, and  other  stakeholders  to  enhance  strategic  decision-  making. Second, this study centers on US-China trade tensions. Further research should explore how firms employ AI during health crises, economic downturns, or policy changes to test the extension of  adversity-  induced  innovation  theory  in  different  contexts. Third,  our  measurement  approach  has  constraints.  Although we quantify AI integration through investments and projects, case studies or interviews could provide richer insights into how AI  influences  innovation,  stakeholder  engagement,  and  firm stability. Finally, our sample is limited to publicly listed Chinese firms, which may restrict generalizability. Future studies should investigate firms in diverse markets and ownership structures to assess how advanced technologies are leveraged under trade tensions across varied contexts.
