## 1. Introduction

The contemporary manufacturing landscape confronts an existential imperative:  traditional  knowledge  management  no  longer  suffices  to drive  innovation  in  emerging  markets.  While  the  global  knowledge economy  generates  over  $9  trillion  annually  (U.S.  National  Science Foundation,  2020),  emerging  market  manufacturing  SMEs  struggle disproportionately to convert knowledge assets into new product performance (Kumar et al., 2020). A troubling implementationperformance gap persists: although AI fundamentally transforms knowledge processes, potentially unlocking $2.3 trillion for emerging markets (World Economic Forum, 2023), most SMEs fail to realize the 40% higher success rates achieved by AI leaders (Rezaei, 2025). This disconnect reveals a critical theoretical void regarding how AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities  function  as  dynamic  capabilities  in  resourceconstrained contexts (Braganza et al., 2021; Priyono et al., 2020).

## A B S T R A C T

Emerging market manufacturing small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) struggle to translate artificial intelligence (AI) investments into innovation outcomes. This study investigates how AI-enabled knowledge capabilities  drive  new  product  performance  by  employing  a  sequential  mixed-methods  design  with  261 manufacturing SMEs. Our findings reveal that AI-enabled knowledge capabilities operate through dual pathways: directly enhancing performance and indirectly through digital product innovation radicalness. Furthermore, this study identifies digital sustainability leadership as a critical boundary condition acting as both mediator and moderator, fundamentally reconfiguring how AI generates value. This study makes significant contributions to dynamic capabilities theory and the knowledge-based view. By positioning AI-enabled knowledge capabilities as critical  dynamic capabilities, this research clarifies the transformation pathways through which AI generates competitive advantages and offer actionable insights for managers navigating resource-constrained environments.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Emerging market manufacturing SMEs

Emerging  market  manufacturing  SMEs  operate  within  distinctive institutional and resource environments characterized by institutional voids, resource scarcity, market volatility, and regulatory uncertainties that fundamentally differentiate their innovation processes from developed  market  counterparts  (Peng  et  al.,  2008).  Recent  research highlights  that  emerging  markets  are  more  malleable  than  mature markets (Petry, 2025; R ¨ oell et al., 2024), evolving through the development of ecosystems of actors who exchange critical resources and knowledge.  To  successfully  instigate  market  formation  in  such  environments,  firms  must  engage  in  disruptive  market-shaping  processes that  extend  beyond  internal  product  development  to  encompass  the ' forging  of  ecosystem  alliances ' and  the ' catalyzing  of  institutional changes ' (Sandvik et al., 2024). This dynamic perspective conceptualizes markets not as static contexts but as shapeable multi-actor value systems where purposive actions by firms can redesign exchange patterns,  reconfigure  networks, and reform institutions through collaboration with ecosystem stakeholders (Flaig & Ottosson, 2022; Nenonen et  al.,  2019).  Specifically,  Sandvik  et  al.  (2024) argue  that  creating critical mass through ecosystem resources and orchestrating ecosystem dynamics are essential dimensions for overcoming barriers to commercializing  disruptive  innovations.  These  firms  face  acute  competitive pressures  stemming  from  limited  access  to  advanced  technologies, constrained  financial  resources,  and  underdeveloped  knowledge  infrastructures,  yet  simultaneously  encounter  unprecedented  opportunities  arising  from  rapidly  growing  domestic  markets  and  increasing global integration (Kumar  et al., 2020). Within this ecosystem perspective,  emerging  market  manufacturing  SMEs  must  perform distinct market-shaping roles, ranging from market drivers proposing new visions to market missionaries building collaborative networks, that require capabilities for discovering value potential and mobilizing resources  across  the  broader  market  system  (Flaig & Ottosson,  2022; Nenonen et al., 2019; Thomson et al., 2022). Theoretically, the dynamic capabilities perspective emphasizes that emerging market SMEs must develop  superior  sensing,  seizing,  and  transforming  capabilities  to navigate environmental turbulence and resource constraints effectively (Teece, 2007), while the knowledge-based view posits that knowledge creation and integration capabilities become particularly critical when firms cannot rely on abundant financial or technological resources to drive  innovation  (Grant,  1996).  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities become especially salient in this ecosystem context, as they enhance firms'  exploring  and  experimenting  capabilities,  enabling  systematic probing of market receptiveness, recognition of system-wide resource linkages, and engagement of diverse stakeholders through data-driven insights (Nenonen et al., 2019). Empirical evidence demonstrates that emerging  market  manufacturing  SMEs  achieve  innovation  success through different pathways than developed market firms, with studies revealing that external knowledge absorption, entrepreneurial orientation, and adaptive capabilities play disproportionately important roles in  resource-constrained  contexts  (For ´ es & Camis ´ on,  2016;  Le  et  al., 2024).  However,  these  firms  simultaneously  face  strategic  dilemmas regarding  knowledge  sharing  and  protection,  where  collaborative innovation activities necessary for capability development create significant knowledge leakage risks that can undermine competitive advantages  (Ritala  et  al.,  2015).  Understanding  how  emerging  market manufacturing SMEs can leverage AI-enabled knowledge capabilities to overcome traditional resource constraints while managing knowledge governance challenges and effectively engaging with market ecosystems represents a critical theoretical and practical imperative, as these firms increasingly  constitute  the  backbone  of  economic  development  yet struggle  disproportionately  to  translate  knowledge  assets  into  new product  performance  outcomes  that  ensure  survival  and  growth  in intensifying global competition.

## 2.2. AI-enabled knowledge capabilities

Following Jensen et al.'s (2007) seminal distinction between ScienceTechnology-Innovation (STI) and Doing-Using-Interacting (DUI) modes of innovation, knowledge capabilities encompass both explicit, codified knowledge  typical  of  STI  modes  (patents,  formal  R & D,  scientific expertise) and tacit, experience-based knowledge characteristic of DUI modes (learning-by-doing, problem-solving, interactive learning). Building  on  foundational  knowledge  management  research,  scholars have distinguished between different types of knowledge accumulation capabilities, internal knowledge creation and external knowledge absorption, that differentially impact incremental versus radical innovation performance (For ´ es & Camis ´ on, 2016; Su et al., 2013).

AI-enabled knowledge capabilities represent an organization's ability to  integrate AI technologies with traditional knowledge management processes to generate, assimilate, and apply knowledge for enhanced operational and strategic outcomes. Building on Singh et al.'s (2025) conceptualization  of  knowledge  capabilities  as  encompassing  knowledge creation, infrastructure, transmission,  and management culture, and Abou-Foul et al.'s (2023) AI capabilities framework, this study defines AI-enabled knowledge capabilities as an organization's ability to systematically collect, process, and apply AI-driven insights to enhance both explicit knowledge codification (STI mode) and tacit knowledge development (DUI mode) for decision-making processes, customer understanding, and innovation activities (Abou-Foul et al., 2023).

Critically, AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities encompass  three interconnected dimensions: (1) AI-driven data acquisition and processing capabilities that enable real-time capture and analysis of production, market, and customer information; (2) AI-facilitated knowledge integration capabilities that synthesize disparate knowledge sources across organizational boundaries; and (3) AI-augmented knowledge application capabilities that translate insights into actionable innovation activities (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021; Wamba et al., 2017). Specifically, AI technologies  enable  manufacturing  SMEs  to  codify  tacit  production knowledge  into  explicit  algorithms  (converting  DUI  to  STI),  while simultaneously  facilitating interactive learning  through  intelligent feedback  systems  (enhancing  DUI  modes)  (Braganza  et  al.,  2021; Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). Moreover, AI platforms enable diverse user interactions and role adoptions that shape knowledge creation processes (Wei & Pardo, 2022), while AI-enabled servitization facilitates execution-based services, expertise-driven roles, and hyperpersonalization (Wahid et al., 2025).

The  theoretical  foundation  for  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities draws  from  the  dynamic  capabilities  perspective  (Teece,  2007)  and knowledge-based view (Grant, 1996), which suggest that organizations must  continuously  reconfigure  their  knowledge  assets  to  maintain competitive advantage. The integration of AI technologies with knowledge capabilities creates a dynamic capability that enables firms to sense market  opportunities, seize knowledge-intensive innovations,  and transform organizational learning processes (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). This alignment supports Singh et al.'s (2025) assertion that knowledge capabilities serve as gateways to essential insights and best practices for informed decision-making.

Empirical evidence suggests that AI-enabled knowledge capabilities positively  influence  new  product  performance  through  enhanced knowledge  processing  and  application  capabilities.  Recent  research demonstrates that AI finds applications across idea generation, concept creation and testing, building robust business cases, and product design, engineering, development, and testing, with these applications showing significant  benefits  including  more effective and productive voice-ofcustomer studies (Cooper, 2024). Research also demonstrates that AIenabled data mining and analytics capabilities improve product innovation  processes  (Abou-Foul  et  al.,  2023),  while  machine  learning models optimize pricing and demand forecasting accuracy (Wirtz et al., 2019). Furthermore, AI-enabled knowledge capabilities facilitate digital product  innovation  radicalness  by  enabling  firms  to  develop  breakthrough  innovations  through  advanced  data  analytics  and  predictive capabilities (Sj ¨ odin et al., 2021). The relationship between AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities  and  digital  sustainability  leadership  is  supported  by  AI's  capacity  to  optimize  resource  allocation  and  identify sustainable practices throughout the value chain (Vinuesa et al., 2020), enabling firms to achieve both innovation and sustainability objectives simultaneously.

## 2.3. Digital product innovation radicalness

Following Garcia and Calantone's (2002) foundational typology of innovation  radicalness as  a  continuum  from  incremental  innovations (minor  improvements  to  existing  products)  to  radical  innovations (fundamental changes requiring new knowledge and competencies), this study adopts Pesch et al.'s (2021) conceptualization of digital product innovation radicalness as the extent to which digital products represent breakthrough innovations that deliver completely new benefits to customers  and  make  existing  technologies  obsolete.  Importantly,  digital products in manufacturing contexts refer to physical products enhanced with digital capabilities (smart sensors, connected machinery, intelligent components) rather than purely digital offerings, representing what Porter and Heppelmann (2014) term ' smart, connected products ' that bridge physical and digital realms. This conceptualization aligns with emerging research on AI-enabled product-service systems, where digital components enable continuous value creation through data-driven services, predictive maintenance, and performance optimization (Kohtam ¨ aki et al., 2025; Paschou et al., 2020). The theoretical framework  differs  from  Christensen's  (2015) disruptive  innovation  framework,  which  focuses  on  market  displacement  patterns;  instead,  this study examines technological discontinuity and customer value creation dimensions (Gatignon et al., 2002), specifically focusing on the degree to  which  new  products  incorporate  digital  technologies,  including embedded  sensors,  IoT  connectivity,  data  analytics  capabilities,  and smart functionalities, to create offerings that significantly depart from existing  market  solutions  in  terms  of  technological  newness  and customer benefit novelty (Nambisan et al., 2017).

## 2.4. Digital sustainability leadership

Building on Parida et al.'s (2019) conceptualization of digital sustainability  as  the  integration  of  digital  technologies  to  achieve  environmental, social, and economic  sustainability outcomes, digital sustainability  leadership  represents  the  strategic  capability  to  guide organizations  in  leveraging  digital  innovations  for  sustainable  value creation (Wang et al., 2025). Specifically, digital sustainability leadership encompasses an organization's strategic orientation toward integrating digital technologies with sustainability goals to create long-term value  for  stakeholders  while  addressing  environmental  and  social challenges. Unlike generic leadership forms, digital sustainability leadership uniquely combines digital competence with sustainability vision, enabling  leaders  to  navigate  the  complex  trilemma  of  technological advancement,  environmental  stewardship,  and  economic  viability (Bouncken et al., 2021). In the context of emerging market manufacturing SMEs, digital sustainability leadership becomes particularly  critical  as  these  firms  face  intensifying  pressures  from  global supply chains, regulatory frameworks, and conscious consumers demanding sustainable practices (Fernando et al., 2019), while simultaneously pursuing digital transformation initiatives. This dual imperative creates opportunities for synergistic value creation, where digital technologies enable resource optimization, waste reduction, and circular economy practices, while sustainability commitments drive innovation in digital product design and business model transformation (Del Giudice et al., 2021). Digital sustainability leadership serves both as a direct driver of new product performance, by aligning innovations with market demands for sustainable solutions, and as a boundary condition that shapes how digital product innovation radicalness translates into performance outcomes, suggesting potential moderated mediation effects in the AI-knowledge-performance relationship.

## 2.5. New product performance

New product performance constitutes a multidimensional construct encompassing the achievement of financial, market, and strategic objectives through successful product launches, including revenue growth, market  share  expansion,  profitability  improvements,  and  customer satisfaction outcomes (Story et al., 2015). The theoretical significance of new product performance extends beyond immediate financial returns to  represent  a  firm's  capacity  to  leverage  innovation  capabilities  for sustained competitive advantage, particularly in resource-constrained emerging market contexts where innovation success becomes a critical determinant of organizational survival and growth (Kumar et al., 2020). Importantly, emerging markets are increasingly recognized as malleable, dynamic value systems that evolve through ecosystems of actors who exchange critical resources and knowledge, rather than static competitive  arenas  (Nenonen  et  al.,  2019).  In  these  contexts,  new product performance reflects not only individual firm outcomes but also the firm's ability to engage in market-shaping activities - purposive actions to re-design exchange relationships, re-configure network structures, and enhance value creation and realization for multiple stakeholders within the market system (Flaig & Ottosson, 2022; Nenonen  et  al.,  2019).  Manufacturing  SMEs  in  emerging  markets  must navigate  diverse  actor  roles  within  their  ecosystems,  from  market drivers who propose innovation visions and commit resources, to market catalyzers  who  set  standards,  to  market  missionaries  who  foster collaborative  networks,  each  contributing  distinctly  to  new  product commercialization success (Flaig & Ottosson, 2022).

Empirically, research demonstrates that new product performance outcomes  vary  substantially  based  on  knowledge  integration  mechanisms (De Luca & Atuahene-Gima, 2007), innovation radicalness (Chang et  al.,  2014),  and  organizational  capabilities  that  enable  effective translation of knowledge  assets into market-relevant innovations (Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018). From an ecosystem perspective, successful commercialization requires alignment between technological capabilities, ecosystem configuration maturity, and business model design that incentivizes  desired  collaborative  behaviors  across  network  partners (Thomson et al., 2022). Firms must develop capabilities to manage not only their own resources but also system-wide resources, making access to  distributed  knowledge  and  complementary  assets  as  critical  as V.H. Dinh resource ownership (Nenonen et al., 2019). However, the literature reveals  inconsistent  findings  regarding  the  antecedents  and  mediating pathways through which firms achieve superior new product performance, with some studies identifying direct effects of knowledge capabilities (MacCurtain et al., 2010) while others emphasize the necessity of mediating mechanisms such as absorptive capacity and market orientation  (Najafi-Tavani  et  al.,  2016).  In  manufacturing  SME  contexts, where  competitive  pressures  intensify  and  resource  constraints  limit innovation experimentation (Adomako et al., 2021), understanding the precise mechanisms through which AI-enabled capabilities and digital innovation strategies translate into superior new product performance outcomes represents a critical theoretical and practical imperative for advancing  innovation  management  scholarship.  This  understanding must  account  for  how  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities  facilitate market-shaping by enabling firms to discover value potential through exploring system-wide resources and experimenting with market propositions, while mobilizing resources through expressing compelling visions  and  engaging  ecosystem  actors  in  collective  value  creation (Nenonen et al., 2019; Thomson et al., 2022).

## 3. Conceptual background and hypothesis development

## 3.1. Study 1: thematic analysis qualitative study to develop the conceptual model

This research employs a sequential mixed-methods design combining qualitative exploration with quantitative validation to comprehensively examine AI-enabled knowledge capabilities in emerging market manufacturing SMEs (Creswell & Clark, 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2016). Given the limited understanding of how AI-enabled knowledge capabilities manifest and operate in resource-constrained emerging market contexts, Study 1 employs qualitative thematic analysis to explore how these capabilities emerge in practice, their connections to digital innovation processes, and their influence on performance outcomes (Edmondson & Mcmanus, 2007; Gioia et al., 2013). Through systematic analysis  of  managerial  experiences,  this  exploratory  phase  develops propositions that form the foundation for our conceptual model. Subsequently, Study 2 employs large-scale survey research with complementary analytical techniques to empirically validate these propositions.  This  sequential  integration  of  qualitative  depth  and quantitative  breadth  ensures  both  contextual  richness  and  statistical generalizability (Venkatesh et al., 2016).

## 3.1.1. Research method

We  conducted  comprehensive  semi-structured interviews with sixteen manufacturing SME managers to investigate the impacts of potential factors when investigating the correlation between AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities  and  new  product  performance  in  emerging market manufacturing SMEs. The qualitative study assists us in formulating the conceptual model in the first stage. While interview methodologies  present  certain  constraints  including  potential  interviewer bias, they enable focused exploration of complex organizational phenomena and capture participants' direct experiences with emerging AI technologies  in  manufacturing  contexts  (Gioia  et  al.,  2013).  Our sequential mixed-methods design adheres to established principles for methodological  alignment  in  organizational  studies  (Edmondson & Mcmanus, 2007), where theoretical domain maturity informs methodological selection. Given that research on AI-enabled knowledge capabilities in emerging market SMEs represents an intermediate theoretical domain  with  evolving  explanations  and  developing  frameworks,  a mixed-methods  approach  offers  suitable  methodological  alignment (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017).

Our  purposive sampling  approach ensured participants could contribute  meaningful  insights  addressing  our  research  questions regarding AI integration in manufacturing SME operations. We implemented  a  criterion-based  sampling  strategy  with  clearly  articulated inclusion  parameters  to  secure  a  representative  sample  (Ngo  et  al., 2020). Participants were chosen according to four specific criteria: (1) employment in manufacturing SMEs for minimum 10 years, ensuring adequate  industry  familiarity;  (2)  direct  engagement  in  AI  implementation initiatives or digital innovation projects; (3) representation across various organizational levels and manufacturing sectors to capture  diverse  viewpoints;  and  (4)  experience  with  technology-enabled business  practices  as  confirmed  through  initial  screening  interviews. We employed stratified purposive sampling to ensure balanced representation across gender, experience levels, and functional roles within manufacturing and technology domains (Morse, 2015).

The interviewed participants included nine males and seven females, with mean age of 41.3 years. Their average manufacturing experience was 13.7 years, with 87% having worked in SME environments for over 12 years. Among these participants, six held managerial positions, seven were  in  operational  roles,  and  three  occupied  specialized  technical functions related to AI or digital innovation. All participants reported involvement in technology adoption initiatives, with varying AI exposure  ranging  from  basic  implementations  to  advanced  deployment projects.  Interviewees  represented  diverse  functional  areas  including operations  management,  product  development,  technology  implementation, and strategic planning, capturing varied perspectives on how AI-enabled knowledge capabilities manifest across different SME contexts. Manufacturing sectors represented included electronics assembly (Interviewees 1, 9), textile production (Interviewees 2, 10), automotive parts (Interviewees 3, 11), food processing (Interviewees 4, 12), chemical  manufacturing  (Interviewees  5,  13),  furniture  production  (Interviewees 6, 14), machinery manufacturing (Interviewees 7, 15), and plastic products (Interviewee 8, 16).

The  semi-structured  interview  protocol  contained  six  open-ended questions  exploring  experiences  with  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities,  digital  innovation  processes,  sustainability  leadership  practices, and  perceived  performance  impacts.  Our  interview  framework  was systematically  developed  following  established  qualitative  research standards  (Creswell & Poth,  2016).  Each  35 -45  min  interview  was conducted individually via video conference, audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim. Participants received a research summary report as acknowledgment for their participation.

## 3.1.2. Data analysis

We  analyzed  data  employing  thematic  analysis  methodologies (Braun & Clarke, 2006), advancing from initial coding through pattern coding to theoretical coding, and utilized member validation and analyst triangulation to strengthen credibility. Our analytical process followed systematic qualitative coding procedures (Gioia et al., 2013). Initially, we  developed  a  comprehensive  coding  framework  with  operational definitions of key constructs drawn from AI capabilities and manufacturing innovation literature. During initial coding, we identified 156 first-order codes across all transcripts using NVivo 15, with each transcript independently analyzed by two researchers ( κ = 0.91, demonstrating strong inter-coder agreement). Through pattern coding, these first-order codes were integrated into 21 s-order themes via iterative researcher discussions and theoretical comparison. During theoretical coding, these themes were synthesized into four core theoretical dimensions:  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities,  digital  product  innovation radicalness, digital sustainability leadership, and new product performance.

## 3.2. Foundational theories and research framework

The  dynamic  capabilities  perspective  posits  that  firms  achieve competitive advantage through their ability to sense, seize, and transform opportunities by reconfiguring internal and external competencies in  response  to  rapidly  changing  environments  (Teece,  2007).  This theoretical framework has been particularly relevant in understanding how organizations develop and deploy capabilities that enable continuous adaptation and innovation (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). Within the context of emerging market manufacturing, the dynamic capabilities theory provides a lens to explore how firms can leverage dynamic capabilities  to  navigate  uncertainty and capitalize on technological opportunities (Peng et al., 2008).

The knowledge-based view complements the dynamic capabilities theory by emphasizing knowledge as the most strategically significant resource  for  firm  performance  and  competitive  advantage  (Grant, 1996). The knowledge-based view suggests that firms exist primarily to create, transfer, and apply knowledge, with superior knowledge integration capabilities serving as the foundation for sustained competitive advantage  (Kogut & Zander,  1992).  In  manufacturing  contexts,  the knowledge-based  view  explains  how  firms  can  transform  knowledge assets into innovative products and processes that drive performance outcomes (Chen & Huang, 2009).

The integration of AI technologies represents both a dynamic capability and a knowledge-intensive resource that enables firms to enhance their sensing, learning, and reconfiguration abilities (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). AI-enabled knowledge capabilities allow organizations to process complex information, identify patterns, and generate insights that support strategic decision-making and innovation processes (Davenport & Ronanki, 2018). These capabilities reflect the foundational logic of dynamic capabilities by enabling firms to continuously sense market opportunities and reconfigure knowledge assets in response to changing competitive conditions.

From a knowledge-based perspective, AI-enabled knowledge capabilities enhance firms' ability to create, acquire, and apply knowledge more  effectively,  thereby  improving  new  product  development  outcomes (Liu et al., 2025; Wamba et al., 2017). The integration of AI with knowledge  processes  enables  firms  to  accelerate  learning  cycles, improve decision quality, and develop more innovative products that meet evolving market demands. However, consistent with both theoretical  perspectives,  this  study  argues  that  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities must be complemented by appropriate organizational mechanisms and leadership capabilities to realize their full potential.

Building on these theoretical foundations and as illustrated in the conceptual model (Fig. 1), this research positions AI-enabled knowledge capabilities as a dynamic capability that enhances new product performance through digital product innovation radicalness. We propose that digital product innovation radicalness serves as a mediating mechanism that translates AI-enabled knowledge capabilities into superior performance outcomes. Furthermore, consistent with the contingency perspective embedded within dynamic capabilities theory, we posit that digital sustainability leadership plays a moderating role, enhancing the effectiveness of digital innovation in driving new product performance outcomes.

Sustainability  leadership  has  emerged  as  a  critical  organizational capability in contemporary business environments, yet existing research inadequately  explains  the  mechanisms  through  which  sustainability imperatives translate into tangible performance outcomes. Traditional studies often conceptualize sustainability as a compliance cost or incremental  improvement  opportunity,  overlooking  its  potential  as  a catalyst for breakthrough innovation development (Hart, 1995). This perspective  fails  to  capture  how  sustainability  challenges  can  drive fundamental reconceptualization of product design, functionality, and market positioning.

The knowledge-based view suggests that sustainability knowledge represents  a  unique  resource  that  can  create  competitive  advantages when properly embedded in innovative products (Grant, 1996), while dynamic capabilities theory indicates that sustainability leadership enables organizations to sense emerging environmental opportunities and transform them through innovation processes (Teece, 2007). However, empirical evidence from Ozdemir et al. (2024) reveals gaps in understanding how customer analytics capabilities specifically enable radical innovation outcomes, while Tao et al. (2025) found that experiential learning mechanisms can enhance innovation performance but noted that collective efficacy may weaken certain learning pathways, particularly  in  digital  product  contexts  where  technological  possibilities intersect with sustainability imperatives.

Digital sustainability leadership drives radical innovation through sustainability-imposed design constraints that necessitate breakthrough solutions rather than incremental modifications (Bag et al., 2021). Unlike traditional approaches that focus on efficiency improvements, digital  technologies  enable  entirely  new  sustainable  product  categories such as circular economy platforms, intelligent resource optimization systems,  and  sustainable  consumption  tracking  solutions  that  fundamentally  transform  user  experiences  and  market  dynamics  (Verhoef et al., 2021). The mediation process begins with sustainability leadership identifying transformative market opportunities, proceeds through radical digital innovation development that leverages sustainability as a differentiating factor, and culminates in superior performance through premium  positioning  in  emerging  markets  where  conventional  competitors face significant entry barriers due to sustainability knowledge requirements. Therefore:

H3 . :  Digital  product  innovation  radicalness  positively  mediates  the relationship between digital sustainability leadership and new product performance.

## 3.3.3. Moderating effect of digital sustainability leadership

Radical innovation outcomes face inherent uncertainty and market acceptance challenges that require organizational capabilities to realize their performance potential. Existing research demonstrates mixed results  regarding  radical  innovation  effectiveness,  with  some  studies finding positive performance impacts while others reveal implementation failures and market rejection risks (Sandberg & Aarikka-Stenroos, 2014).  This  inconsistency  suggests  that  radical  innovations  require supportive  organizational  contexts  to  translate  into  superior  performance outcomes. Zhou et al. (2005) found that market orientation enhances  radical  innovation  performance,  while  O'Connor  and  Veryzer (2001) revealed  that  organizational  context  significantly  influences radical innovation success rates.

From a dynamic capabilities perspective, organizational leadership represents  the  sensing  and  orchestrating  mechanisms  that  determine how  innovations  are  developed,  positioned,  and  commercialized  in markets (Teece, 2007). The knowledge-based view suggests that leadership capabilities influence how innovation knowledge is integrated and applied to create market value (Grant, 1996). However, traditional studies focus primarily on general leadership effects rather than examining  how  specific  leadership  orientations  interact  with  innovation characteristics to influence performance outcomes. Cui and Wu (2016) demonstrated  that  customer  knowledge  utilization  effectiveness  depends on technological capabilities, while Jin et al. (2019) found that knowledge characteristics interact with organizational factors to influence innovation outcomes.

Digital  sustainability  leadership  specifically  enhances  the  performance impact of radical digital innovations through several mechanisms that address typical radical innovation challenges. First, sustainability leadership provides legitimacy and market credibility for breakthrough innovations that might otherwise face skepticism, as sustainability imperatives  create  market  demand  for  transformative  solutions  (Hart, 1995; Sharma & Vredenburg, 1998). Research by Ozdemir et al. (2024) indicates that regulatory focus in innovation teams influences performance outcomes, suggesting that leadership orientation shapes innovation effectiveness. Second, sustainability leadership enables premium positioning strategies that justify the higher costs often associated with radical innovations, as demonstrated by Bag et al. (2021) who found that sustainable manufacturing capabilities support advanced innovation adoption. Dangelico and Pujari (2010) further showed that green product innovation strategies enhance firm performance through environmental positioning advantages.

The moderating mechanism operates through sustainability leadership's ability to identify and develop market segments that specifically value radical sustainable innovations, thereby reducing market uncertainty that typically constrains radical innovation performance (Klewitz & Hansen,  2014).  Additionally,  sustainability  leadership  facilitates stakeholder  engagement  and  partnership  development  that  supports radical  innovation  commercialization  (Nidumolu  et  al.,  2009),  while traditional leadership approaches may lack the vision and commitment necessary to overcome radical innovation barriers. However, Tao et al. (2025) noted  that  collective  organizational  factors  can  sometimes weaken innovation learning effects, and Chang et al. (2014) found that environmental dynamism can moderate knowledge integration effectiveness,  suggesting  that  sustainability  leadership  effectiveness  may depend on organizational alignment and capability complementarity. ## 4.1. Methods

## 4.1.1. Research design

This study pursues three primary research objectives: (a) to extend the  knowledge-based  view  and  dynamic  capabilities  perspective  by incorporating  AI-enabled  knowledge  capabilities  into  new  product development processes, (b) to examine how AI-enabled knowledge capabilities  and  digital  sustainability  leadership  influence  new  product performance through digital product innovation radicalness, and (c) to investigate the moderating role of digital sustainability leadership in the innovation-performance  relationship.  Through  this  investigation,  we develop novel constructs and adapt existing measurement instruments to  capture  the  unique  characteristics  of  AI  integration  in  emerging market manufacturing contexts. In the study 2, the research employs a quantitative approach using survey methodology to collect primary data from manufacturing SMEs in emerging markets that have implemented AI technologies in their product development activities. This methodological choice aligns with established practices in innovation and capabilities research, where survey-based approaches enable systematic examination  of  organizational  phenomena  across  multiple  firms  and contexts (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The focus on firms actively utilizing AI in manufacturing processes ensures that respondents possess relevant experience and knowledge to provide informed assessments of the study constructs.

## 4.2. Data collection and sampling method

This  study  collected  data  from  manufacturing  SMEs  in  Viet  Nam through the Keieijuku Vietnam Community to evaluate our conceptual model. The rationale for this approach was threefold. First, manufacturing SMEs represent a critical sector in Viet Nam's economy and provide an appropriate context for examining AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities  within  resource-constrained  environments.  Second, the Keieijuku Viet Nam Community serves as a comprehensive business network that maintains extensive connections with manufacturing firms across  Viet  Nam,  facilitating  access  to  our  target  population.  Third, Vietnam's emerging market context offers valuable insights into how firms in developing economies leverage AI technologies for competitive advantage.

The Keieijuku Viet Nam Community is an established business association that provides consulting services, networking opportunities, and  innovation  support  to  Vietnamese  enterprises.  This  organization maintains a database of over 1000 registered manufacturing SMEs and functions as a hub for business development, knowledge sharing, and technology adoption initiatives throughout Viet Nam.

We employed a time-lagged design with three data collection waves to examine the temporal relationships between our study constructs and mitigate common method bias concerns (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Timelagged  research  designs  have  been  widely  adopted  in  management research to establish temporal precedence and reduce common method variance, with prior studies demonstrating that separating independent, mediating, and dependent variable measurements across distinct time periods strengthens causal inference and minimizes same-source bias (Chang et al., 2020; Podsakoff et al., 2012). The three-month intervals between measurement waves align with established practices in digital innovation and AI capability research, where recent empirical studies have successfully employed comparable time lags to capture the effects of  AI-enabled  capabilities  and  digital  transformation  on  innovation outcomes in SME contexts (Raji et al., 2025; Roux et al., 2023; Sarfo et al., 2025). This temporal spacing is particularly appropriate given that digital technologies accelerate knowledge processing cycles in agile SME environments, while providing sufficient time for organizational processes to unfold and maintaining respondent engagement (Jansen et al., 2006;  Najafi-Tavani  et  al.,  2018).  A  comprehensive  list  of  700 manufacturing companies was extracted from the Keieijuku Viet Nam Community database based on two selection criteria: (1) firms operating for a minimum of three years to ensure adequate market experience, and (2) active implementation of digital technologies in their manufacturing processes.  These  criteria  ensured  that  participating  firms  possessed sufficient organizational maturity and technological readiness to provide meaningful insights regarding AI-enabled capabilities.

Upon receiving approval from the Keieijuku Viet Nam Community, we randomly selected 300 firms from the master list of 700 using systematic sampling, where firms were arranged according to annual revenue. This sampling approach enhanced representativeness by ensuring coverage across different firm sizes and revenue levels. The Keieijuku Viet Nam Community facilitated access to their member database and provided  endorsement  for  the  research  project,  which  significantly improved response rates among participating firms.

The survey instrument was administered in Vietnamese to ensure clear  communication  with  respondents.  An  academic  fluent  in  both English and Vietnamese developed the initial questionnaire in English, which was subsequently translated into Vietnamese by the same scholar. A  second  bilingual  academic  then  back-translated  the  Vietnamese version into English. We compared both English versions to preserve the original  meaning  of  each  question  and  ensure  translation  accuracy (Brislin, 1970).

Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews and online surveys, implemented by a professional research agency experienced  in  business-to-business  research.  This  mixed-method  approach was selected for two reasons. Face-to-face interviews enhance response quality  and  reduce  misunderstandings,  particularly  important  when collecting data from busy executives. Online surveys provide flexibility for  respondents  who  prefer  digital  completion  methods,  thereby increasing overall participation rates.

The time-lagged design involved three distinct phases. During the first  wave  (Time  1),  participants  evaluated  their  firms'  AI-enabled knowledge capabilities and provided demographic information including age, gender, education, and job position. In the second wave (Time 2), conducted 3 months later, participants assessed digital product innovation radicalness and digital sustainability leadership initiatives. The third wave (Time 3), conducted 3 months after the second wave, captured new product performance outcomes and additional firm characteristics.  This  temporal  sequencing  follows  theoretical  logic wherein  knowledge  capabilities precede innovation  development, which subsequently influences performance outcomes, consistent with dynamic  capabilities  theory  and  previous  longitudinal  studies  examining  capability-innovation-performance  relationships  (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Teece, 2007; Wilden et al., 2013).

At the beginning of each survey wave, participants were informed about the study's academic purpose, the voluntary nature of participation,  and  assurances  regarding  anonymity  and  confidentiality  of  responses.  The  data  collection  procedures  received  approval  from  the Keieijuku  Viet  Nam  Community,  with  oversight  provided  by  the research team throughout the process.

The  author  coordinated  with  target  respondents  during  business networking events organized by the Keieijuku Viet Nam Community or V.H. Dinh arranged individual meetings at corporate offices. Upon  survey completion, the researcher verified completeness and requested completion  of  any  missing  items.  Unique  identification  codes  were assigned to each respondent to enable matching across the three time periods while maintaining anonymity. When target executives declined participation,  the  researcher  approached  subsequent  firms  from  the randomly selected list until reaching the target sample size.

All  constructs  were  measured  using  established  scales  from  prior literature with minor adaptations for the manufacturing SMEs context. The SRMR value (0.058) is well within acceptable limits ( < 0.08), and the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (4.144) remains reasonable for complex  models  (Byrne,  2013).  Research  in  organizational  contexts, particularly with smaller samples and complex constructs such as AIenabled capabilities in emerging markets, has documented that strict adherence to traditional thresholds may be overly restrictive  (Heene et  al.,  2011;  McNeish  et  al.,  2018).  The  model  shows  substantial improvement over the null model ( χ 2 = 4023.683), and the combination of acceptable SRMR, reasonable χ 2 /df ratio, and strong construct-level psychometric properties suggest adequate model fit for theory testing purposes (Xiao et al., 2019).

The measurement model exhibited robust psychometric properties across all constructs. As demonstrated in Table 3, all constructs showed excellent internal consistency with Cronbach's α values ranging from 0.884 to 0.927 and CR values between 0.920 and 0.940, substantially exceeding  the  recommended  0.70  threshold  (Hair  et  al.,  2019). When  combined  with adequate AVE values and distinct factor loading patterns, this supports acceptable discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). Notably, the AIenabled  knowledge  capabilities  construct  demonstrated  particularly strong psychometric properties with the highest CR (0.940) and AVE (0.658). These comprehensive reliability and validity assessments provide robust empirical support for our measurement model, establishing a solid methodological foundation for subsequent structural equation modeling procedures.

## 4.4.2. Common method bias (CMB)

To  mitigate  potential  common  method  bias  (CMB)  arising  from single-source data collection, we implemented several procedural remedies during data collection, including questionnaire anonymity, randomized item ordering, and clear instructions emphasizing no correct or incorrect responses (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We subsequently conducted Harman's single-factor test to assess CMB statistically (Podsakoff et al., 2012). The principal component analysis revealed that the first unrotated factor explained 44.79% of total variance, remaining below the critical  50%  threshold  that  would  indicate  problematic  CMB.  Additionally, the extraction of multiple factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1.0  collectively  accounted  for  substantial  variance,  with  the  largest factor not dominating the variance structure (MacKenzie & Podsakoff, 2012).  We  further  applied  the  marker  variable  technique  (Lindell & Whitney, 2001) using gender as a theoretically unrelated marker variable. Results showed a minimal common method variance (CMV) estimate  (r_m = 0.005),  with  all  CMV-adjusted  correlations  remaining statistically significant ( p < 0.001) and exhibiting negligible differences from original correlations (maximum difference = 0.003), confirming that CMB did not substantially inflate observed relationships (Williams et  al.,  2010).  These  procedural  and  statistical  safeguards  collectively suggest that CMB is unlikely to substantially bias our structural model results, providing confidence in the validity of our empirical findings.

## 5. Discussion and implications

Our findings demonstrate strong alignment with prior research while revealing unique contextual insights for emerging market manufacturing SMEs. The direct positive effect of AI-enabled knowledge capabilities on new product performance (H1) corroborates findings by Adomako et al. (2021) and Najafi-Tavani et al. (2018). Theoretically, this extends the knowledge-based view (Grant, 1996) by demonstrating that AI integration transforms traditional knowledge  capabilities through enhanced pattern recognition that transcends human cognitive limitations (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). This aligns with extant evidence showing AI revolutionizes business landscape with 13 -15 year adoption windows peaking before decade's end, enabling applications across new product development target areas with significant benefits for better solutions (Cooper, 2024), though the urgency to embrace AI becomes evident  given  SMEs'  modest  readiness-to-adopt  despite  immense  potential for propelling businesses into enhanced productivity, efficiency, and innovation futures (Cooper, 2025). The relationship strength exceeds traditional knowledge management studies (De Luca & AtuaheneGima, 2007), suggesting AI enables emerging market SMEs to overcome institutional voids through automated knowledge processing. Qualitative findings provide contextual explanation, with one manager noting: ' AI gives us 70% accuracy in seasonal demand prediction ' (I3).  Unlike developed  markets,  emerging  market  SMEs  face  acute  resource  constraints  that  make  AI-enabled  automation  particularly  valuable  for competitive knowledge utilization (Kumar et al., 2020).

The mediating role of digital product innovation radicalness (H2) aligns with De Luca and Atuahene-Gima's (2007) knowledge integration mechanisms. This  supports  the  dynamic  capabilities  perspective  that knowledge reconfiguration must result in novel market positions. This finding  resonates  with  recent  research  demonstrating  that  service innovation dimensions mediate generative AI capabilities-competitive advantage relationships (Li et al., 2025), while AI-enabled information systems improve decision-making performance that fosters innovation when  strategic  agility  moderates  at  moderate  levels.  Unlike  studies emphasizing incremental innovation (Chang et al., 2014), our findings highlight radical innovation's importance where market volatility creates  leapfrogging  opportunities (Sj ¨ odin et al.,  2021). Qualitative  evidence shows participants distinguishing between incremental improvements and breakthrough innovations: ' AI knowledge enabled our first  smart  textile  innovation ' (I6).  Resource  constraints  drive  radical innovation  effectiveness,  as  emerging  market  SMEs  must  pursue breakthrough innovations for differentiation (Le et al., 2024).

Digital sustainability leadership's mediation (H3) represents a novel contribution, extending Hart's (1995) natural-resource-based view by demonstrating that sustainability imperatives drive rather than constrain  innovation  through  design  constraints  necessitating  breakthrough solutions (Bag et al., 2021), challenging traditional compliancecost  perspectives  and  enabling  access  to  developed  market  segments demanding sustainable solutions (Dangelico & Pujari, 2010). ## 5.2. Practical implications

This research provides several actionable insights for manufacturing SME  managers,  policymakers,  and  technology  providers  seeking  to leverage AI-enabled knowledge capabilities for enhanced new product performance in emerging market contexts.

Initially, while manufacturing SMEs increasingly recognize the potential of digital technologies for innovation, many lack clear guidance on how AI-enabled knowledge capabilities specifically contribute to new product success. Our findings demonstrate that these capabilities function  as  strategic  enablers  rather  than  mere  technological  additions. Manufacturing SME managers should conceptualize AI-enabled knowledge capabilities as integrated organizational competencies that enhance market sensing, customer understanding, and innovation processes rather than standalone IT implementations.

Managers  should  prioritize  developing  comprehensive  AI-driven knowledge systems that facilitate data collection, pattern recognition, and insight generation across product development cycles. This involves cultivating  capabilities  to  transform  market  intelligence,  customer feedback, and technological trends into actionable innovation strategies. Manufacturing SMEs pursuing competitive advantage should embed AIenabled knowledge capabilities throughout their innovation workflows, utilizing these capabilities to anticipate market shifts, identify unmet customer needs, and accelerate product development timelines. Specifically, managers should implement three concrete actions: (1) establish AI-powered customer analytics platforms that integrate real-time feedback from multiple touchpoints (e.g., social media, after-sales service, sales channels) to identify emerging needs and predict market trends; (2)  deploy  machine  learning  algorithms  for  competitive  intelligence analysis that continuously monitor competitor product launches, pricing strategies, and technological innovations to inform differentiation strategies; and (3) create cross-functional AI taskforces comprising R & D, marketing, and operations personnel who meet bi-weekly to translate AI-generated insights into specific  product features and development priorities.

Subsequently,  the  results  emphasize  the  critical  importance  of fostering digital product innovation radicalness as a mediating mechanism  between  AI  capabilities  and  performance  outcomes.  Managers must  ensure  that  AI-generated  insights  translate  into  breakthrough product  innovations  rather  than  incremental  improvements.  This  requires establishing innovation processes that encourage experimentation, challenge conventional approaches, and pursue disruptive opportunities. Manufacturing SMEs should develop organizational cultures  that  embrace  radical  thinking  while  maintaining  operational efficiency.

Practical  implementation  involves  creating  dedicated  innovation V.H. Dinh teams that combine AI expertise with domain knowledge, establishing innovation labs for prototype development, and implementing stagegate processes that evaluate both technological feasibility and market disruption potential. Without this systematic approach to radical innovation, firms risk underutilizing their AI investments and missing opportunities  for  significant  market  differentiation.  To  operationalize radical innovation, managers should: (1) allocate a minimum of 15 -20% of  R & D  budgets  specifically  to  AI-enabled  exploratory  projects  that challenge  existing  product  architectures,  following  the  ambidextrous innovation  model  that  balances  exploitation  and  exploration;  (2) implement ' innovation sprints ' lasting 8 -12 weeks where AI insights drive  rapid  prototyping  of  radical  product  concepts  with  predefined success metrics (for example, novelty scores, potential market disruption indices); and (3) establish partnerships with AI technology startups and university research centers to access cutting-edge algorithms and methodologies that can inspire breakthrough innovations beyond internal capabilities.

Furthermore, the findings regarding digital sustainability leadership offer crucial strategic guidance. Manufacturing SME managers should integrate sustainability considerations into their AI-driven innovation strategies from the outset rather than treating them as compliance requirements. This involves developing leadership capabilities that align environmental  responsibility  with  technological  advancement  and market performance.

Managers  should  establish  cross-functional  sustainability  committees  that  guide  AI-enabled  innovation  processes,  ensuring  that  new product  development  considers  environmental  impact,  resource  efficiency, and social responsibility. This may require investing in sustainability  analytics  tools,  establishing  partnerships  with  environmental technology providers, and developing metrics that balance economic performance with sustainability outcomes. Concretely, executives should: (1) deploy AI-powered lifecycle assessment (LCA) tools during the  early  design  phase  to  evaluate  and  optimize  the  environmental footprint of new products across raw material sourcing, manufacturing, use,  and  end-of-life  stages;  (2)  implement  AI-driven  supply  chain transparency systems that track and verify sustainable sourcing practices, enabling real-time monitoring of supplier environmental compliance  and  carbon  emissions;  and  (3)  develop  sustainability-linked innovation KPIs (for example, percentage reduction in material waste, energy efficiency improvements, circular economy integration) that are reviewed monthly alongside traditional financial metrics and directly inform executive compensation schemes.

Additionally, for policymakers and development organizations supporting emerging market manufacturing SMEs, this research suggests targeted  interventions  to  facilitate  AI  adoption  and  knowledge  capability development. Government agencies should develop sector-specific AI adoption  roadmaps  tailored  to  manufacturing  subsectors  (for instance, textiles, electronics, food processing) that outline technology maturity  levels,  investment  requirements,  and  expected  innovation outcomes at each stage of AI capability development (Ozdemir et al., 2024).

Policy initiatives should include: (1) creating regional AI innovation hubs that provide manufacturing SMEs with subsidized access to cloudbased  AI  platforms,  pre-trained  industry-specific  models,  and  on-site technical consultants for 6 -12 month engagement periods; (2) launching competency-based certification programs for SME employees in AI applications for product development, quality control, and sustainability management, with government co-financing of 70 -80% of training costs and industry-recognized credentials; (3) establishing ' AI sandbox ' regulatory environments where manufacturing SMEs can pilot AI-driven sustainable innovations  with  temporary  exemptions  from  certain compliance  requirements,  enabling  experimentation  while  gathering evidence for future policy design; and (4) developing public procurement  programs  that  prioritize  purchasing  from  SMEs  demonstrating advanced  AI-enabled  sustainable  product  innovations,  creating  guaranteed market demand that justifies AI investments. These interventions can accelerate the diffusion of AI-enabled knowledge capabilities across emerging market manufacturing ecosystems.

## 5.3. Limitations and directions for future research

This study presents several limitations that offer opportunities for future research. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and  future longitudinal  studies  should  examine  how  AI-enabled knowledge  capabilities  evolve  over  time  and  identify  temporal  dynamics in  capability  development  trajectories.  Future  research  could investigate threshold effects and curvilinear relationships between AI investment intensity and performance returns. Second, this study's focus on emerging market manufacturing SMEs may limit generalizability, suggesting  future  research  should  test  our  framework  across  diverse contexts,  and  examine  how  different  AI  technologies  differentially impact knowledge capabilities. Third, future studies could benefit from mixed-methods  approaches  incorporating  qualitative  case  studies  to provide  deeper  insights  into  AI  capability  deployment  mechanisms. Fourth, the AI capability measurement may not capture rapidly evolving technologies  like  generative  AI,  representing  a  significant  limitation given  transformative  potential  of  large  language  models,  indicating future research should develop more dynamic instruments. Fifth, this study did not examine inter-organizational networks, overlooking how SMEs leverage partnerships to compensate for internal capability gaps, suggesting future investigations should explore AI capabilities within broader  innovation  ecosystems  and  coopetition  dynamics.  Future research  should  investigate  the  dark  side  of  AI  adoption,  including algorithmic  bias,  privacy  risks,  and  ethical  dilemmas,  and  examine organizational  learning  culture's  role  in facilitating  AI  capability development. Finally, this study did not consider cultural or institutional moderators, representing a significant gap given that national innovation  systems substantially influence AI adoption, presenting opportunities for cross-cultural comparative research examining how national contexts shape AI adoption outcomes.
