## ABSTRACT

This study analyzes how artificial intelligence (AI) innovation affects firms' financial performance and value among S&P 500 companies in the US market, using a panel data regression model for the period 2000-2024. The study finds mixed evidence of a small positive impact of AI innovation on firms' return on assets, with the effect stronger for more R&D-  intensive firms. This study also tests whether the market considers the environmental impact of AI innovation when valuing a firm's AI innovation. The findings demonstrate that AI innovation increases firm value for companies that perform well on sustainability-  related metrics. This effect is stronger under Democratic administrations than under Republican governments, indicating the influence of political environments on the market's perception of firms' actions. The results remain robust to multiple checks, including system-  GMM and PSM. The study makes a compelling argument for incorporating sustainability as a crucial strategic component in the new AI-  driven business world, while providing insights for policymakers, executives, and investors.

## 1   |   Introduction

The global AI market, valued at USD 189 billion in 2023, is expected to increase 25-  fold over the next 10 years, reaching USD 4.8 trillion by 2033 (United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) 2025). AI is assuming increasingly  important  roles  in  business  operations  like  customer service,  inventory  management,  and  process  automation (Haan  and  Watts  2023).  AI-  based  technologies  have  helped firms enhance productivity and improve process efficiencies (Aghion et al. 2017; Agrawal et al. 2019; Czarnitzki et al. 2023; Dirican 2015). Corporate strategists view AI as a critical component of their future success, given that nearly 50% of strategic planning  and  execution  activities  are  expected  to  be  automated (Gartner Inc. 2023). Situating AI innovations in firm and industry-  specific scenarios can help firms develop a costeffective,  sustainable,  competitive  advantage  (Kemp  2024). The adoption of AI can lead to the emergence of related complementarity and substitution dynamics, rendering conventional competitive advantages obsolete (Krakowski et al. 2023). This study begins by analyzing whether AI innovation, measured as the number of AI-  related patents a firm is granted in a year, translates into profitability by improving its return on assets (RoA). Existing literature mostly focuses on how AI impacts firm-  level productivity (Damioli et al. 2021; Duan et al. 2023; Zhai and Liu 2023). Since AI innovation requires significant investments, it is necessary to analyze whether firms receive tangible  financial  benefits  from  it.  Consequently,  our  study is the first to examine the direct impact of AI innovation on firm profitability. The USA is at the forefront of AI research and innovation, making it the ideal market to base our study. Since some studies have also found that AI adoption can negatively  affect  firm  value  due  to  high  implementation  costs (Lui  et  al.  2022),  we  aim  to  investigate  whether  large  firms with sufficient buffers for AI investments can achieve better financial performance from these technologies. Hence, in the US market, we select firms in the S&P 500 as of December 31, 2024, as our sample. These are the leading 500 US public companies, collectively accounting for approximately 80% of the US equity market capitalization (S&P Global), with their market performance thoroughly monitored by investors. Our analysis finds AI innovation to enhance the RoA of a firm, the effect being stronger for firms that are more R&D (research and development) intensive, providing evidence of the financial viability of investing in AI innovation and complementary dynamic organizational  capabilities  to  properly  absorb  such innovation into firm operations.

This study then examines the role of sustainability in the age of rising AI innovation, particularly given its substantial environmental impact. The rise of AI has led to an increase in the number of data centers from 500,000 in 2012 to 8,000,000 in 2024 (United Nations Environment Programme 2024). This exponential rise comes with an estimated 120 million metric tons of ewaste by 2050 (World Economic Forum 2019), a huge strain on freshwater resources (P. Li, Yang, et al. 2023), and an expansive carbon footprint. Training an AI model can emit up to 626,000 pounds of carbon dioxide, 57 times the carbon footprint of an average human, and 5 times the emissions of an average car in its lifetime (Strubell et al. 2019). The energy consumption associated with developing AI technologies is expected to increase exponentially  as  they  become  more  commonplace.  Thus,  this study analyzes the impact of AI innovation on firm value, based on overall sustainable performance.

Focusing  on  sustainability  in  firm  operations  is  becoming a  significant  strategic  decision  for  firms,  especially  considering  the  global  policy  push  towards  sustainable  development (IPCC 2022; OECD 2012; United Nations 2015). More than 190 nations have also adopted recommendations on the ethical  and  environmentally  sustainable  application  of  AI (UNESCO 2021). It is now becoming apparent that focusing on sustainability while developing or adopting AI is not only a moral but a strategic imperative (Ren and Wierman 2024). Hence,  we  move  on  to  analyze  whether  AI  innovation  by  a sustainable  firm  is  viewed  differently  from  innovation  by  a non-  sustainable firm.

Studies  have  already  demonstrated  the  financial  benefits  of adopting sustainable business practices (Stefan and Paul 2008) and observed a positive link between sustainable performance and  firm  value  (Aydoğmuş  et  al.  2022;  Laskar  and  Gopal Maji 2018; Tahmid et al. 2022; Yu and Zhao 2015). Existing literature mostly focuses on either the environmental cost of AI (Strubell et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2023) or on the positive impact of AI in terms of resource efficiency (Li et al. 2022; J. Li, Ma, et al. 2023). Thus, we analyze if the market perception of AI innovation differs depending on the sustainable performance of the firm, making our study the first to address the relevance of firm-  level sustainability while evaluating the impact of AI innovation on firm value. AI innovation by a sustainable firm might lead investors to believe that (1) the AI-  related technology  is  being  developed  in  an  energy-  efficient  manner  or  (2) the AI technology will be utilized sustainably by the firm, reducing its carbon footprint (Hassanein et al. 2024), or (3) the firm can offset the excessive energy and resource requirement of AI innovation due to its sustainable policies. In any of these cases,  the  firm  should  be  viewed  positively  by  the  market. Using the  composite  environmental,  social,  and  governance

(ESG) and the independent environmental pillar score as our sustainability metrics, we find, in a subsample analysis, that AI  innovation  by  sustainable  firms  leads  to  an  increase  in firm value, whereas firms that fare poorly on sustainability receive no significant reward to their market value. The effect is even more pronounced in the period following the 2015 Paris Agreement, with sustainability  attracting  growing  attention worldwide.

Sustainable performance is also observed to be affected by cultural and governance-  related contexts (Hassanein and  Elmaghrabi  2025;  Hassanein  et  al.  2024;  Elmaghrabi et al. 2025). Building on this premise, we examine the relevance of sustainability in the market perception for firm-  level AI innovation in Democratic and Republican administrations due to their divergent views on sustainability (Jones 2023). The analysis further reveals that this effect is significant during the years of Democratic leadership in the United States, highlighting the difference in attitudes towards environmental issues based on political affiliation (Smith et al. 2024).

This study significantly advances the literature across several important dimensions. Empirically, it provides evidence of the benefits of investing in AI and in aligned organizational capabilities (R&D investment), thereby validating the strategic decision by firms to incur such significant expenses. AI innovation acts as a valuable, inimitable resource, providing firms with a sustained competitive advantage. This is the first study to shed light on the role of sustainability as we move towards the AI-  driven future, with empirical evidence of sustainable firms receiving improved firm valuations for their AI innovations. It focuses on market perceptions of the environmental costs associated with AI and why adopting a combination of technological  advancements  and  sustainable  growth  can  be a  rewarding strategic choice. These results align more with, and provide evidence for, the value-  enhancing theory, which posits that improved sustainable performance can help firms improve their market value (Hart and Milstein 2003), over the shareholder theory, which prioritizes profit maximization over social responsibility (Friedman 1970), enriching the theoretical contributions of the study. On the policy front, this study is also the first to examine how governmental support for sustainable  initiatives  affects  firm  value,  depending  on  policylevel  support  for  sustainable  initiatives,  aligning  the  results with the perspectives of institutional theory (North 1990). Our study has practical implications for policymakers, managers, and investors. The findings suggest that, rather than merely a moral commitment, sustainable performance is emerging as a driver of firms' market value. Business executives can strategically disclose their ESG and energy-  efficient AI initiatives as signals of long-  term quality and market resilience, thereby translating  sustainable  performance  into  tangible  financial returns  and  enduring  competitive  advantage.  Policymakers can  design  incentives  targeted  towards  sustainable  technology development, such as tax rebates and subsidies for firms with strong ESG performance, and by funding more research in  energy-  efficient  AI  and  green  computing  infrastructure (Bolón-  Canedo  et  al.  2024).  A  positive  market  perception  of sustainable firms increases the likelihood of success for such policies, thereby reducing enforcement costs. Investors can incorporate ESG-  based AI metrics into their portfolio evaluation to  make smarter, more efficient, and more profitable investment choices, helping maximize long-  term capital gains while reducing exposure to transition risk.

## 2   |   Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1   |   Artificial Intelligence and Firm Performance

The  term  'artificial  intelligence'  was  first  used  by  John McCarthy  in  a  workshop  at  Dartmouth  in  1956.  Since  the 1990s, AI has advanced at a considerable pace, reaching the current  stage  of  Artificial  General  Intelligence,  which  utilizes deep learning and Big Data analytics to handle increasingly complex problems like loan underwriting, fintech, and robo-  advising (Chen et al. 2019; D'Acunto et al. 2019; Fuster et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2006). Multiple studies have explored how different aspects of firm performance are influenced by AI. Industrial robot applications have been found to improve firm productivity significantly (Duan et al. 2023). Yang (2022) observes that AI innovation in the Taiwanese market leads to higher firm productivity, accompanied by a reduction in the labor  force  with  lower  educational  qualifications.  Increased productivity  was  also  observed  in  German  firms  after  the adoption of AI technologies (Czarnitzki et al. 2023). In a sample  of  Chinese  listed  firms,  AI  adoption  enhanced  a  firm's total factor productivity through reduced costs, more efficient labor  utilization,  and  increased  innovation,  with  the  effect more  pronounced  for  larger  firms,  state-  owned  enterprises, and labor-  intensive industries (Zhai and Liu 2023). A similar increase  in  productivity  following  AI  adoption  was  also  observed  in  small-  and  medium-  sized  enterprises  and  servicesector firms (Damioli et al. 2021). Firms already investing in synergistic technologies are observed to extract more productive use of AI after incorporating it into their business operations (Calvino and Fontanelli 2023).

On the other  hand,  certain  studies  have  also  found  firm  performance to remain unaffected by AI (Huang and Lin 2025), while some have observed AI adoption to cause a reduction in firm value (Lui et al. 2022) due to high implementation costs. AI technologies have significant potential to transform business operations, resulting in reduced costs, increased efficiency, minimized  human  errors,  and  accelerated  processes.  Despite  the well-  documented operational benefits, there is limited research examining whether AI innovation translates into firm-  level financial profitability. This leads us to our first research question, which examines whether AI innovation improves firm profitability, measured by RoA, among S&P 500 firms. Thus, our first hypothesis is as follows:

H1. AI innovation positively impacts the profitability (RoA) of S&P 500 firms .

Complementarities  research  suggests  that  technological  innovation  leads  to  enhanced  firm  performance  when  it  is combined  with  aligned  organizational  capabilities  (TorrentSellens et al. 2025a, 2023). Within the technology-organization-environment  framework  (Tornatzky  et  al.  1990),  there is  a  need  for  sufficient  internal  innovation  and  absorptive capacity to successfully incorporate AI technologies into firmlevel operations. In this study, we select R&D intensity as one such  complementary  capability.  Research  has  established the importance of R&D as a critical component of the innovation  ecosystem  of  a  firm  (Díaz-  Chao  et  al.  2021;  Camiña et al. 2020), which leads to an improvement in its technological absorption capability (Doraszelski and Jaumandreu 2013; Luintel et al. 2014). Thus, the profitability gains through AI innovation are expected to be stronger for firms with higher R&D  intensity.  This  leads  us  to  the  second  hypothesis  of our study:

H2. R&D intensity moderates the relationship between AI innovation and profitability (RoA) of S&P 500 firms .

## 2.2   |   Sustainability, Artificial Intelligence, and Firm Value

Research  interest  in  sustainability  and  sustainable  finance has been increasing rapidly (Benameur et al. 2024; Benameur et al. 2025). In the ongoing efforts to combat climate change, firms are now expected to engage more in sustainable business practices, with such performance being rewarded by the market. An improved ESG (environmental, social, and governance) score is linked to better firm value and performance (Aydoğmuş et al. 2022; Tahmid et al. 2022). In their systematic literature review on this topic, X. Li et al. (2024) find that most studies link better ESG performance to increases in a firm's market value, highlighting the rising importance of sustainable business practices in investors' and markets' eyes. Environmental and social disclosure by firms in Europe and India has been observed  to  enhance  their  valuations  and  help  them  reduce policy-  induced uncertainty (Ahsan and Qureshi 2021; Laskar and Gopal Maji 2018). Small- and medium-  sized enterprises also reported improved performance due to green innovation (Schrank and Kijkasiwat 2024). Factors like gender diversity, earnings  management, and corporate innovation also affect firm-  level  sustainable  performance  (Alazzani  et  al.  2017; Tahat and Hassanein 2024; Abdelbaky et al. 2025; Hassanein and  Elsayed  2025).  Sustainability  is  steadily  emerging  as  a major corporate strategy, with greater managerial participation in such incentives enhancing a firm's market reputation (Stefan and Paul 2008). A positive relationship, aligned with value-  enhancing theory rather than shareholder value theory, has  been  established  between  sustainable  performance  and firm value. This positive effect is stronger in countries with high disclosure levels and strong investor protection (Yu and Zhao 2015).

AI  innovation  carries  both  potential  environmental  benefits and  concerns.  The  computing  power  required  for  training, operating,  and  updating  AI  doubles  every  100 days,  with  projections  indicating  a  millionfold  surge  over  the  following  five years (Zhu et al. 2023). Tech giants like Microsoft and Google have reported that their greenhouse gas emissions have risen by 30% and 50%, respectively, over the past 4years, primarily due to the expansion of data centers to sustain their AI infrastructure  (Google  2024;  Kimball  2024).  Conversely,  AI  utilization through industrial robots has reduced carbon intensity and improved energy efficiency across multiple industrial sectors (Y. Li et al. 2022; J. Li, Ma, et al. 2023). Hussain et al. (2024) demonstrated that firms with higher AI adoption exhibit greater green innovation, with the effect more pronounced for private enterprises. AI has improved natural resource utilization efficiency (Wang et al. 2023), while facilitating the development of renewable energy across nations (Zhao et al. 2024), and helping firms achieve their sustainability-  related metrics (Torrent-  Sellens et al. 2025b). AI technology, if properly harnessed, is expected to potentially reduce 5%-10% of global greenhouse gas emissions (Kemene et al. 2024) and revolutionize the approach to sustainability in the financial sector (Hassanein and Tharwat 2024).

[S. 4]

The presence of such mixed results opens up the avenue to investigate  whether  AI  innovation  influences  firm  value  differently based on the firm's sustainability score. No existing study has explored the relevance of sustainable performance to market perceptions of a firm's AI innovation. Based on prior research, we propose that AI innovation in firms with sustainable performance should be viewed more positively by the market than innovation in firms that do not fare as well. This leads us to the third hypothesis of our study, presented below:

H3a. AI innovation leads to an increase in firm value for firms with better sustainability-  related outcomes .

H3b. AI innovation leads to a decrease/no effect in firm value for firms with poorer sustainability-  related outcomes .

## 2.3   |   Sustainability and the Political Environment

Moving forward, firm-  level  sustainable  performance is  also  significantly influenced by cultural, governance, and market-  related contexts (Hassanein and Elmaghrabi 2025; Hassanein et al. 2024; Elmaghrabi  et  al.  2025).  Institutional  theory  (North  1990)  also suggests that the institutional environment (in the form of regulations, laws, and policies) influences firm outcomes and market valuations. This makes it reasonable to assume that market reactions to sustainability may differ depending on the ruling dispensation's viewpoint. Consequently, we expect political affiliation to significantly influence how the market perceives a firm's sustainability actions, particularly environmental sustainability. There has been an increasing divide between Democratic and Republican viewpoints  concerning  sustainability  in  the  United  States.  Since  the 1990s,  Republicans  have  given  lower  priority  to  environmental issues than Democrats (E. K. Smith et al. 2024). 78% of Democrats prioritize the environment over economic growth, while only 20% of Republicans share this sentiment (Jones 2023). The percentage of Democratic voters who believe the top priority for a President should be 'Protecting the Environment,' 'Global Warming,' and 'Climate  Change'  is  85%,  56%,  and  78%,  respectively.  The  corresponding figures for the Republican voters are 39%, 16%, and 21%, respectively (Kennedy and Johnson 2020). Acknowledging the distinct partisan disparity in matters related to environmental sustainability, we are the first study to investigate whether AI innovation in sustainable firms is evaluated positively by the financial  market  during  the  more  sustainability-  oriented  Democratic administrations compared to Republican administrations. Hence, we formulate the following fourth hypothesis:

H4a. AI innovation leads to an increase in firm value for firms with  better  sustainability-  related  outcomes  during  Democratic administrations .

H4b. AI  innovation  does  not  significantly  affect  firm  value for firms with better sustainability-  related  outcomes  during Republican administrations .

## 3   |   Data and Methodology

## 3.1   |   Dependent Variables-Return on Assets (RoA) and Tobin's Q

This study examines how AI innovation affects the performance and value of S&P 500 firms, utilizing annual data from 2000 to 2024. The S&P 500 comprises the 500 largest publicly traded firms in  the  US  equity  market,  accounting  for  approximately  80%  of the total US market capitalization. It is considered one of the best indicators of the US financial market and broader global market trends. These firms also lead in innovation in their respective sectors, with substantial investments in research and development. S&P 500 firms also have approximately 80% institutional ownership (Greenspon 2019). Institutional investors are considered more capable monitors of firm performance (Shleifer and Vishny 1986) and can ensure higher disclosure quality (Haq et al. 2024). Thus, the market valuation effects for these firms would be most measurable and meaningful. All these factors make them an ideal sample to determine whether AI innovation enhances their financial performance and firm value. Following prior literature, we have excluded financial sector firms from our study due to differences in their capital structure, accounting practices, and regulatory requirements compared to other firms (Poulsen et al. 2013; Tahat and Hassanein 2024). The dependent variable selected for the first hypothesis is return on assets (RoA). RoA is useful for understanding how efficiently a firm utilizes its resources to generate profits. It is also a widely accepted measure of firm financial performance (Coles and Li 2023; Dang et al. 2018; Mehran 1995). ## 3.2   |   Explanatory Variable-AI Patents

Following  prior literature,  we  measure  AI  innovation  by the  number  of  AI-  related  patents  granted  to  S&P  500  firms (Ménière 2022; Parteka and Kordalska 2023; Tseng and Ting  2013;  USPTO  2020;  Y.  Wang  and  Liu  2025;  Zhai  and Liu  2023).  Patents  are  considered  a  reliable  proxy  for  AI  innovation since firms are highly incentivized to patent any AI technology  developed  by  them  to  prevent  imitation  and  build a  competitive  advantage  (Yang  2022).  We  utilize  the  methodology  outlined  in  the  PATENTSCOPE  Artificial  Intelligence Index to identify such patents. This index utilizes a combination of keywords, Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC), and International  Patent  Classification  (IPC)  symbols  to  identify patents related to AI technology. These patents cover a broad spectrum of AI techniques (machine learning, neural networks, deep learning, etc.), functional applications (augmented reality, predictive analytics, natural language processing, etc.), and application  fields  (business,  biomechanics,  telecommunications, etc.). The full list of keywords and patent classification symbols used  is  publicly  available  on  the  World  Intellectual  Property Organization (WIPO) website. The AI-  patent database is then filtered using the firms' common names to identify patent ownership for S&P 500 firms.

## 3.3   |   Other Variables

## 3.3.1   |   Control Variables

Control variables at the firm level include-firm size (natural logarithm of total assets), cash holdings (cash and equivalents divided by total assets), leverage (total debt divided by total assets),  profitability  (operating  income  divided  by  total  assets), R&D intensity (research and development expenses divided by total assets), return on equity, and analyst following (count of analysts covering the firm). The lagged values of dependent variables are also included in the model. The data for these variables is sourced from the LSEG Workspace.

## 3.3.2 | Sustainability-  Related Variables

Sustainability filters include the composite ESG score as well as the separate Environmental pillar score for each firm. This data is sourced from the LSEG Workspace. The earliest available annual ESG data for our sample dates back to 2000, so we have selected the period from 2000 to 2024 for our study.

## 3.3.3 | Dummy Variables

We also incorporate certain dummy variables in our analysis. A COVID dummy that takes a value of 1 for 2020-2022, when the pandemic was prevalent globally, and 0 otherwise. Similarly, there are dummy variables for the dot-  com bubble bust and the Great Recession, which take the value 1 for the years 2000-2002 and 2008-2009, respectively. Investigating the influence of different political administrations on firm value, the sample will be analyzed under both Democratic and Republican presidencies in a subsample analysis. The Democratic presidencies include the periods of 2000 (under President Bill Clinton), 2009-2016 (under President Barack Obama), and 2021-2024 (under President Joe Biden). Similarly, the Republican presidencies include the periods of 2001-2008 (under President George W. Bush) and 20172020 (under President Donald Trump). This impact is analyzed in three ways: (1) on the entire sample; (2) on a subsample with ESG score > 50; and (3) on a subsample with ESG score ≤ 50. Since we have focused on the environmental impact of AI innovation, we also use the environmental pillar score (EPS) of the ESG data as a subsampling criterion. ESG scores below 50 indicate poor performance, while scores above 50 are considered satisfactory. LSEG uses a percentile-  ranked system for its ESG scoring, with a score of 50 indicating that the firm has performed better than 50% of its peers in the same industry, making 50 a reasonable threshold for separating more sustainable firms from less sustainable ones (LSEG 2024). Furthermore, the median values for the ESG and EPS scores in our sample are 55.04 and 49.52, respectively, and Table 1 shows that the mean values  are  also  around  50,  providing  additional  justification for choosing 50 as the cutoff value. It might also not be  prudent  to  expect  a  similar  market  perception  regarding sustainability throughout the entire sample. Media attention to  sustainability  has  increased  significantly  since  the  2015 Paris  Agreement,  in  turn  increasing  the  focus  and  discussion on corporate sustainability (Zheng et al. 2024). Figure 1 shows the number of AI-  related patents granted to the S&P 500 firms over the years. The patent count is also significantly higher  after  2015. Tobin's Q value rises by 0.0005 with each additional AI patent. While the individual impact may seem small, its economic magnitude can be assessed by considering a realistic change in AI innovation. An increase in AI patents by one standard deviation (115.66 from Table 1) leads to the Tobin's Q value of the firm rising by 0.058, translating to a 3% increase in firm value compared to the sample mean of 1.89. This positive effect is, however, weakly significant at just the 10% level. Huge investment costs may have led investors to have a negative perception of the firm's value. In Columns (3) and (5) of Table 6b, AI innovation in firms with better sustainable performance leads to a 6% relative increase in Tobin's Q for  AI patents increasing by one standard deviation, with the effect being significant at 5% for the ESG score parameter and at 1% for the EPS parameter.

## 4.3   |   Impact of Political Administration

For the fourth hypothesis, we aim to investigate whether market perceptions of AI innovation and sustainability differ across political  administrations.  Similar  to  the  third  hypothesis,  we run the regressions for the three time periods, but only for the subsample with better sustainability performance. Tables 7a and 7b display the findings. The positive results in Table 7a, with a  1%  level  of  significance,  show  that  firms  with  higher  ESG and EPS scores exhibit an approximate 8% relative increase in firm value for each standard deviation increase in AI patents during  Democratic  regimes.  This  aligns  with  the  notion  that Democratic policies and their supporters place a high degree of importance on environmental issues. In Table 7b, it is noted that AI innovation has an insignificant impact on firm value during Republican  administrations.  This  result  is  understandable, given that the United States announced its exit from the Paris Agreement when the Republican administration took office in 2017 and later rejoined in 2021, only after a Democratic administration was elected. The negative impact during 2000-2015 is consistent with our results in Table 6a. These results align with our fourth hypothesis that AI innovation in a sustainable firm is viewed more favorably by the market when the administration is  more  supportive  of  sustainability  as  a  principle.  This  result can be understood from the perspective of institutional theory (North 1990). Institutions, defined as the 'formal and informal rules  of  the  game,'  influence  firm  outcomes  and  market  valuations. Managers also seek legitimacy by aligning firm decisions with existing institutional environments (Suchman 1995). Institutional  investors  also  drive  a  firm's  sustainable  performance when these issues find support among the community (Dyck et al. 2019). Hence, stronger institutional alignment with sustainability  in  Democratic  regimes,  through  proactive  environmental regulations and green incentives, could improve the legitimacy of sustainable firms and enhance market perception of their AI innovation.

## 5   |   Robustness Check and Further Analysis

## 5.1   |   Endogeneity

Our model creates a dynamic panel data set by using lagged values of the dependent variables, which can introduce endogeneity in the sample. Thus, our dynamic panel regression uses SystemGMM (generalized method of moments) estimators to address these  concerns  (Blundell  and  Bond  1998).  Clustered  standard errors are employed in the two-  step GMM model to address concerns about heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. The results are shown in Table 8, with Columns (1)-(8) displaying the main results of interest in our study for the entire sample period. AI innovation  remains  statistically  significant,  aligning  with  our main regression results and indicating that our findings are robust to potential concerns about endogeneity and heteroskedasticity. AI innovation improves firm performance and enhances firm value when coupled with sustainable business practices.

## 5.2   |   Propensity Score Matching

As  a  robustness  check  to  support  our  panel  regression  and GMM estimates, we use propensity score matching (PSM). This technique alleviates concerns about non-  random selection into treatment by making treated and control firms observationally similar  on  important  firm-  level  covariates.  We  define  treated firms as those in the top quartile for the number of AI patents, signifying high innovation intensity, while the remaining firms form the control group, and then calculate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). Table 9 reports the results of this analysis. Panel A focuses on the treatment effect on our main outcome variables, while Panel B analyzes the covariate balancing after matching. The mean bias is significantly reduced, the pseudoR 2  remains below 0.05, and the percentage of covariates with variance ratios between 0.5 and 2 remains above 60% for all our scenarios. This indicates that the matching of treated and control  groups  has  been  successful.  The  results  from  Panel  A demonstrate that the RoA of high AI innovation intensity firms is higher by 0.5%, significant at the 10% level. This is in line with H1, albeit at a lower significance level. Hence, we obtain mixed results concerning AI innovation and its effect on firm financial performance. As far as firm value is concerned, firms with the most AI patents tend to have a Tobin's Q premium of approximately 0.15, which is significant at the 5% level. This premium increases to 0.27 and 0.23 for firms having ESG and the environmental pillar scores above 50. This again underscores that firm value is enhanced by sustainable performance. The premium in firm value remains significant for firms with an environmental pillar score above 50 under both Democratic and Republican governments; however, the magnitude of the difference is approximately twice as large under Democratic regimes. For firms with an ESG score greater than 50, the significant positive difference in firm value is significant only during Democratic administrations.  These  results  provide  empirical  support  for  all our proposed hypotheses.

While we have employed GMM and PSM in our study, residual endogeneity concerns remain. Since firms with higher market capitalizations may have sufficient capital and technological capabilities  to  invest  in  AI  innovation,  which  can  influence  the AI-  performance relationship, reverse causality cannot be fully ruled  out.  Time-  varying  omitted  variables,  such  as  shifts  in managerial strategy or firm-  level risk tolerance, can influence both AI innovation and market outcomes and cannot be fully resolved with fixed effects. PSM cannot account for unobservable differences between high and low-  innovation firms, leading to residual selection bias. We acknowledge these residual concerns as limitations of our study which can be improved upon in future research.

## 5.4   |   Generative vs. Non-  Generative AI Innovation

Generative AI is gaining popularity and business interest with the release  of  technologies  such  as  DALL-  E,  Midjourney,  ChatGPT, and DeepSeek. It is a subcategory of AI that analyzes underlying patterns in existing data using advanced machine learning models to produce new content, such as images, text, or videos. It now accounts for more than 20% of all AI investment (Maslej et al. 2025). It  is  an  extremely energy-  intensive process, with a ChatGPT request  using  about  10  times  as  much  energy  as  a  simple  Google search (Strubell et al. 2019). Consequently, it carries a higher environmental burden in the form of increased energy demand and a larger carbon footprint (Crawford 2024; Ding et al. 2025; Bashir et al. 2024). This makes it interesting to conduct a heterogeneity analysis to assess whether there are differences in how generative AI patents affect firm performance and market value compared with other non-  generative AI patents. Generative AI patents are identified  as  per  the  keywords,  CPC,  and  IPC  codes  provided by  the  World  Intellectual  Property  Organization  (WIPO).  Nongenerative AI patents are calculated as the difference between total AI patents and the generative AI patents. Contemporary innovation in generative AI began after the introduction of Generative Adversarial Networks in 2014 (World Intellectual Property Organization  2024).  Hence,  we  conduct  a  comparative  analysis of generative AI vs. non-  generative AI innovation for the period 2015-2024 to maintain parity in observations. The results of our analysis are displayed in Tables 11a and 11b. Here, we observe that while the patents alone were unable to impact firm profitability, companies with higher R&D intensity noticed a highly significant impact of AI innovation on their RoA. The impact on firm value remains mostly consistent with the results of our main analysis for  both generative and non-  generative AI innovation. The only difference is that generative AI innovation by firms with an ESG score lower than 50 has a weakly significant but negative impact on firm value.

## 6   |   Contribution, Implications, Limitations, and Future Scope

Our study significantly contributes to research on the interaction  among  sustainability,  technology,  and  firm  performance. AI is growing at a breakneck pace, entering almost every sphere of  corporate  activity.  All  major  businesses  are  trying  to  capitalize on the potential of AI innovation to revolutionize their operations.  We  selected  AI  innovation  (proxied  by  AI-  related patents) as our primary explanatory variable because the development of proprietary, in-  house technology will be a critical factor for firms seeking to develop a strategic advantage over their competitors moving forward. Our study first provides evidence that investing in AI innovation can yield tangible profitability for firms, with more R&D-  intensive firms observing better results. While this result focuses on why firms opt for AI innovation, it is equally important to examine how their actions affect the environment around them and whether the market places any emphasis on the same. AI innovation, in all its stages (development, training, and deployment), is an energy-  intensive process with an enormous carbon footprint. Hence, discussing the role of sustainability in AI innovation is paramount. This study is the first to analyze how the market perceives AI innovation by sustainable firms compared to other firms. The results demonstrate that AI innovation, coupled with sustainable performance, increases firm value. However, poor sustainability performers are not  penalized in terms of firm value; instead, better performers tend to receive higher market valuations as AI innovation increases.  The  results  remain  significant  even  after  applying multiple robustness checks, including a two-  step system GMM, PSM, and an alternative analysis using interaction terms and the logarithm of AI patents as independent variables. Our study also observes the impact of the political environment on this market perception. The rise in firm value is significant only during Democratic  governments  in  the  United  States,  aligning  with institutional and legitimacy theories that highlight how the institutional policy environment can shape market perceptions of partisan topics.

The study has theoretical implications across different strategic frameworks. From the perspective of the resource-  based view (Barney 1991), firms can generate a sustained competitive advantage  through  AI  patents  as  they  represent  an  inimitable, rare, and valuable organizational resource, reflected here as a positive impact on firm profitability. The dynamic capabilities framework posits that sustaining such a competitive advantage depends on developing internal technological processes within the  firm  (Teece  et  al.  1997),  as  evidenced  by  R&D  intensity moderating the impact of AI innovation on firm profitability. The  positive  effect  of  AI  innovation  on  the  value  of  sustainable  firms  aligns  with  the  value-  enhancing  theory  (Hart  and Milstein  2003),  supporting  the  argument  that  financial  markets reward more socially responsible firms (Malik 2015). This paper  has  implications  for  different  stakeholders.  Managers and executives can encourage AI innovation, coupled with an increase  in  complementary  R&D  intensity,  to  effectively  integrate new technologies and improve the financial performance of their firms. To manage AI investments and firm sustainability  goals,  they  should  integrate  sustainability  metrics  in  their AI innovation process, like using AI to reduce a firm's carbon footprint,  or  developing  AI  with  energy-  efficient  models  and less computationally-  intensive algorithms (Bolón-  Canedo et al. 2024), or incorporating more renewable energy sources in their energy mix. Disclosure of such green AI innovation practices  in  their  annual  reports  can  strengthen  market  signaling and enhance firm value. Since financial markets value AI innovation by sustainable firms, policymakers can encourage sustainable innovation by providing targeted incentives, such as tax breaks and subsidies, linked to measurable green AI innovation

Investors  can  incorporate  sustainability-  related AI innovation indicators into their portfolio screening models as a signal of market value. Long-  term investors can sustain investment in AI innovation by sustainable firms as a value-  creating strategy, since the effects of political cycles can average out over a longer investment horizon (Starks et al. 2026). Short-  term investors can actively adjust portfolio allocation for such firms depending on the political regime.

However,  this  study  is  not  without  its  limitations.  Firstly,  it focuses solely on the US market. While the United States is a leader  in  AI  research  and  development,  countries  like  China are also emerging as a hotbed of AI innovation. The scope of the analysis is also limited to S&P 500 firms, which can restrict the  generalizability  of  the  results.  The  results  may  be  strong for large, resource-  rich firms with existing technological capabilities and may not apply to smaller firms or non-  US markets. Future studies can conduct a multi-  country analysis to bring out a more comprehensive impact of AI innovation on firm value. They can also incorporate more firms in their sample and perform a heterogeneity analysis across industries, market capitalizations, and other firm characteristics. Using AI patents as an innovation proxy also has its limitations. Patents capture invention capacity more than adoption intensity, and the propensity to patent also varies across firms. Alternative proxies like R&D investments and AI adoption intensity (Babina et al. 2024) can be used to complement the AI patent count to achieve a more comprehensive measure of AI innovation. Future research can also  conduct  a  comparative  study  between  AI  innovators  and AI adopters. This may help shed light on how the market perceives sustainable performance in relation to active AI innovation and passive AI adoption. Investor attention could also be included as an additional explanatory variable to test if the impact of AI innovation and sustainability on firm value differs depending on the attention it receives by the investor community. Comprehensive  studies  on  the  topic  can  help  different  stakeholders adopt sustainability as a core company strategy, rather than just an obligatory afterthought.

## 7   |   Conclusion

We analyzed the effect of AI innovation on firm financial performance  and  firm  value.  The  results  suggest  AI  innovation to  be  a  financially  profitable  endeavor  for  firms,  with  firms investing in complementary capabilities, via a higher R&D intensity, being better positioned to translate this innovation into increased  profitability.  The  results  also  provide  evidence  that the market rewards AI innovation when coupled with sustainable  performance,  especially  post  the  2015  Paris  Agreement, highlighting the increasing relevance of corporate sustainability  among  investors.  The  broader  institutional  environment also plays an important role in this scenario, with the increase in firm value being consistently significant during Democratic governments in the United States, which is perceived as more pro-  environment in its policymaking decisions.

Hence, our study has important implications for multiple groups, including investors, policymakers, and firm executives. With AI

set to become an indispensable part of business processes at a large scale, this paper makes a strong case for adopting sustainability as a fundamental pillar of corporate strategy as they invest in such technologies. It shows that sustainability and firm growth can co-  exist, and firms do not necessarily have to choose between them.
