## A B S T R A C T

This  study  examines  how  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  adoption  is  associated  with  corporate  investment efficiency among U.S. firms. We measure AI adoption using AI mentions , defined as the proportion of AI-related terminology in senior management remarks during earnings calls, and validate this measure by showing that it is positively associated with 10-K discussions of AI applications  in  operational  decision-making,  product  development,  and  business  expansion.  We construct an instrumental variable based on exposure to university-authored AI research -measured as a normalized score of textual similarity between non-corporate academic publications and industry descriptions -to address concerns that AI adoption may be endogenous to firms ' investment decisions and investment efficiency. Our baseline results indicate that higher AI adoption is associated with greater investment efficiency, and this association remains in twostage least squares regressions using the AI technology exposure instrument. We also find that firms with higher AI mentions tend to have higher Tobin ' s q over longer horizons, consistent with a  gradual  reflection  of  AI-related  benefits  in  firm  value.  Additional  analyses  suggest  several mechanisms: AI adoption is associated with more accurate management sales forecasts, higher financial reporting quality, and greater process and product innovation intensity. Cross-sectional evidence further indicates that regulatory frictions, such as exposure to data-privacy regulation, attenuate the association between AI adoption and investment efficiency. Overall, the findings suggest that AI adoption is an emerging factor in corporate investment and capital allocation.

Empirical evidence shows that AI-based tools can support data-driven decision-making and generate systematic corporate policy recommendations (Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016; Campello et al., 2025). At the same time, studies document that firms investing in AI or related general-purpose digital technologies affect core business and investment policies rather than only support functions (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Bloom et al., 2021; Fedyk et al., 2022; Babina et al., 2024a). However, it is not clear ex ante whether these technologies lead to measurably more efficient investment within firms.

A large literature emphasizes that information quality and managerial forecasting ability are important determinants of investment efficiency. Classic theories link information asymmetry and agency conflicts to deviations from optimal investment, while richer information environments help align financing and investment with underlying fundamentals (Jensen and William, 1976; Myers and Nicholas, 1984; Biddle et al., 2009). Empirical work further shows that managers with stronger forecasting and planning capabilities undertake investments and acquisitions closer to value-maximizing benchmarks, exhibiting more efficient capital expenditures and higher-quality deals (Goodman et al., 2014; Tanaka et al., 2020). Complementing this evidence, Gondhi (2023) shows, within a rational inattention framework, that managers ' limited information-processing capacity and attention allocation lead to resource misallocation and lower aggregate output, even when decisions are otherwise rational. Against this backdrop, AI can be viewed as a technology that can improve investment efficiency by enhancing managerial forecasting, supporting financial reporting quality, and expanding firms ' capacity to discover and evaluate innovative projects.

Realizing these potential benefits, however, is neither immediate nor costless. Effective use of AI typically requires complementary investments in data infrastructure, human capital, and organizational redesign, and these intangible outlays can be sizable and frontloaded (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). Moreover, the diffusion of disruptive technologies such as AI is gradual and uneven across regions, firms, and occupations, and complementary capabilities and adoption costs shape when and where AI gains materialize (Bloom et al., 2021). AI systems can also introduce new sources of risk -including opacity, overfitting, and sensitivity to data quality -that may lead to suboptimal portfolio choices or regulatory concerns in financial applications (D ' Acunto et al., 2019; Campello et al., 2025). Experimental evidence further shows that AI agents can generate hallucinations, agency-like problems, or costly ' agreement spirals ' when tools and oversight are imperfect, highlighting additional operational risks from AI deployment (Canayaz, 2025). In addition, AI-related standards and governance can amplify or dampen AI investment and capital budgeting, and AI adoption has been associated with higher systematic risk and changes in firms ' cost of capital; long-run effects appear to depend on how well AI is integrated with existing human and organizational capabilities (Babina et al., 2024b; Cao et al., 2024; Canayaz and Wang, 2025). In this setting, AI-related risks, adjustment costs, and implementation frictions represent costs that must be weighed against the potential gains from improved information processing and prediction.

Taken together, AI adoption may improve investment efficiency by enhancing information and decision-making, but it may also introduce new risks and require sizable complementary investments. Whether AI adoption is associated with higher investment efficiency is therefore an empirical question. Using a sample of 16,145 firm-year observations for 2401 U.S. firms from the StreetEvents -Compustat -CRSP universe over 2010 -2021, we examine how firms ' AI adoption relates to investment efficiency.

To measure AI adoption, we construct a firm-year measure based on the frequency of AI-related terminology in earnings call transcripts, serving as a proxy for senior executives ' stated strategic focus on AI. Specifically, we define AI mentions as the ratio of AIrelated keyword counts (Appendix C) in senior management remarks and responses during earnings calls to the total number of words in the corresponding transcripts. Because there is no widely accepted, comprehensive AI keyword dictionary, we develop our own using  ChatGPT-assisted  term  generation  and  manual  textual  analysis,  following  keyword-discovery  and  dictionary-construction methodologies in recent work (Sautner et al., 2023; Eisfeldt et al., 2026). This measure allows us to capture two aspects of firms ' use of AI in investment decisions: the extensive and intensive margins of AI applications. The extensive margin involves expanding activities, discovering new opportunities, or increasing the pool of options available to a firm, whereas the intensive margin reflects the extent to which AI technologies may help firms select better projects from their existing opportunity set. 1 We validate the measure by examining its association with AI-related discussions in 10-K filings. AI mentions is positively correlated with discussions of AI applications aimed at enhancing decision-making in existing operations and expanding investment opportunities. It is also significantly associated with whether firms describe intensive (process -focused) and extensive (opportunity -expanding) AI benefits in their 10-K disclosures, suggesting that it captures meaningful variation in AI adoption related to investment decision-making.

Our main analysis indicates that AI adoption is positively associated with greater investment efficiency, measured as firms ' deviations from expected investment given their growth opportunities, after controlling for observable firm characteristics. A onestandard-deviation increase in AI mentions is associated with a 2.17% (3.65%) increase in investment efficiency at the mean (median). Given the potential for endogeneity in AI adoption, we estimate a two-stage least squares (2SLS) specification that uses exposure to university-authored AI research -measured by textual similarity between academic AI publications and industry descriptions -as an instrumental variable for AI mentions , in the spirit of Chen and Srinivasan (2024). This instrument is strongly correlated with firms ' propensity to discuss AI, satisfying the relevance condition. Because we filter out all corporate coauthorships, the instrument captures knowledge spillovers arising exclusively from academic research rather than from firm-specific investment decisions, thereby mitigating -though not eliminating -concerns about violations of the exclusion restriction. Because AI adoption appears to be associated with improved capital allocation, we examine whether its benefits are reflected in firm performance. We find that firms with higher AI mentions tend to have higher Tobin ' s q over one- to three-year horizons, consistent with gradual increases in firm value as firms adjust and invest in complementary assets. Analyses of stock returns indicate generally positive, but smaller and less consistent, associations with AI mentions ,  suggesting that equity markets may incorporate expected benefits relatively quickly while valuation effects become more pronounced over time.

Next, we examine potential mechanisms through which AI adoption relates to investment efficiency. First, we study whether AI adoption is associated with more accurate management sales forecasts. AI may enhance managerial prediction by enabling analysis of large-scale datasets beyond traditional human judgment, helping firms better align investment with growth opportunities and reduce costly misallocations. In line with this view, we find that higher AI mentions is associated with significantly lower management sales forecast errors over multi-year horizons. Additional tests suggest that the mechanism operates primarily through managers ' internal use of AI rather than through improvements in analyst forecast accuracy or reductions in firm risk.

Second, we examine whether AI adoption improves the quality of external financial reporting by constraining earnings management. To the extent that AI-enabled systems increase the internal visibility of accounting irregularities, they may raise the expected cost of earnings management and thereby reduce information asymmetry between managers and outside investors. Consistent with this view, we find that higher AI mentions is associated with lower real and accrual-based earnings management. These results suggest that AI adoption may contribute to higher investment efficiency in part through improved financial reporting quality, even though its effects on conventional operating efficiency measures appear limited in our setting.

Third, we analyze patent activity as an additional mechanism. AI may lower the cost of evaluating and screening new projects, enabling firms to allocate capital toward higher-quality opportunities. We find that higher AI mentions is associated with greater intensity of patenting activity, measured as process and product patents scaled by sales, suggesting that AI helps managers identify and develop efficiency-enhancing process improvements and new products, potentially contributing to more effective capital allocation over time.

We conduct several robustness tests to validate our findings. First, our subsample analyses of early and later AI adoption periods provide limited evidence that the positive association between AI adoption and investment efficiency is concentrated among early adopters. Second, we use the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as a regulatory shock and find that the positive association between AI mentions and investment efficiency weakens for firms more exposed to the European Union (EU) after its adoption. Third, we address concerns about time-varying, industry-level shocks by using industry-year fixed effects rather than year fixed effects and find that our main inferences remain similar. Fourth, we decompose AI-related terminology into five subfields and find that discussions of computing and hardware infrastructure, and to a lesser extent data and analytics tools in the initial period, are more strongly associated with investment efficiency than other AI subfields. Fifth, we decompose investment into capex and non-capex components and find that AI mentions is particularly associated with improved efficiency in R & D-related investment, consistent with AI facilitating the allocation of innovative capital over time. Sixth, we distinguish between underinvestment and overinvestment and find that AI adoption tends to improve investment efficiency for firms prone to underinvestment. Lastly, we use the share of AI specialists in the high-skilled workforce as an alternative proxy for AI adoption and obtain results that are broadly consistent with our baseline.

Our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it extends research on the diffusion of new technologies and their effects on economic growth and firm valuation (Bartel et al., 2007; Comin and Mestieri, 2014; Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016). Recent work provides further evidence on how AI investments and exposure affect firm value, systematic risk exposure, workforce outcomes, and AI-based prediction of corporate performance (Babina et al., 2024a; 2024b; Campello et al., 2025; Eisfeldt et al., 2026). Our research examines the role of AI in corporate decision-making by analyzing how AI technologies may help firms identify new projects and allocate capital more efficiently within their existing opportunity sets.

Second, our study contributes to the literature by examining how AI may help address constraints that distort the investment process, such as information asymmetry and limited managerial capacity (Harris and Raviv, 1996; Jagannathan et al., 2016). Our analysis suggests that AI adoption is associated with improved investment decision-making, as managers may use AI-based analytical tools to assist in selecting and valuing investment projects. By quantifying AI adoption and its association with investment efficiency, we provide evidence on an important performance metric for corporate stakeholders.

Finally, our study contributes to research on how new technologies transform human decision-making in specific contexts, such as judicial bail decisions (Kleinberg et al., 2018), robo-advising for retail investors in India (D ' Acunto et al., 2019), auditing processes (Fedyk et al., 2022), and algorithmic underwriting (Jansen et al., 2025). In contrast to these studies, which focus on discrete decision problems in law, personal finance, auditing, or consumer credit, we investigate how AI adoption influences corporate investment decision-making and its broader associations with capital allocation and firm performance. We also highlight AI ' s potential to complement, rather than replace, human judgment in high-skill occupations, as evidenced by research on AI-based tools that enhance financial  decision-making  and  productivity  (Grennan  and  Michaely,  2021;  Noy  and  Zhang,  2023).  Our  findings  suggest  that  AI adoption is associated with more accurate management forecasts and more efficient investment decisions.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis development

## 2.1. AI adoption by firms

AI adoption has become more common across industries, prompting growing scholarly inquiry into its effects on firm performance, labor markets, decision-making, and sector-specific outcomes. Studies document that AI and related digital tools can affect operational efficiency and competitive positioning, including in financial services (D ' Acunto et al., 2019; Abis and Veldkamp, 2024). For example, D ' Acunto et al. (2019) examine how a robo-advisory optimizer changes portfolio diversification, risk-taking, and behavioral biases among retail investors at a large Indian broker, while Abis and Veldkamp (2024) analyze how AI-driven data analysis reshapes information production and business models in investment management and other data-intensive services. Babina et al. (2024a) show that firms with greater AI investment experience faster growth in sales, employment, and market value, primarily through increased product innovation rather than pure process-cost reductions. A growing body of research finds that decision quality can improve when algorithmic or AI-based tools complement human judgment, especially in complex information environments (Kleinberg et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2024). In the venture capital context, Lyonnet and Stern (2025) show that combining human expertise with algorithmic analytics is associated with more accurate market assessments and greater innovation among portfolio firms. AI adoption is also associated with changes in labor market dynamics, shifting the capital-labor mix and reallocating demand toward certain skilled occupations while displacing tasks in others (Abis and Veldkamp, 2024; Eisfeldt et al., 2026). The need for new skill sets and organizational adaptation thus increases as firms broaden their use of AI. Taken together, these studies suggest that AI adoption is associated with improved decision quality and performance, motivating our focus on investment efficiency.

## 2.2. AI adoption and investment efficiency

Accurate forecasting plays an important role in shaping firms ' real investment decisions. Improved prediction can reduce financing frictions and agency-driven deviations from value-maximizing investment by alleviating information asymmetry (Jensen and William, 1976; Myers and Nicholas, 1984). While higher forecast accuracy is associated with more efficient investment outcomes (Goodman et al., 2014; Tanaka et al., 2020), limited managerial information-processing capacity and attention allocation can contribute to resource misallocation even when choices are otherwise rational (Gondhi, 2023). AI adoption may directly address these informational and cognitive frictions: recent advances in machine learning and natural language processing enable firms to analyze large-scale datasets and improve forecasting accuracy in ways that go beyond traditional human judgment (Kleinberg et al., 2018; Campello et al., 2025), and algorithmic models have been shown to outperform human decision-makers in complex prediction tasks (Kleinberg et al., 2018; Jansen et al., 2025), suggesting that AI-supported managerial forecasting may meaningfully reduce the information gaps that lead to over- or underinvestment.

AI adoption may also improve investment efficiency through the quality of external financial reporting. High-quality financial reporting reduces information asymmetry between managers and outside investors, enabling efficient capital allocation (Richardson, 2006; Biddle et al., 2009). However, reporting quality can be undermined by earnings management, whereby managers exercise discretion over accruals or real activities to misrepresent firm performance (Cohen et al., 2008; Cohen and Zarowin, 2010). AI-enabled systems may constrain such manipulation by automating transaction monitoring and anomaly detection across large volumes of financial data, raising the visibility of accounting irregularities and the expected cost of opportunistic manipulation. Consistent with this view, Fedyk et al. (2022) find that AI adoption in audit firms reduces the incidence of restatements related to accruals and revenue recognition. To the extent that AI adoption reduces earnings management and improves reporting quality, it may further support investment efficiency.

AI adoption may further foster innovation in both product development and production processes, which can directly shape firms ' investment opportunities and capital allocation decisions (Babina et al., 2024a). Cockburn et al. (2018) argue that AI serves as a general-purpose method of invention, enabling firms to leverage large datasets and learning algorithms to open up new approaches to R & D and accelerate the discovery of commercially viable projects. In particular, AI may help managers assess new projects more effectively  and  screen  potential  investments,  reducing  the  cost  of  experimentation  and  enabling  firms  to  commit  capital  to higher-quality opportunities. Process innovations, which involve new methods of production, can facilitate adjustments in firms ' input mix and protect firm value (Bena et al., 2022), while product innovations expand firms ' investment opportunity set. To the extent that AI adoption promotes both types of patenting activity, it may further contribute to more effective capital allocation.

At the same time, these potential benefits are accompanied by nontrivial costs. Regulatory limits on data access can weaken predictive performance, and adopting AI systems often requires substantial investments in staff training, data infrastructure, and process changes (Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016; Brynjolfsson et al., 2021; Aridor et al., 2023). AI models may also introduce risks, including overfitting, opacity, and sensitivity to poor-quality or incomplete data, which can in turn create scope for erroneous decisions or regulatory concerns (D ' Acunto et al., 2019; Campello et al., 2025; Canayaz, 2025; Canayaz and Wang, 2025). Investments in AI are further associated with higher exposure to systematic risk, which can raise firms ' cost of capital (Babina et al., 2024b), and realizing long-term gains may depend on complementary investments (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Fedyk et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2024). As a result, the gains from AI adoption may be partly offset by regulatory, organizational, and model-related frictions.

In sum, AI adoption may improve investment efficiency through improved forecast accuracy, higher financial reporting quality, and greater innovation activity. However, implementation costs, regulatory constraints, and modeling risks may limit or counteract these benefits. Thus, the overall effect of AI adoption on investment efficiency is an empirical question.

Hypothesis (stated as the null): Firms with higher levels of AI adoption do not differ in investment efficiency compared to other firms.

## 3. Data and variable definitions

## 3.1. Sample

Our sample spans the period from 2010 to 2021, during which demand for AI-skilled jobs increased significantly (Babina et al., 2024a). Table 1 summarizes the sample construction. We begin with 72,124 firm-year observations and 630,468 quarterly earnings call transcripts from the Refinitiv StreetEvents database, including non-U.S. firms. We then exclude 38,594 firm-year observations not found in either Compustat or CRSP, 7027 firm-year observations in financial industries (Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes 6000 -6999), and 992 firm-year observations in utility industries (SIC codes 4900 -4999), because investment for regulated firms differs systematically from that of other firms (Biddle et al., 2009). Following Acemoglu et al. (2022) and Babina et al. (2024a), we also exclude 4587 firm-year observations in AI-innovating industries (North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes 51 and 54) to focus on firms that primarily use AI rather than develop AI technologies. Finally, we exclude 4779 firm-year observations with missing values for key variables. After applying these criteria, our final sample consists of 2401 unique U.S. firms, yielding 16,145 firm-year observations in the merged StreetEvents -Compustat -CRSP dataset. 2

## 3.2. Measure of AI adoption: AI mentions in earnings calls

Our main proxy for firm-level AI adoption is constructed from earnings conference call transcripts and is based on executives ' statements.  We  analyze  both  management  presentations  and  question-and-answer  interactions  with  analysts,  investors,  and  the media. 3 The text is pre-processed using standard natural language processing techniques, including tokenization and lowercasing, to handle variation in word forms across calls. We identify sentences from quarterly calls that contain AI-related keywords, initially defined using the dictionary developed by Babina et al. (2024a). We then prompt ChatGPT, a large language model instructed to act as an AI expert, to answer three questions based on excerpts from these transcripts. The first question classifies whether and how a firm discusses AI involvement -distinguishing among active application, investment, strategic focus, and acquisitions. In our sample of 2401 firms, 305 (12.70%) mention AI applications during earnings calls. Follow-up questions elicit brief descriptions of the types, purposes, and strategic areas of AI use. Fig. 1 presents word clouds for three benchmark years -2012, 2016, and 2020 -showing changes in AI-related terms over time, with a broader range of keywords appearing in more recent years. Appendix B describes this procedure in detail.

Using these outputs, we compile and filter AI-related keywords, drawing on multiple sources including internet searches and technical glossaries (Appendix C). We then define AI mentions as the ratio of AI-related keyword counts used by a firm ' s  senior management team to the total number of words in the transcripts. This measure is intended to capture the intensity with which top managers discuss AI applications, and we interpret it as potentially reflecting management ' s strategic emphasis on and commitment to AI. To assess classification accuracy, we manually review a subset of transcripts. Finally, we compute each firm ' s annual average AI mentions from the quarterly observations.

To relate our AI vocabulary to standard terminology, we benchmark the Appendix C dictionary against glossaries from AI textbooks (e.g., Russell and Norvig, 2024) and technical standards. This reference vocabulary allows us to assess how closely managerial language  aligns  with  recognized  AI  terminology.  The  analysis  indicates  that  executives ' language  is  concentrated  in  applied domains -such as machine learning, deep learning, computer vision, natural language processing, and predictive analytics -while many specialized technical topics in the reference glossaries (for example, specific learning algorithms or automata theory) do not appear in earnings calls. 4 This pattern is consistent with evidence that firms and standard-setting bodies emphasize applied AI concepts rather than the full range of technical vocabulary (Canayaz and Wang, 2025).

## 3.3.2. Intensive and extensive AI application indicators in 10-K filings

We further estimate ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions to provide additional validation for AI mentions . Specifically, we analyze Item 1 of the 10-K, which describes a company ' s business and key strategic activities and is widely used in the literature to study firms ' operations and competitive positioning (Hoberg and Phillips, 2024). Our approach is based on the premise that firms that meaningfully adopt AI technologies are more likely to discuss these technologies and their strategic implications in Item 1.

Using a methodology consistent with that applied to conference calls (Appendix B), we identify passages that contain AI-related terminology and are explicitly linked to operational or strategic applications. We construct two indicator variables to distinguish between the extensive and intensive margins of AI-related efficiency gains in investment decision-making. Intensive AI benefits equals one if the 10-K mentions AI-related keywords or phrases describing how AI technologies assist firms in improving decision-making for existing  operations,  and  zero  otherwise.  Examples  include  improving  pricing  strategies,  enhancing  inventory  management,  and implementing continuous product evaluation. Extensive AI benefits equals one if the 10-K mentions AI-related keywords or phrases describing how AI technologies help firms expand investment opportunities, and zero otherwise. Examples include launching new products, expanding market reach, and identifying strategic partnerships.

Appendix F presents the regression results. The dependent variables are Intensive AI benefits in column (1) and Extensive AI benefits in column (2). In addition to firm-level characteristics, we control for Log (1 + 10-K size) to account for the effect of report length on the likelihood of discussing AI applications. The coefficients on AI mentions are positive and significant at the 1% level, indicating that firms with higher AI mentions are more likely to discuss both intensive and extensive AI benefits. For example, a one-standard-deviation increase in AI mentions of 0.137 is associated with a 1.51 percentage point (1.30 percentage point) higher probability of discussing AI applications with intensive (extensive) benefits. These changes correspond to 18.57% (16.39%) of unconditional probabilities, suggesting economically meaningful associations.

Taken together, the validation results indicate that AI mentions is associated with AI applications across various business operations, including those connected to investment decisions. This supports our interpretation of AI mentions as a proxy for AI adoption, capturing both the expansion of investment opportunities and changes in project selection.

## 4. AI adoption, investment efficiency, and firm value

## 4.1. Measure of investment efficiency

We measure investment efficiency following Biddle et al. (2009), a specification widely used in empirical studies of corporate investment decisions (e.g., Cheng et al., 2013; García Lara et al., 2016; Harford et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2021; Chay et al., 2023). In this framework, investment inefficiency is the deviation of a firm ' s actual investment from its predicted level, based on industry-year sales growth. For each industry-year group, defined using the Fama French 48 industry classification and requiring at least 20 observations, we estimate Investment = β 0 + β 1 Sales growthi, t-1 + ε it , where i denotes the firm and t the year. Following Biddle et al. (2009), we define investment as the sum of capital expenditures, R & D expenses, and acquisition expenditures, scaled by total assets. 6 This definition is consistent  with  subsequent  investment  efficiency  research,  captures  key  components  of  firms ' growth-related  expenditures,  and preserves the interpretation of residual deviations from predicted investment as over- or underinvestment. Sales growth is the firm ' s prior-year sales growth rate. The residuals ε it capture deviations from the expected investment level implied by growth opportunities: positive  residuals  indicate  higher-than-expected  investment  (potential  overinvestment),  whereas  negative  residuals  indicate lower-than-expected investment (potential underinvestment). To obtain an overall measure of investment efficiency, we take the absolute  value  of  the  residuals  and  multiply  by 1  for  interpretability,  so  that  higher  values  correspond  to  greater  investment efficiency.

Unobservable firm characteristics could jointly affect investment efficiency and managers ' emphasis on AI in earnings calls. To address this concern, we employ an instrumental variables (IV) approach. In the spirit of Chen and Srinivasan (2024), we construct an instrumental variable for AI mentions based on textual similarity between non-corporate academic AI publications and detailed 2017 NAICS industry descriptions. 8 Specifically, we measure AI technology exposure as the normalized score derived from this similarity, using time-varying academic publications and fixed industry descriptions, to capture the extent to which each industry is exposed to developments in AI research. The relevance condition relies on the idea that the AI exposure score -derived from the volume and content of academic AI research linked to each industry -predicts firm-level AI adoption and managers ' articulation of AI as a strategic priority. This association is estimated in the first-stage  regression.  Because  the  instrument  is  constructed  exclusively  from  academic  publications  authored  by  university researchers -with all corporate coauthorships filtered out -it captures knowledge spillovers that are external to the corporate sector. This non-corporate origin is central to the exclusion argument: since the underlying research is produced by academics with no direct stake in firm-level investment decisions, the instrument reflects technological supply conditions in the broader research environment rather  than  demand-side  factors  specific  to  individual  firms.  Accordingly,  the  instrument  is  unlikely  to  influence  unobserved firm -specific shocks to investment efficiency other than through firms ' AI -related activities, supporting the exclusion restriction. While these design choices help mitigate common threats to IV identification, the exclusion restriction is fundamentally untestable and may not hold under certain conditions. 9 We therefore interpret the 2SLS estimates as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, the OLS and mechanism analyses, and view them as part of a broader body of evidence rather than as standalone causal estimates.

Panel B of Table 3 presents the first- and second-stage results of the 2SLS estimation. In the first stage, AI technology exposure is positively and significantly associated with AI mentions at the 1% level, and the Cragg and Donald (1993) F -statistic of 118.83 exceeds the Stock -Yogo (2005) critical value at the 5% level, suggesting that weak instruments are unlikely to be a concern. 10 In the second stage, reported in column (2), we regress Investment efficiency on the instrumented AI mentions and the full set of controls. The coefficient on instrumented AI mentions is positive and significant at the 5% level, indicating that the positive association between AI adoption and investment efficiency is unlikely to be fully attributable to endogeneity.

## 5. Potential mechanisms of AI adoption

## 5.1. Management sales forecast error

We focus on management sales forecast accuracy as a potential mechanism linking AI adoption to investment efficiency. AI may improve managerial forecasting by enabling the analysis of large-scale datasets and enhancing prediction beyond traditional human judgment. More accurate forecasts help firms better align investment with capacity and growth opportunities, reducing costly misallocations (Goodman et al., 2014; Tanaka et al., 2020).

In Panel A of Table 5, the dependent variable is Management sales forecast error , expressed as a percentage and measured as the absolute value of the difference between actual sales and the management sales forecast, divided by the management sales forecast. 12 The coefficients on AI mentions are consistently negative and significant across specifications that use different time windows (one-year sales, two-year average sales, and three-year average sales), indicating that higher AI adoption is associated with lower management sales forecast error and thus greater forecasting accuracy. Panel B of Table 5 presents the 2SLS estimates. The coefficient on instrumented AI mentions is negative but insignificant for one-year sales and becomes negative and significant when sales are averaged over two- and three-year horizons, suggesting that higher AI adoption is associated with improved management sales forecast accuracy, which may in turn contribute to more efficient investment decisions. 13

To shed further light on this mechanism, we examine whether AI adoption is associated with changes in analyst sales forecast accuracy. Because analysts rely mainly on public information and management disclosures, whereas managers can use internal AI tools to refine proprietary forecasts, AI adoption is likely to affect managerial forecasts more directly than analyst forecasts. Consequently, any improvements in analyst forecast accuracy are likely to be indirect and limited. Reestimating the OLS regressions in Table 5, using Analyst sales forecast error -measured as the absolute value of the difference between actual sales and analyst forecasts, divided by analyst forecasts -as the dependent variable, we find that the coefficient on AI mentions is statistically insignificant across all time windows (Online Appendix Table A.5). Taken together, this evidence is consistent with the view that AI adoption primarily improves internal decision-making rather than the broader information set available to external parties.

## 5.3. Patent activity

We also examine whether AI adoption is associated with firm-level patenting outcomes. AI may help managers better assess new projects and screen potential investments, lowering the cost of experimentation and enabling firms to commit capital to higher-quality opportunities (Cockburn et al., 2018). Process innovations, which involve new methods of production, can facilitate adjustments in firms' input mix and protect firm value, while product innovations expand firms' investment opportunity set (Bena et al., 2022; Babina et al., 2024a).

Table 7 presents the results. Columns (1) and (2) report OLS regressions in which the dependent variables are Process patents to sales and Product patents to sales , defined as the ratio of the number of process and product patents, respectively, to sales. 15 Because these ratios scale patent counts by firm size, they capture innovation intensity rather than the level of innovative output. Process (product) patents are identified as those for which over 70% of claims are classified in the respective category, following Ganglmair et al. (2022). The regressions control for R & D intensity and other variables that are likely to affect firm innovation outcomes and capital allocation. The coefficients on AI mentions are positive and significant in both columns, indicating that higher AI adoption is associated with greater process and product patenting intensity. Columns (3) and (4) present the 2SLS estimates, which yield similar inferences regarding the association between AI mentions and patenting intensity.

Taken together, these results suggest that increased process and product patenting intensity is one channel through which AI adoption may be associated with more effective capital allocation and, potentially, improved firm performance.

## 6. Additional tests

## 6.1. Early versus later AI adoption periods

We examine whether the association between AI adoption and investment efficiency differs across earlier and later periods. Previous work suggests that firms may not immediately benefit from AI adoption, as they need time to establish new processes and invest in complementary intangible assets such as digital skills and technology readiness (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). At the same time, broader AI adoption in later periods could attenuate any positive association with investment efficiency, as competition for AI talent and technology intensifies and stakeholders become more aware of AI ' s potential biases and limitations, which may foster skepticism about AI-based tools (D ' Acunto et al., 2019). To explore these possibilities, we split the sample into an early period (2010 2015) and a later period (2016 2021) and reestimate the regressions in column (3) of Table 3 for each subsample. Table 8 reports the results. In the OLS regressions (columns (1) and (2)), the coefficient on AI mentions is positive and significant only in the early period. In the 2SLS specifications (columns (3) and (4)), the coefficients on instrumented AI mentions are not significant in either period. Overall, these results provide only weak evidence that any positive association between AI adoption and investment efficiency is concentrated among early adopters.

## 6.2. GDPR and AI-related investment efficiency

To assess how regulation may influence the association between AI adoption and investment efficiency, we focus on the GDPR in the EU, which has been in effect since May 25, 2018. The GDPR sets strict standards for handling personal data and applies to both EUbased and non-EU firms that target or monitor individuals in the EU, potentially constraining the use of data-driven AI in decisionmaking (Aridor et al., 2023). 16

We implement a difference-in-differences design comparing treatment firms with significant EU exposure to control firms without such exposure. Treatment firms are those in the top 30% of average EU sales from 2015 to 2017 in the FactSet Revere Geographic Revenue database, and control firms are matched one-to-one using propensity scores from a logistic model that includes the variables in column (3) of Panel A of Table 3 and industry fixed effects, yielding 2755 firm-year observations for 296 treatment and 296 control firms over 2016 -2021 (excluding 2018). We hypothesize that, after the GDPR, treatment firms experience a weaker association between AI adoption and investment efficiency if regulatory frictions constrain AI use.

## 6.3. Controlling for industry-level regulatory and other changes

A potential concern is that our estimates might be confounded by time-varying, industry-level shocks, such as broad regulatory changes. Our main analyses focus on U.S. firms and include firm and year fixed effects, which control for time-invariant firm characteristics and common macroeconomic shocks. As reported in Online Appendix Table A.8, we re estimate all baseline OLS specifications using industry -year fixed effects in place of year fixed effects, thereby absorbing time-varying shocks at the industry level. The results are broadly similar across specifications, although the estimates for financial reporting quality are somewhat weaker. While this approach cannot rule out all remaining sources of bias, the overall evidence is not consistent with the idea that omitted regulatory shocks fully account for our findings.

## 6.5. AI mentions and investment efficiency by investment type

We decompose total investment into two components: capex, scaled by total assets, and non-capex, which includes R & D expenses and acquisition expenditures, also scaled by total assets. R & D investments are often high-risk and long-term, and their payoffs are S.-S. Chen et al.

[S. 19]

difficult to evaluate because the resulting knowledge assets are intangible and only imperfectly reflected in financial statements. This complexity likely increases the demand for AI ' s predictive and analytical capabilities when assessing project prospects and allocating capital to R & D. To examine how AI adoption relates to different investment types, we calculate the absolute value of the residuals from the investment model separately for capex and non-capex and reestimate the regressions in column (3) of Panel A of Table 3 using these two measures as dependent variables (Online Appendix Table A.10). The estimates indicate that AI mentions is more strongly associated with higher efficiency in non-capex (R & D and acquisition) investment than in capex, consistent with a more pronounced role for AI in settings where project outcomes are highly uncertain and information is harder to verify. These results are consistent with prior evidence that AI adoption is associated with greater product innovation and firm growth (Babina et al., 2024a) and suggest that, in our setting, AI adoption is also linked to higher efficiency in R & D-related investment.

## 6.6. AI mentions, underinvestment, and overinvestment

We decompose suboptimal investment into underinvestment and overinvestment to examine whether AI adoption is differentially associated with these two forms of investment inefficiency. Underinvestment is measured by negative residuals from the investment model, and Overinvestment is measured by positive residuals. We reestimate the regressions from column (3) of Panel A of Table 3, replacing the dependent variable with Underinvestment and Overinvestment (Online Appendix Table A.11). The results suggest that AI mentions is positively associated with investment efficiency for firms prone to underinvestment. The coefficient for overinvestment is negative but insignificant.

## 6.7. AI expertise as an alternative measure of AI adoption

We use the intensity of AI talent within the high-skilled workforce, measured as the ratio of AI specialists to total high-skilled workers, as an alternative proxy for AI adoption. This measure is constructed from USProfile and Compustat using AI-related job titles defined in Babina et al. (2024a), and high-skilled workers are identified based on specific vocational preparation (SVP) levels of four or higher in the USProfile database. This ratio reflects the extent to which AI expertise is integrated among skilled employees who interact with senior management and provide direct input into decision-making. We re estimate the main OLS regressions using this alternative  measure  and  find  that  the  results  are  generally  consistent  with  those  reported  in  the  main  analysis  (Online Appendix Table A.12). The estimates indicate that a higher share of AI talent within the workforce is associated with greater investment efficiency, improved management forecast accuracy, better financial reporting quality, and higher levels of innovation, particularly in process development. Moreover, the interaction between AI talent intensity and the GDPR treatment indicator remains negative and significant after the regulation, suggesting that GDPR is associated with a weaker positive association between AI adoption and investment efficiency.

## 7. Summary and conclusion

This study examines how AI adoption relates to investment efficiency in a setting where AI may enhance managerial forecasting, financial reporting quality, and innovation activity, yet also entails sizable implementation costs and risks. We proxy AI adoption using AI mentions in earnings calls, defined as the proportion of AI-related terminology in senior management remarks and responses during these calls, and find that this measure is positively associated with 10-K discussions of AI applications in operational decision-making, product development, and business expansion, suggesting that it captures economically meaningful variation in AI adoption.

We find that higher AI adoption is positively associated with greater investment efficiency, measured as firms ' deviations from expected investment conditional on their growth opportunities. This association generally remains in 2SLS specifications that instrument AI mentions with exposure to non-corporate academic AI research, constructed from textual similarity between universityauthored AI publications and detailed industry descriptions. While short-horizon stock returns do not appear to fully reflect the potential benefits of AI adoption, firms with higher AI mentions tend to exhibit higher Tobin ' s q over longer horizons, consistent with a gradual accumulation of complementary investments and organizational adjustments.

Additional analyses suggest several channels through which AI adoption may be associated with investment efficiency. Firms with higher AI mentions exhibit more accurate management sales forecasts, which may help align investment with capacity and growth opportunities and reduce costly misallocations. AI adoption is also associated with higher financial reporting quality and greater process and product innovation intensity, as captured by earnings management measures and patenting activity, respectively. At the same time, regulatory frictions, including data-privacy regulation, appear to attenuate the association between AI adoption and investment efficiency, suggesting that the benefits of AI may depend on the broader regulatory environment.

Overall, the evidence suggests that AI adoption is an increasingly relevant factor in how firms allocate capital, as reflected in investment efficiency. Our study underscores AI as a technology that may shape how firms implement their investment decisions, though the magnitude of its effects may depend on the regulatory and organizational environment in which firms operate
